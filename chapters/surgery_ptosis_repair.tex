\chapter{Ptosis Repair}

\section*{Introduction \& Clinical Indications}
Ptosis repair is a surgical intervention aimed at correcting blepharoptosis, a condition characterized by the drooping of the upper eyelid. This condition can manifest as unilateral or bilateral drooping and may arise from congenital factors, such as developmental muscle dystrophy, or acquired causes, most notably aponeurotic dehiscence due to aging or trauma. The surgical procedure is significant not only for its cosmetic benefits but also for its functional implications, as it improves the superior visual field and alleviates associated symptoms like brow ache and fatigue. The surgery involves repositioning the upper eyelid to restore its normal height and contour, which is crucial for both aesthetic appearance and optimal visual function. The choice of surgical technique is tailored to the underlying etiology of the ptosis and the functional status of the levator palpebrae superioris muscle, making preoperative assessment essential for successful outcomes.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Blepharoptosis Repair
  \item Eyelid Lift Surgery
  \item Levator Resection or Advancement
  \item Frontalis Sling Procedure
  \item M\"uller's Muscle Conjunctival Resection (MMCR)
  \item Aponeurotic Ptosis Repair
  \item Eyelid Droop Correction
\end{itemize}

The primary surgical principle of ptosis repair is to restore the upper eyelid margin to a physiologically appropriate position, typically 1-2 mm below the superior limbus. This restoration is essential for ensuring clear vision and aesthetic symmetry. The specific surgical technique chosen depends on the underlying cause of the ptosis, the severity of the droop, and the functional status of the levator palpebrae superioris muscle, which is assessed preoperatively through levator function measurement.

In cases of aponeurotic ptosis, the most common type in adults, the procedure focuses on repairing the stretched or disinserted levator aponeurosis. This often involves advancing, resecting, or plicating the aponeurosis and reattaching it to the anterior surface of the tarsal plate, effectively tightening the eyelid's primary elevator. For mild ptosis with good M\"uller's muscle function, a transconjunctival M\"uller's muscle conjunctival resection (MMCR) may be performed. In severe cases with poor levator function, particularly in congenital ptosis, a frontalis suspension procedure is often necessary, connecting the tarsal plate to the frontalis muscle to allow the eyebrow to lift the eyelid. The overarching technical goals are to achieve stable, symmetric eyelid height, a natural contour, and full visual clearance while minimizing the risk of lagophthalmos and exposure keratopathy.

The surgical correction of ptosis has undergone significant evolution since its inception in the late 19th century. Early attempts were rudimentary, often involving simple skin excisions or muscle plications. Dransart is credited with early descriptions of levator plication in the 1880s. By the early 20th century, more anatomically precise techniques emerged, with Blaskovics describing an internal levator resection technique in 1923 that laid the groundwork for modern approaches.

Surgeons like Everbusch and Friedenwald explored frontalis suspension methods in the 1890s and 1930s, respectively, utilizing the brow muscle to elevate the eyelid in cases of severe levator weakness. A major advancement occurred in 1961 when Fasanella and Servat introduced the M\"uller's muscle conjunctival resection, a technique still widely used for mild ptosis. Over the latter half of the 20th century, surgeons such as Putterman, Beard, and Jones refined external levator advancement and resection techniques, emphasizing precise anatomical repair and intraoperative adjustments. The development of various sling materials for frontalis suspension, from autogenous fascia lata to synthetic materials, marked further progress, allowing for durable solutions in complex cases. Modern techniques continue to focus on individualized approaches, minimizing complications, and optimizing both functional and aesthetic outcomes.

Ptosis repair is indicated for various clinical scenarios, particularly when the drooping eyelid causes functional impairment or significant cosmetic distress.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Significant obstruction of the superior visual field, leading to difficulty with reading, driving, or other daily activities, often quantifiable by visual field testing.
  \item Chronic brow ache or fatigue resulting from compensatory over-activation of the frontalis muscle to lift the eyelid.
  \item Amblyopia in children with congenital ptosis, necessitating early intervention to prevent visual axis obstruction.
  \item Cosmetic concerns impacting the patient's quality of life, even in the absence of severe visual obstruction.
  \item Asymmetry between the eyelids causing functional or aesthetic distress, often requiring bilateral surgery.
  \item Acquired ptosis secondary to aponeurotic dehiscence, neurogenic causes, or myogenic conditions after medical management fails.
  \item Pseudoptosis due to dermatochalasis or brow ptosis, where the eyelid position is normal but appears low.
  \item Traumatic ptosis where the levator complex has been damaged, requiring reconstruction.
\end{itemize}

Ptosis repair is primarily a definitive surgical treatment for symptomatic blepharoptosis. For most patients presenting with acquired aponeurotic ptosis or congenital ptosis, it is the initial and often curative intervention aimed at restoring visual function and aesthetic balance. The choice of specific technique is determined by the severity of ptosis and the underlying levator function, making it a primary procedure with varying approaches tailored to individual clinical presentations.

However, ptosis repair can also serve as a secondary or revision procedure in cases where an initial repair was unsuccessful, resulting in under-correction, over-correction, or unsatisfactory contour. In such scenarios, the goal of the secondary surgery is to refine the eyelid position and contour, often requiring more complex dissection due to scar tissue. While not typically an adjunct to other non-eyelid procedures, it may be performed concurrently with other oculoplastic surgeries such as blepharoplasty or brow lift to achieve comprehensive periorbital rejuvenation.

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
Understanding the anatomy of the upper eyelid and surrounding structures is crucial for performing ptosis repair effectively. The upper eyelid consists of several layers, including the skin, orbicularis oculi muscle, orbital septum, preaponeurotic fat pads, levator palpebrae superioris muscle, M\"uller's muscle, and the conjunctiva lining the posterior surface of the tarsal plate. The tarsal plate provides structural support to the eyelid.

The levator palpebrae superioris muscle, which is the primary elevator of the upper eyelid, originates from the lesser wing of the sphenoid bone and is innervated by the superior division of the oculomotor nerve (cranial nerve III). It transitions into a broad aponeurosis that inserts onto the anterior surface of the tarsal plate, contributing to the eyelid crease. M\"uller's muscle, a smooth muscle innervated by sympathetic fibers, provides an additional 1-2 mm of eyelid elevation. The orbicularis oculi muscle, innervated by the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII), is responsible for eyelid closure. 

The arterial supply to the eyelids is primarily from the medial and lateral palpebral arteries, branches of the ophthalmic artery, forming marginal and peripheral arcades. Venous drainage mirrors the arterial supply, draining into the ophthalmic and facial veins. Sensory innervation is provided by branches of the trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V). Lymphatic drainage from the lateral two-thirds of the upper eyelid goes to the preauricular lymph nodes, while the medial one-third drains to the submandibular nodes. Key surgical landmarks include the superior limbus, eyelid crease, tarsal plate, and orbital septum.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Thorough preoperative preparation is crucial for successful ptosis repair, focusing on patient safety and optimizing surgical outcomes. This begins with a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination, including detailed history, visual acuity, visual field testing, ocular motility assessment, tear film evaluation, measurement of levator function, margin reflex distance, and assessment for Bell's phenomenon and lagophthalmos. A detailed medical history and physical examination are conducted to identify any comorbidities, allergies, and current medications.

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Pre-anesthesia evaluation:** Routine laboratory tests, electrocardiogram, and chest X-ray may be ordered based on the patient's age and medical history.
  \item **NPO status:** Patients are typically instructed to be NPO for 6-8 hours prior to surgery.
  \item **Medication adjustments:** Blood-thinning medications are usually discontinued 7-10 days pre-operatively to minimize bleeding risk.
  \item \textbf{Prophylactic antibiotics:} Antibiotic use is individualized; routine intravenous prophylaxis is not required for every uncomplicated eyelid procedure.
  \item **Informed consent:** A detailed discussion of the procedure, its potential benefits, risks, alternatives, and the possibility of revision surgery is conducted, and the patient provides informed consent.
\end{itemize}

Careful patient selection is essential to minimize risks and optimize outcomes for ptosis repair.

Situations requiring treatment, optimization, or an alternative technique:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Uncontrolled severe dry eye syndrome, as surgery can exacerbate symptoms.
  \item Active ocular infection in the operative eye, which must be treated prior to surgery.
  \item Expectations inconsistent with the likely functional or cosmetic result should be addressed before surgery.
  \item Severe systemic illness may require medical optimization or a different anesthetic plan.
  \item Active thyroid eye disease, progressive neurologic disease, or unstable ocular-surface disease may warrant treatment or stabilization before elective repair.
\end{itemize}

Relative Contraindications and Precautions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Insufficient levator function for specific techniques, requiring consideration of alternative procedures.
  \item Pre-existing significant lagophthalmos or poor Bell's phenomenon.
  \item Bleeding diathesis or current use of anticoagulant medications.
  \item Unstable or active dermatological conditions affecting the eyelids.
  \item Certain progressive neurological conditions that may affect future eyelid function.
  \item Monocular patients, where the risk of complications must be weighed against potential benefits.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Ptosis repair is typically performed under local anesthesia with intravenous sedation, allowing for intraoperative patient cooperation to assess eyelid height and symmetry. General anesthesia may be used for children or complex revision cases. The patient is positioned supine with the head slightly elevated.

The procedure begins with precise preoperative markings of the desired eyelid crease and the target eyelid height relative to the superior limbus. Local anesthetic is infiltrated into the upper eyelid.

The most common approach for aponeurotic ptosis is the external levator advancement/resection:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item **Incision:** An incision is made along the natural upper eyelid crease, extending laterally.
  \item **Dissection:** The skin and underlying orbicularis oculi muscle are carefully dissected to expose the orbital septum.
  \item **Identification of Structures:** The orbital septum is opened, revealing the preaponeurotic fat pads, which are gently retracted superiorly to expose the levator aponeurosis.
  \item **Aponeurosis Repair:** The surgeon identifies the stretched or disinserted levator aponeurosis, which is then advanced, resected, or plicated to shorten its effective length.
  \item **Tarsal Fixation \& Adjustment:** The repositioned aponeurosis is reattached to the anterior surface of the tarsal plate with fine sutures. Eyelid height and contour are meticulously adjusted until optimal symmetry is achieved.
  \item **Crease Formation:** The skin and orbicularis muscle are sutured to the newly positioned aponeurosis to recreate a natural eyelid crease.
  \item **Closure:** The skin incision is closed with fine sutures, which may be absorbable or non-absorbable.
\end{enumerate}

For M\"uller's muscle conjunctival resection (MMCR), a transconjunctival approach is used, where M\"uller's muscle and a portion of the conjunctiva are resected from the undersurface of the eyelid. In cases of severe levator dysfunction, a frontalis suspension procedure is performed, where a sling material is anchored to the frontalis muscle in the brow, allowing the brow to lift the eyelid. Drains are typically not required, and implants are only used in frontalis sling procedures.

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
As with any surgical procedure, ptosis repair carries inherent risks, which can be categorized as general surgical risks and procedure-specific complications.

General Surgical Risks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item **Bleeding:** May manifest as ecchymosis, hematoma formation, or retrobulbar hemorrhage.
  \item **Infection:** Cellulitis of the eyelid or wound infection requiring treatment.
  \item **Anesthesia complications:** Nausea, vomiting, allergic reactions, or serious cardiac events.
  \item **Pain:** Postoperative discomfort, usually mild and manageable.
\end{itemize}

Procedure-Specific Risks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item **Asymmetry of eyelid height or contour:** The most common complication, which may necessitate revision surgery.
  \item **Under-correction:** Persistent ptosis requiring further surgical intervention.
  \item **Over-correction:** Eyelid retraction leading to exposure keratopathy.
  \item **Dry eye syndrome:** Exacerbation of pre-existing dry eye or new onset symptoms.
  \item **Suture granuloma or cyst formation:** Requiring removal.
  \item **Scarring:** Hypertrophic scarring or keloid formation.
  \item **Eyelid contour abnormalities:** Affecting aesthetic outcome.
  \item **Damage to the globe:** Rare but serious complications.
  \item **Diplopia:** May occur due to injury to the superior rectus muscle.
  \item **Loss of eyelid crease:** Or an unnatural-looking crease.
  \item **Recurrence of ptosis:** Over time, especially with progressive underlying conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Recovery}
Immediately following ptosis repair, the patient is transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for monitoring of vital signs and initial recovery from anesthesia. Ice packs are typically applied to the eyelids intermittently to minimize swelling and bruising. Mild discomfort is common and usually managed with oral analgesics. Most patients are discharged home the same day with detailed postoperative instructions.

During the first 24-48 hours, continued intermittent application of ice packs is highly recommended. Swelling and bruising are expected and gradually subside. Patients are advised to avoid strenuous activities and rubbing their eyes. Over the first 1-2 weeks, swelling and bruising continue to resolve, with non-absorbable skin sutures typically removed around 5-7 days post-operatively. Patients should refrain from wearing eye makeup or using contact lenses until cleared by the surgeon. Most patients can return to light activities and work within 1-2 weeks. Full resolution of swelling and the final aesthetic outcome may take several weeks to months.

During ptosis repair, the surgeon documents key findings that guide the procedure and inform postoperative assessment. These include the type of ptosis, measured levator function, degree of aponeurotic dehiscence, and final intraoperative eyelid height and contour achieved. For congenital ptosis, the levator muscle may appear thin or infiltrated with fat. Pathological findings are generally not relevant unless an underlying mass or lesion was biopsied during the procedure.

A normal postoperative course following ptosis repair is characterized by gradual improvement in eyelid appearance and function. Patients can expect initial swelling and bruising to resolve over 1-2 weeks, with mild discomfort manageable by oral analgesics. The eyelid margin should be positioned appropriately, with good symmetry between both eyes if bilateral surgery was performed. Patients typically report high satisfaction with both functional and cosmetic improvements.

Postoperative care involves a structured follow-up schedule to monitor healing and assess outcomes.

\begin{itemize}
  \item **First post-operative visit:** Scheduled 5-7 days after surgery for suture removal and initial assessment.
  \item **Subsequent visits:** Usually at 1 month, 3 months, and 6-12 months to monitor long-term results.
  \item **Medications:** Patients are typically prescribed topical antibiotic ointment and may use artificial tears for dry eye symptoms.
  \item **Activity resumption:** Gradual return to normal activities, avoiding strenuous exercise for 2-4 weeks.
  \item **Warning signs:** Patients are instructed to contact their surgeon for sudden vision changes, severe pain, excessive swelling, or purulent discharge.
\end{itemize}

Revision surgery may be considered for significant under-correction or over-correction but is typically deferred until adequate healing time has passed.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Ptosis affects individuals across all age groups, with a bimodal distribution. Congenital ptosis occurs in approximately 1 in 840 live births and can lead to amblyopia if not corrected early. Acquired ptosis, particularly aponeurotic ptosis, is more common in older adults, accounting for over 70\% of adult cases. Neurogenic and myogenic ptosis are less common but require specific diagnostic workups. While precise incidence figures for ptosis requiring surgical correction vary, it represents a significant portion of oculoplastic surgical volume globally. Mortality directly related to ptosis repair is rare, primarily associated with anesthesia risks in medically fragile patients.

Functional outcomes are often good when the diagnosis, levator function, ocular surface, and surgical technique are appropriately matched. There is no universal success or revision rate: eyelid asymmetry, under- or overcorrection, exposure symptoms, dry eye, contour abnormalities, and recurrent ptosis remain possible. Visual-field and symptom improvement should be assessed against preoperative findings.
