\chapter{Nissen Fundoplication}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Nissen fundoplication is a surgical procedure aimed at treating severe gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) by enhancing the function of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES). This operation involves wrapping the upper part of the stomach, known as the fundus, around the lower esophagus to create a new anti-reflux barrier. The procedure is indicated for patients who have not responded adequately to medical management, particularly those experiencing complications such as esophagitis, strictures, or Barrett's esophagus. By restoring the anatomical integrity of the gastroesophageal junction, Nissen fundoplication aims to prevent the backflow of gastric contents into the esophagus, thereby alleviating symptoms and improving the patient's quality of life. The surgery can be performed via an open or laparoscopic approach, with the latter being more common due to its benefits in terms of recovery time and postoperative pain.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Laparoscopic Nissen Fundoplication (LNF)
  \item Total Fundoplication
  \item Anti-reflux Surgery
  \item Fundoplication
  \item GERD Surgery
  \item Nissen Wrap
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
Understanding the anatomy surrounding the gastroesophageal junction is crucial for the successful execution of Nissen fundoplication. The **distal esophagus**, approximately 2-4 cm long, passes through the **esophageal hiatus** of the diaphragm and is anchored by the **phrenoesophageal ligament**. This ligament can be compromised in patients with hiatal hernias, which are often associated with GERD. The **diaphragmatic crura**, particularly the right crus, encircle the esophagus and contribute to the extrinsic component of the LES, playing a vital role in maintaining the anti-reflux mechanism.

The **gastric fundus**, located superiorly and laterally to the gastroesophageal junction, is mobilized during the procedure. The **short gastric vessels**, which connect the fundus to the spleen, must be divided to allow for adequate mobilization. The **vagus nerves** run alongside the esophagus and are critical to preserve during surgery, as injury can lead to complications such as delayed gastric emptying. The arterial supply to the distal esophagus primarily comes from the left gastric artery and inferior phrenic arteries, while venous drainage occurs through the esophageal veins into the azygos system and gastric veins into the portal system. Lymphatic drainage follows these vascular pathways to the periesophageal and paracardial lymph nodes.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The primary surgical principle of Nissen fundoplication is to restore the barrier function of the gastroesophageal junction, which is often compromised in patients with GERD due to a hypotensive LES or the presence of a hiatal hernia. The procedure aims to create a new high-pressure zone that functions as a one-way valve, allowing food to enter the stomach while preventing the reflux of gastric acid and bile into the esophagus. 

The rationale behind this surgical intervention is to mechanically augment the LES, thereby alleviating symptoms and promoting healing of the esophageal mucosa. Key technical goals include ensuring a tension-free wrap around the esophagus, repairing any associated hiatal hernia, and preserving the surrounding vascular and nerve structures to minimize postoperative complications.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The surgical treatment of GERD has evolved significantly since the early 20th century. The modern technique of fundoplication was pioneered by Rudolf Nissen, who, in 1955, inadvertently wrapped the gastric fundus around the esophagus during surgery for esophageal varices. This led to the resolution of the patient's heartburn symptoms, prompting Nissen to formally describe the 360-degree fundoplication technique in 1956. Initially performed via an open approach, this technique became the gold standard for treating medically refractory GERD.

A significant advancement occurred in 1991 when Bernard Dallemagne introduced the laparoscopic approach to Nissen fundoplication, which reduced postoperative pain and recovery time. This minimally invasive technique has since gained widespread acceptance, leading to variations such as the "floppy Nissen" and partial fundoplications tailored to specific patient needs. The introduction of robotic-assisted surgery has further refined the laparoscopic technique, enhancing precision and visualization during the procedure.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Nissen fundoplication is indicated for patients with chronic and severe GERD who meet specific criteria after thorough evaluation. Indications include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Persistent GERD symptoms despite optimal medical management with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for at least 8-12 weeks.
  \item Presence of GERD-related complications such as severe erosive esophagitis, esophageal stricture, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, or Barrett's esophagus with low-grade dysplasia.
  \item Atypical manifestations of GERD, including chronic cough, asthma, hoarseness, or non-cardiac chest pain, confirmed by objective testing.
  \item Significant hiatal hernia (Type II, III, or IV) contributing to reflux that is not adequately managed by medical therapy.
  \item Young patients preferring surgical intervention over lifelong medication or those intolerant to PPIs.
  \item Predominant symptoms of regurgitation that are less responsive to PPIs, particularly with large hiatal hernias.
  \item Documented pathological acid reflux on 24-hour pH monitoring correlating with symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Nissen fundoplication is primarily considered the first-line surgical treatment for GERD. It is typically pursued after conservative measures and medical therapy have proven insufficient or have led to complications. However, in certain cases, it may be appropriate as a primary intervention, particularly for young, otherwise healthy individuals with severe GERD or significant extra-esophageal symptoms who wish to avoid long-term medication. The procedure is also indicated for patients with complications such as recurrent strictures or severe esophagitis that are not controlled by medical therapy.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Nissen fundoplication is most commonly performed laparoscopically, although an open approach may be necessary in complex cases. The procedure generally follows these steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia and Positioning:} The patient is placed under general anesthesia and positioned supine with legs abducted or together, often in reverse Trendelenburg to enhance exposure.
  
  \item \textbf{Access and Insufflation:} The abdomen is accessed via an umbilical incision, and pneumoperitoneum is established. Multiple small incisions are made for trocar placement.

  \item \textbf{Liver Retraction:} The left lobe of the liver is retracted to expose the gastroesophageal junction and diaphragmatic hiatus.

  \item \textbf{Hiatal Dissection and Crural Repair:} The phrenoesophageal ligament is divided, and the distal esophagus is mobilized to achieve adequate intra-abdominal length. Any hiatal hernia sac is reduced, and the crura are approximated with non-absorbable sutures.

  \item \textbf{Fundic Mobilization:} The short gastric vessels are divided to allow for the mobilization of the fundus, ensuring a tension-free wrap.

  \item \textbf{Bougie Placement and Wrap Creation:} A large bougie is placed in the esophagus to guide the wrap. The fundus is wrapped around the esophagus and secured with sutures, creating a 360-degree wrap.

  \item \textbf{Wrap Assessment and Closure:} The wrap is inspected for proper tension and length. Hemostasis is confirmed, and the port sites are closed in layers.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A comprehensive preoperative evaluation is essential for optimizing surgical outcomes. This includes confirming the diagnosis of GERD and assessing the patient's fitness for surgery.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic Workup:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Upper Endoscopy (EGD):} To assess esophageal and gastric mucosa and rule out malignancy.
      \item \textbf{Esophageal Manometry:} To evaluate esophageal motility and rule out severe dysmotility disorders.
      \item \textbf{24-hour pH Monitoring:} To confirm the presence and severity of pathological acid reflux.
      \item \textbf{Barium Swallow Study:} To assess esophageal anatomy and identify hiatal hernia size and type.
    \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Medical Clearance:} Comprehensive evaluation, including cardiac and pulmonary assessments, along with routine blood tests.

  \item \textbf{Medication Adjustments:} Discontinuation of blood thinners and continuation of PPIs until the day of surgery.

  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients must be NPO for at least 6-8 hours before surgery.

  \item \textbf{Antibiotic Prophylaxis:} Intravenous antibiotics are administered prior to incision.

  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} Detailed discussion regarding the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
Careful patient selection is crucial for optimizing outcomes.

\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Severe Esophageal Dysmotility:} Conditions such as achalasia or severe diffuse esophageal spasm.
  \item \textbf{Uncorrectable Coagulopathy:} Significant bleeding disorders that pose a risk of hemorrhage.
  \item \textbf{Inability to Tolerate General Anesthesia:} Severe cardiopulmonary compromise.
  \item \textbf{Active Esophageal Malignancy:} Fundoplication is not indicated in the presence of cancer.
  \item \textbf{Undiagnosed GERD:} Surgery should not be performed without objective evidence of reflux.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications and Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Morbid Obesity:} Increases technical difficulty and risk of complications.
  \item \textbf{Previous Upper Abdominal Surgery:} May lead to adhesions and increased operative time.
  \item \textbf{Short Esophagus:} May necessitate additional procedures to prevent tension on the wrap.
  \item \textbf{Severe Cardiopulmonary Disease:} Requires thorough preoperative optimization.
  \item \textbf{Uncontrolled Psychiatric Illness:} May affect compliance and satisfaction with outcomes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Nissen fundoplication carries potential risks, categorized into general surgical risks and procedure-specific complications.

\textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage.
  \item \textbf{Infection:} Surgical site infection or intra-abdominal abscess.
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications:} Adverse reactions to anesthesia.
  \item \textbf{DVT/PE:} Blood clots that can lead to serious complications.
  \item \textbf{Injury to Adjacent Organs:} Accidental injury during dissection.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dysphagia:} Commonly transient but can be persistent.
  \item \textbf{Gas-Bloat Syndrome:} Inability to belch, leading to discomfort.
  \item \textbf{Wrap Herniation:} Migration or disruption of the fundoplication.
  \item \textbf{Esophageal or Gastric Injury:} Serious complications requiring repair.
  \item \textbf{Vagal Nerve Injury:} Can lead to delayed gastric emptying or diarrhea.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Postoperative recovery begins in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU), where patients are monitored for vital signs, pain, and nausea. Most patients can ambulate within hours of surgery. 

During the first 24-48 hours, a gradual advancement of diet is initiated, starting with clear liquids and progressing to a soft diet. Patients are typically discharged within 1-2 days, provided they are tolerating liquids and ambulating independently. For the first 1-2 weeks, a soft diet is recommended, with restrictions on strenuous activities and heavy lifting for 4-6 weeks. Full recovery may take several weeks to months, with most patients returning to normal activities within 6-8 weeks.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
Intraoperatively, surgeons document findings such as the presence of hiatal hernias, the condition of the phrenoesophageal ligament, and any esophageal inflammation. The integrity of the diaphragmatic crura and the successful creation of a tension-free wrap are critical observations. 

Pathological examination of any biopsies taken during preoperative endoscopy confirms the diagnosis of esophagitis or Barrett's esophagus, providing essential information for long-term management.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course is characterized by controlled pain, gradual improvement in dysphagia, and resolution of GERD symptoms. Patients typically tolerate a liquid diet soon after surgery, progressing to solid foods over several weeks. Most patients experience significant relief from GERD symptoms, allowing for a reduction or cessation of anti-reflux medications.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative follow-up is crucial for monitoring recovery and addressing complications. 

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Initial Visits:} Follow-up occurs 1-2 weeks post-surgery to assess wound healing and dietary progression.
  \item \textbf{Dietary Counseling:} Guidance on diet advancement and management of potential side effects.
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Gradual return to normal activities with restrictions on heavy lifting.
  \item \textbf{Medication Management:} Discontinuation of PPIs and management of residual symptoms.
  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients should contact their surgeon for severe pain, persistent vomiting, or signs of infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Epidemiology}
GERD is prevalent in approximately 10-20\% of the adult population in Western countries. While most cases are managed effectively with lifestyle changes and medications, a subset of patients develops severe or refractory symptoms, making them candidates for surgical intervention. Nissen fundoplication is the most commonly performed anti-reflux surgery, with a peak incidence in the early 2000s following the adoption of laparoscopic techniques. The procedure is performed across a wide age range, with a slight male predominance in those with severe complications. Mortality rates are low, generally less than 0.1-0.2\%, while morbidity rates range from 5-15\%.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Long-term outcomes of Nissen fundoplication are generally favorable, with 85-90\% of patients experiencing significant symptom relief at one year. The ability to discontinue or reduce PPI use is achieved in 80-90\% of patients. However, a small percentage may experience recurrence of symptoms, often due to wrap failure or migration. Prognostic factors for successful outcomes include a positive response to preoperative PPI therapy and normal esophageal motility. While the procedure effectively controls reflux, it does not reverse established Barrett's esophagus. For carefully selected patients, Nissen fundoplication offers a durable solution for chronic GERD.
