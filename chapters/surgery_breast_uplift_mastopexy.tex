\chapter{Breast Uplift (Mastopexy)}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Mastopexy, commonly referred to as breast lift surgery, is a surgical intervention aimed at elevating and reshaping sagging breasts, a condition known as breast ptosis. This procedure involves the excision of excess skin, tightening of the underlying breast tissue, and repositioning of the nipple-areola complex (NAC) to a higher and more aesthetically pleasing location on the breast mound. Breast ptosis can occur due to various factors, including aging, significant weight fluctuations, pregnancy, and breastfeeding, leading to a loss of skin elasticity and breast volume. Mastopexy not only enhances the aesthetic appearance of the breasts but also addresses physical discomfort associated with severe ptosis, such as skin irritation in the inframammary fold. By restoring a youthful breast contour, mastopexy can significantly improve a patient's self-esteem and quality of life.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Breast Lift Surgery
  \item Breast Reshaping Surgery
  \item Nipple Repositioning Surgery
  \item Mammary Gland Suspension
  \item Breast Ptosis Correction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The breast is a complex structure composed of glandular tissue, adipose tissue, and fibrous connective tissue, situated over the pectoralis major muscle on the anterior chest wall. It typically extends from the second to the sixth rib vertically and from the sternal edge to the mid-axillary line horizontally. The glandular tissue is organized into 15-20 lobes, which are connected by ducts that lead to the nipple. Cooper's ligaments provide structural support by anchoring the breast tissue to the overlying skin and deep fascia, playing a crucial role in maintaining breast shape.

The arterial supply to the breast is primarily derived from the internal mammary artery, lateral thoracic artery, and thoracoacromial artery, with additional contributions from intercostal perforators. Venous drainage parallels the arterial supply, draining into the axillary and internal mammary veins. Sensory innervation to the breast, particularly the NAC, is provided by the lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves (T4-T6). Preservation of these neurovascular bundles is essential during mastopexy to maintain NAC viability and sensation. Lymphatic drainage predominantly occurs through the axillary lymph nodes and internal mammary nodes, which is particularly relevant in the context of breast cancer surgery.

Key surgical landmarks include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Inframammary Fold (IMF): The natural crease where the breast meets the chest wall, crucial for assessing ptosis.
  \item Nipple-Areola Complex (NAC): The central structure of the breast, whose position is a key determinant of breast aesthetics.
  \item Cooper's Ligaments: Connective tissue structures that provide support and shape to the breast.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The primary surgical principle of mastopexy is to create a new, elevated breast mound by excising redundant skin, reshaping the glandular tissue, and repositioning the nipple-areola complex (NAC) while preserving its vascularity and innervation. The rationale behind this procedure is to counteract the effects of gravity, aging, and other factors that contribute to breast ptosis, thereby restoring a youthful contour and projection.

During the procedure, excess skin is removed from the lower pole of the breast, effectively lifting the entire breast envelope. The underlying breast parenchyma is often reshaped and plicated to create a firmer and more conical breast contour, providing internal support for the newly elevated breast. The NAC is transposed to its new position, typically at the apex of the newly formed breast mound, ensuring its viability through a carefully designed pedicle of breast tissue. Various surgical techniques, including periareolar, vertical, and inverted T patterns, are employed based on the degree of ptosis and desired aesthetic outcome. The overarching goals include achieving optimal symmetry, reducing areolar diameter if necessary, and minimizing scarring.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The history of mastopexy is closely linked to the evolution of breast surgery, particularly breast reduction techniques. One of the earliest documented procedures for breast ptosis correction was performed by Joseph Thorek in the 1920s, which involved significant skin removal and nipple grafting, often resulting in loss of sensation and viability. In 1928, Biesenberger introduced a technique that focused on glandular resection and reshaping, marking a pivotal shift towards addressing the underlying breast tissue.

Throughout the mid-20th century, surgeons such as Marc Dufourmentel and Ivo Pitanguy refined mastopexy techniques, introducing various skin patterns and glandular suspension methods aimed at preserving nipple sensation and improving aesthetic outcomes. Pitanguy's inverted T-scar technique, developed in the 1960s, became a standard for moderate to severe ptosis. The late 20th century saw further advancements with Madeleine Lejour's vertical mastopexy in the 1990s, which reduced horizontal scarring and improved breast projection. Today, mastopexy continues to evolve, emphasizing minimal scarring and long-term stability, often integrating techniques with breast augmentation or reduction for comprehensive aesthetic improvement.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Mastopexy is indicated for individuals experiencing breast ptosis, which can manifest in various degrees and forms. The following clinical indications warrant consideration for mastopexy:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Correction of true breast ptosis, where the NAC falls below the inframammary fold, often classified using the Regnault classification (Grade I, II, III).
  \item Improvement of pseudoptosis, where breast tissue sags but the NAC remains above the inframammary fold, typically due to glandular atrophy and skin laxity.
  \item Addressing breast asymmetry caused by differential sagging or volume loss between the breasts.
  \item Repositioning of a malpositioned or enlarged NAC to a more central and aesthetically appropriate location.
  \item Enhancing breast shape and firmness following significant weight loss, which often results in deflated and sagging breasts.
  \item Restoring a youthful breast contour after pregnancy and breastfeeding, which can lead to glandular involution and skin laxity.
  \item Alleviating physical discomforts such as skin irritation or hygiene issues in the inframammary fold due to skin-on-skin contact.
  \item As an adjunct to breast augmentation (mastopexy-augmentation) for patients desiring both a lift and increased breast volume.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Mastopexy is primarily considered a first-line elective cosmetic procedure for individuals seeking to correct breast ptosis and enhance breast aesthetics. It is the primary intervention when the main goal is to lift and reshape sagging breasts without significant changes in breast volume. Patients typically present with concerns about breast shape, nipple position, and overall breast youthfulness, making mastopexy the direct solution for these aesthetic goals.

Additionally, mastopexy can serve as a secondary or adjunct procedure in various contexts. It is frequently combined with breast augmentation for patients desiring both a lift and increased breast volume, addressing both ptosis and hypomastia simultaneously. Conversely, it is often performed in conjunction with breast reduction for individuals with both macromastia and significant ptosis, where excess breast tissue is removed, and the remaining breast is lifted and reshaped. Furthermore, mastopexy may be considered a revisionary procedure for patients who have experienced recurrence of ptosis after a previous breast lift or to correct unsatisfactory outcomes from prior breast surgeries.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Mastopexy is typically performed under general anesthesia, ensuring patient comfort and immobility throughout the procedure. The patient is positioned supine, with arms extended on arm boards for optimal access to the breast and axillary regions. Preoperative markings are crucial and are performed with the patient in an upright position to accurately assess the degree of ptosis and plan the new NAC position.

The surgical steps generally follow this structured sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia and Markings}: General anesthesia is administered. Precise preoperative markings are made on the breast to guide skin excision and NAC repositioning.
  
  \item \textbf{Infiltration}: A local anesthetic solution containing epinephrine is infiltrated into the planned incision lines to minimize intraoperative bleeding and provide postoperative pain relief.
  
  \item \textbf{Skin Excision}: Incisions are made according to the chosen mastopexy pattern (e.g., periareolar, vertical, or inverted T). The predetermined excess skin is carefully excised.
  
  \item \textbf{NAC Transposition}: The NAC is dissected on a pedicle of underlying breast tissue and transposed to its new, higher position, preserving its blood supply and sensation.
  
  \item \textbf{Glandular Reshaping and Plication}: The remaining breast tissue is reshaped and plicated to create a more conical and projected breast mound, providing internal support.
  
  \item \textbf{Hemostasis}: Meticulous hemostasis is achieved using electrocautery to minimize postoperative bleeding and the risk of hematoma formation.
  
  \item \textbf{Closure}: The skin flaps are redraped and closed in layers, with deep dermal sutures used to approximate the skin edges. Drains may be placed to collect any fluid accumulation.
  
  \item \textbf{Dressings}: Sterile dressings are applied to the incision sites, and a surgical bra or compression garment is fitted to provide support and reduce swelling.
\end{enumerate}

The duration of the procedure typically ranges from 2 to 4 hours, depending on the complexity of the ptosis and whether it is combined with other procedures.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Thorough preoperative preparation is essential for a safe and successful mastopexy. This process begins with a comprehensive consultation with the plastic surgeon, where the patient's medical history, surgical goals, and expectations are discussed.

Key preparatory steps include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Medical Evaluation}: A complete medical history and physical examination to assess overall health and identify chronic conditions.
  
  \item \textbf{Laboratory Tests}: Routine preoperative blood tests to ensure the patient is fit for surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Imaging}: A baseline mammogram or breast ultrasound may be required, especially for patients over 40 years of age.
  
  \item \textbf{Medication Adjustments}: Patients are advised to stop taking blood-thinning medications at least two weeks prior to surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Smoking Cessation}: Patients are strongly advised to cease smoking for at least 4-6 weeks before and after surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{NPO Status}: Patients must adhere to "nil per os" (NPO) guidelines before surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Prophylactic Antibiotics}: A single dose of intravenous prophylactic antibiotics is typically administered preoperatively.
  
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent}: A detailed informed consent form is reviewed and signed.
  
  \item \textbf{Preoperative Photography}: Standardized photographs are taken for medical record purposes.
\end{itemize}

Patients should arrange for transportation home after surgery and have assistance for the first 24-48 hours of recovery.

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
Careful patient selection is paramount for mastopexy to ensure safety and optimize outcomes.

\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Active breast infection or inflammation.
  \item Untreated or active breast malignancy.
  \item Uncontrolled systemic medical conditions that significantly increase surgical risks.
  \item Severe coagulopathy or bleeding disorders.
  \item Pregnancy or active breastfeeding.
  \item Body dysmorphic disorder or unrealistic expectations regarding surgical outcomes.
  \item Inability to provide informed consent due to cognitive impairment.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Smoking}: Increases the risk of wound healing complications.
  
  \item \textbf{Obesity}: Significant obesity can increase surgical risks.
  
  \item \textbf{Planned Future Pregnancy/Breastfeeding}: Subsequent pregnancies can cause recurrence of ptosis.
  
  \item \textbf{Certain Medications}: Chronic use of corticosteroids or anticoagulants may require careful management.
  
  \item \textbf{Unstable Weight}: Patients with significant weight fluctuations should achieve a stable weight before surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Prior Breast Surgery}: Previous surgeries may alter anatomy and increase complexity.
  
  \item \textbf{Large Breast Volume}: For very large breasts, a reduction-mastopexy may be more appropriate.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Mastopexy carries inherent risks, which can be categorized into general surgical risks and procedure-specific complications.

\textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications}: Reactions to general anesthesia, including nausea and respiratory depression.
  
  \item \textbf{Bleeding}: Postoperative hematoma requiring drainage.
  
  \item \textbf{Infection}: Surgical site infection requiring antibiotics or drainage.
  
  \item \textbf{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE)}: Rare but serious complications.
  
  \item \textbf{Poor Wound Healing}: Delayed healing or skin necrosis.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scarring}: Permanent scars that may become hypertrophic or keloid.
  
  \item \textbf{Asymmetry}: Some degree of asymmetry may persist postoperatively.
  
  \item \textbf{NAC Sensation Changes}: Temporary or permanent changes in sensation.
  
  \item \textbf{Nipple Necrosis}: Compromised blood supply leading to tissue death.
  
  \item \textbf{Contour Irregularities}: Dimpling or puckering of the breast contour.
  
  \item \textbf{Recurrence of Ptosis}: Some degree of sagging may recur over time.
  
  \item \textbf{Fat Necrosis}: Hard lumps in the breast tissue.
  
  \item \textbf{Seroma}: Collection of clear fluid under the skin.
  
  \item \textbf{Persistent Pain}: Chronic pain in the breast area.
  
  \item \textbf{Unsatisfactory Aesthetic Outcome}: Patient dissatisfaction despite technical success.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Following mastopexy, patients are transferred to a Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for close monitoring. Pain management is initiated, and patients typically have dressings applied and are fitted with a surgical support bra.

In the first 24-48 hours, moderate pain, swelling, and bruising are common, managed with prescribed oral pain relievers. Activity is restricted, emphasizing rest and avoiding strenuous movements. Patients are advised to sleep on their back with their upper body elevated to reduce swelling. Over the first 1-2 weeks, swelling and bruising gradually subside. Drains, if present, are typically removed within the first week. Patients are usually advised to avoid showering for a few days and then gently wash the incision sites.

Long-term recovery involves scar maturation, which can take 6-12 months. The final breast shape and position will become apparent as swelling resolves over several months. Patients can expect to gradually resume light activities within 1-2 weeks and return to normal activities by 4-6 weeks.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During mastopexy, the surgeon documents key findings, including the degree of breast ptosis, skin quality, and the original position of the NAC. Any unexpected findings, such as palpable masses, are noted for further investigation. The vascularity of the breast tissue and the planned pedicle for the NAC are assessed to ensure viability.

For pure mastopexy, resected tissue is typically sent for routine pathological examination only if there was a preoperative concern. If significant breast tissue is removed, the pathology report will detail the histological composition of the resected tissue, confirming the absence of malignancy and providing a description of any benign breast disease.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course following mastopexy is characterized by positive changes and a smooth recovery trajectory. Patients will experience a significant lift and reshaping of their breasts, with initial swelling and bruising present. Pain typically resolves with oral medication, and swelling diminishes over the first 2-4 weeks. Incision lines will initially appear red but are expected to flatten and fade over 6 to 12 months.

The NAC will be appropriately positioned, and its sensation may be altered, often improving over several months. Patients can gradually resume light activities within 1-2 weeks and return to most normal activities by 4-6 weeks. The final breast shape and projection will become evident as all residual swelling resolves, typically around 3-6 months post-surgery.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative care for mastopexy involves follow-up visits and specific instructions to ensure proper healing and monitor for complications. Adherence to these guidelines is crucial for optimal results.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Initial Postoperative Visits}: Scheduled within the first week to assess healing and remove drains.
  
  \item \textbf{Suture Removal/Care}: External sutures are usually removed at 1-2 weeks, with instructions on wound care.
  
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption}: Gradual return to normal activities, with guidance on resuming light exercise (usually 3-4 weeks) and strenuous activities (6-8 weeks).
  
  \item \textbf{Support Garment Use}: Continued use of a surgical bra or compression garment for several weeks.
  
  \item \textbf{Scar Management}: Recommendations for scar massage or topical treatments to optimize scar appearance.
  
  \item \textbf{Long-term Follow-up}: Visits at 3, 6, and 12 months to assess long-term results and address concerns.
  
  \item \textbf{Routine Breast Screening}: Patients should continue with age-appropriate breast cancer screening.
\end{itemize}

Patients are advised to contact their surgeon immediately if they experience warning signs such as fever, excessive pain, significant redness, or changes in breast shape or sensation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Breast ptosis is a common condition affecting a significant portion of the adult female population, with its prevalence increasing with age, parity, and body mass index. While precise incidence rates for mastopexy alone are challenging to isolate, it is consistently ranked among the most commonly performed aesthetic breast procedures globally. The typical patient undergoing mastopexy is a woman in her 30s to 50s, often multiparous, who has experienced breast sagging due to pregnancy, breastfeeding, or aging.

The demand for mastopexy has steadily risen as women seek to restore a youthful breast contour and improve body image. Mortality rates associated with mastopexy are exceedingly low, typically reported to be less than 0.01\%. Morbidity can include complications such as hematoma (1-5\%), infection (1-3\%), and wound healing issues, with rates varying based on patient factors and surgical technique. Long-term complications like recurrence of ptosis (5-15\%) or changes in nipple sensation (up to 20-30\% temporary, 5-10\% permanent) are also reported, influencing patient satisfaction and potential for revision surgery.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes of mastopexy are generally highly favorable, with patient satisfaction rates often exceeding 85-90\%. Studies show that the procedure significantly improves breast shape and projection, leading to enhanced body image and quality of life. Functional outcomes include alleviation of physical discomforts and improved fit of clothing. The long-term safety profile is excellent.

The durability of results is influenced by factors such as skin quality, breast size, and future life events. While mastopexy provides a long-lasting lift, some degree of recurrent ptosis is possible, with rates varying from 5% to 15% over 5-10 years. Prognostic factors for optimal outcomes include meticulous surgical technique, appropriate patient selection, adherence to postoperative care, and a healthy lifestyle. Most patients achieve their desired aesthetic goals with a single procedure, though a small percentage may opt for revision surgery to address minor asymmetries or improve scar appearance.
