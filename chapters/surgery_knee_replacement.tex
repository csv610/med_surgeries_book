\chapter{Knee Replacement}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Knee replacement, also known as total knee arthroplasty (TKA), is a significant surgical intervention aimed at restoring function and alleviating pain in patients with severe knee joint damage. This procedure is primarily indicated for individuals suffering from end-stage degenerative conditions, particularly osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and post-traumatic arthritis, which lead to debilitating pain, impaired mobility, and progressive deformity. During TKA, the damaged articular surfaces of the distal femur, proximal tibia, and often the patella are surgically resected and replaced with artificial components made from durable materials such as metal alloys and high-density polyethylene. The primary objective of this surgery is to restore the knee's mechanical function, improve the patient's quality of life, and enable them to engage in daily activities with reduced pain and increased mobility.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA)
  \item Knee Joint Replacement
  \item Knee Reconstruction Surgery
  \item Total Condylar Arthroplasty
  \item Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty (Partial Knee Replacement)
  \item Knee Resurfacing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The knee joint is a complex synovial hinge joint formed by the articulation of the distal femur and the proximal tibia, with the patella situated anteriorly. The articular surfaces are covered by hyaline cartilage, which facilitates smooth movement. The medial and lateral menisci, fibrocartilaginous structures, provide shock absorption and stability. Key ligaments, including the medial collateral ligament (MCL), lateral collateral ligament (LCL), anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL), contribute to joint stability and motion control.

The quadriceps muscle group, which extends the knee, connects to the patella via the quadriceps tendon, and the patellar tendon attaches to the tibial tuberosity. Neurovascular structures critical during TKA include the popliteal artery and vein, located in the popliteal fossa, and the common peroneal nerve, which runs laterally around the fibular head. Damage to these structures can lead to significant complications. Lymphatic drainage follows the major vascular pathways to the popliteal and inguinal lymph nodes. Important surgical landmarks include the medial and lateral epicondyles of the femur, the tibial tuberosity, and the patella, which guide incision placement and bone resection.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The surgical principle of total knee arthroplasty revolves around the excision of damaged articular surfaces and the restoration of knee function through the implantation of prosthetic components. The rationale for TKA is to alleviate the pain caused by the erosion of articular cartilage, which leads to bone-on-bone contact, inflammation, and joint dysfunction. By replacing these damaged surfaces with smooth, biocompatible implants, the procedure aims to restore the mechanical axis of the limb and facilitate pain-free movement.

The operative technique involves precise bone resection to create stable beds for the implants, followed by soft tissue balancing to ensure joint stability throughout the range of motion. The goals include restoring the anatomical alignment of the lower limb, achieving balanced flexion and extension gaps, ensuring proper ligamentous tension, and securing the implants to the host bone, typically using bone cement. The femoral component resurfaces the distal femur, the tibial component covers the proximal tibia, and a polyethylene insert serves as the new bearing surface. Patellar resurfacing, when indicated, replaces the posterior surface of the patella with a polyethylene button, contributing to the overall stability and function of the knee joint.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The evolution of knee arthroplasty began in the late 19th century with early attempts at interpositional arthroplasty using materials such as fascia and fat. These initial efforts were largely unsuccessful, leading to the development of more sophisticated techniques in the mid-20th century. A landmark moment occurred in 1968 with Frank Gunston's Geomedic knee, which introduced the concept of resurfacing the femoral condyles and tibial plateau separately.

The introduction of the Total Condylar Knee by John Insall and Peter Walker in 1974 marked a pivotal advancement in knee replacement surgery. This design featured a metal femoral component and a metal-backed tibial component with a polyethylene insert, establishing foundational principles that remain relevant today. Over the following decades, improvements in biomaterials, fixation methods, and implant designs, including posterior-stabilized and cruciate-retaining prostheses, have enhanced surgical outcomes. Recent innovations such as computer-assisted navigation and robotic-assisted surgery have further refined the precision of the procedure, leading to improved patient outcomes and longevity of the implants.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Total knee arthroplasty is indicated for patients with severe knee pain and functional impairment due to advanced knee joint disease that has not responded to conservative management.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Severe chronic knee pain that limits daily activities and quality of life, unresponsive to conservative treatments.
  \item Radiographic evidence of advanced degenerative joint disease, including severe osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or post-traumatic arthritis.
  \item Significant functional impairment, such as difficulty walking, climbing stairs, or performing daily activities.
  \item Progressive knee deformity, including varus or valgus malalignment, contributing to pain and instability.
  \item Persistent knee stiffness or severe loss of range of motion interfering with normal function.
  \item Failure of previous knee surgeries to provide lasting relief or improvement.
  \item Inflammatory arthropathies leading to severe joint changes.
  \item Avascular necrosis of the femoral condyles causing severe pain and functional limitation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Total knee arthroplasty is considered a definitive surgical treatment for end-stage knee joint disease. It is not a first-line intervention but rather a secondary or tertiary option pursued after conservative treatments have failed. These conservative measures include activity modification, weight management, physical therapy, and pharmacologic interventions.

TKA becomes the primary treatment when joint degeneration and pain reach a level that significantly impairs function and quality of life. In rare cases, such as severe trauma or rapidly progressing arthritis, TKA may be considered earlier in the treatment pathway. For the majority of patients with chronic degenerative conditions, TKA represents the final step in a comprehensive treatment approach aimed at restoring function and improving quality of life.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Total knee arthroplasty is performed in a sterile operating room, typically lasting 1 to 2 hours.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia:} The procedure is usually performed under general anesthesia, spinal anesthesia, or a combination of both to ensure effective pain control.
  
  \item \textbf{Patient Positioning \& Preparation:} The patient is positioned supine, and the affected leg is prepared and draped in a sterile manner. A pneumatic tourniquet is often applied to enhance visibility during the procedure.
  
  \item \textbf{Incision:} A longitudinal incision, typically 15-25 cm, is made over the anterior knee, commonly using a medial parapatellar approach to allow for optimal exposure.
  
  \item \textbf{Bone Resection:} Specialized cutting guides are utilized to resect the damaged articular surfaces of the distal femur and proximal tibia, ensuring proper alignment and joint line restoration.
  
  \item \textbf{Soft Tissue Balancing \& Trial Implants:} Trial components are inserted to assess joint stability and range of motion. Adjustments are made as necessary to achieve optimal balance.
  
  \item \textbf{Definitive Implantation:} Once satisfied with the trial components, the definitive femoral and tibial components are cemented in place, along with a polyethylene insert. Patellar resurfacing may also be performed.
  
  \item \textbf{Closure:} The joint is irrigated, hemostasis is achieved, and the surgical layers are closed meticulously. A drain may be placed, and a sterile dressing is applied.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A thorough preoperative workup is essential for optimizing outcomes and minimizing risks associated with total knee arthroplasty.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Comprehensive Medical Evaluation:} A detailed assessment by the primary care physician and anesthesiologist is necessary to evaluate the patient's overall health and identify any comorbidities.
  
  \item \textbf{Medication Review \& Adjustment:} All medications, particularly anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents, should be reviewed and adjusted as necessary to minimize bleeding risks.
  
  \item \textbf{Dental Clearance:} A recent dental examination is often required to rule out active infections that could lead to postoperative complications.
  
  \item \textbf{Physical Therapy/Pre-habilitation:} Preoperative physical therapy may be beneficial in strengthening muscles and improving range of motion.
  
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients are instructed to be NPO for a specified period before surgery to prevent aspiration.
  
  \item \textbf{Prophylactic Antibiotics:} Intravenous antibiotics are administered prior to incision to reduce the risk of infection.
  
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} Patients must be fully informed about the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives before providing consent.
  
  \item \textbf{Home Preparation:} Patients should prepare their home environment for recovery, including arranging for assistance with daily tasks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
Careful patient selection is critical for successful total knee arthroplasty.

\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Active infection in the knee joint or elsewhere in the body.
  \item Severe peripheral vascular disease compromising limb circulation.
  \item Dysfunction of the extensor mechanism preventing effective rehabilitation.
  \item Neuropathic arthropathy with severe joint instability.
  \item Skeletally immature patients with open growth plates.
  \item Uncontrolled medical comorbidities posing high surgical risks.
  \item Active osteomyelitis in the affected limb.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications \& Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Morbid obesity, which increases surgical complexity and complication risks.
  \item Severe osteoporosis, which may compromise implant fixation.
  \item Poor skin condition around the knee, increasing infection risk.
  \item Significant psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment affecting cooperation.
  \item Unrealistic patient expectations regarding outcomes.
  \item Severe flexion contracture or ankylosis of the knee.
  \item History of prior knee infection, increasing the risk of recurrence.
  \item Chronic pain syndromes complicating postoperative management.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Total knee replacement is generally safe, but it carries potential risks and complications.

\textbf{General Surgical Complications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia-related risks:} Adverse reactions, respiratory depression, and cardiac events.
  \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Excessive blood loss may require transfusion and can lead to hematoma formation.
  \item \textbf{Infection:} Surgical site infections can range from superficial to deep periprosthetic joint infections.
  \item \textbf{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE):} Blood clots can form and may be life-threatening.
  \item \textbf{Wound healing problems:} Delayed healing or dehiscence can occur, especially in high-risk patients.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedure-Specific Complications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nerve Injury:} Damage to surrounding nerves can lead to transient or permanent deficits.
  \item \textbf{Vascular Injury:} Rare but serious damage to major blood vessels may require urgent repair.
  \item \textbf{Arthrofibrosis:} Excessive scar tissue formation can limit range of motion.
  \item \textbf{Implant Loosening or Failure:} Over time, prosthetic components may loosen or wear out.
  \item \textbf{Periprosthetic Fracture:} Fractures around the implant may require additional surgery.
  \item \textbf{Instability:} Improper implant positioning can lead to instability or dislocation.
  \item \textbf{Patellar Complications:} Issues may arise if the patella is resurfaced.
  \item \textbf{Persistent Pain:} Some patients may experience ongoing pain despite successful surgery.
  \item \textbf{Leg Length Discrepancy:} Minor discrepancies may occur, requiring further intervention if symptomatic.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
The postoperative recovery timeline for total knee arthroplasty is structured to maximize functional recovery and minimize complications.

Immediately after surgery, patients are monitored in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for vital signs and pain management. Early mobilization is encouraged, often beginning on the same day or the next day with physical therapy assistance. Patients typically start with gentle range-of-motion exercises and partial weight-bearing.

The hospital stay usually lasts 1 to 3 days, during which physical therapy sessions focus on regaining knee flexion and extension, strengthening muscles, and practicing safe ambulation. Upon discharge, patients may transition to home health or outpatient physical therapy. The first 1-2 weeks involve managing pain and swelling while gradually increasing activity. Most patients can walk with minimal assistance by 4-6 weeks. Full recovery, including return to low-impact activities, may take 3 to 6 months, with continued improvement in strength and endurance for up to a year.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During total knee arthroplasty, the surgeon documents intraoperative findings, including the extent of cartilage loss, osteophyte presence, and ligamentous laxity. These findings confirm the diagnosis of end-stage arthritis and guide the surgical approach.

Resected bone and cartilage specimens are sent for pathological examination, typically confirming degenerative changes consistent with osteoarthritis. The pathology report serves to provide histological confirmation of the disease process and rule out unexpected findings, although such occurrences are rare.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course following total knee arthroplasty is characterized by progressive improvement in pain and function. Patients can expect significant pain reduction, allowing for improved sleep and decreased reliance on pain medications within weeks. Functionally, patients should experience increased mobility, with a target range of motion of at least 0-110 degrees of flexion.

The surgical incision should heal cleanly, typically within 2-3 weeks, with swelling and bruising gradually subsiding. Ultimately, a normal postoperative course leads to improved quality of life, with most patients returning to desired low-impact activities within 3-6 months.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Comprehensive postoperative care is essential for optimal recovery after total knee arthroplasty.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Postoperative Clinic Visits:} Follow-up appointments are scheduled at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year post-surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Suture/Staple Removal:} Skin sutures or staples are typically removed at the first postoperative visit.
  
  \item \textbf{Physical Therapy:} Intensive physical therapy continues for 6-12 weeks, focusing on strengthening and range of motion.
  
  \item \textbf{Imaging:} Follow-up X-rays are taken periodically to monitor implant position and detect any complications.
  
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Patients are guided on a gradual return to activities, avoiding high-impact sports.
  
  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients are educated on critical warning signs to report, such as increasing pain or signs of infection.
  
  \item \textbf{Dental Prophylaxis:} Patients may require prophylactic antibiotics before dental procedures for a period after surgery.
\end{itemize}

Long-term care focuses on maintaining joint health and monitoring for any new symptoms.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Total knee arthroplasty is one of the most common orthopedic procedures performed globally, with an increasing incidence due to an aging population and rising obesity rates. In the United States, over 700,000 TKAs are performed annually, with projections indicating continued demand. The procedure is most prevalent in individuals over 60, although younger patients are increasingly undergoing TKA. Women are more likely to have the procedure than men, primarily due to a higher prevalence of osteoarthritis. The primary indication for TKA remains end-stage osteoarthritis, accounting for over 90\% of cases. Mortality rates associated with elective TKA are low, typically less than 0.5\%, while major morbidity rates range from 1\% to 5\%.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes following total knee arthroplasty are overwhelmingly positive, with high rates of patient satisfaction and significant improvements in quality of life. Success rates for pain relief and functional improvement exceed 90-95\%. Most patients experience substantial pain reduction and increased ability to perform daily activities.

Implant survival rates are favorable, with 10-year survival rates typically exceeding 90-95\% and 20-year survival rates around 80-85\%. Prognostic factors influencing long-term outcomes include patient age, activity level, body mass index, and adherence to rehabilitation protocols. While complications such as infection or loosening can necessitate revision surgery, modern techniques and materials have made TKA a durable and effective solution for end-stage knee arthritis.
