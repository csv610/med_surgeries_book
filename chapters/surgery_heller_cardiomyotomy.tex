\chapter{Heller Cardiomyotomy}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Heller cardiomyotomy, commonly known as a Heller myotomy, is a definitive surgical intervention primarily performed to treat achalasia, a chronic esophageal motility disorder. This procedure involves the precise longitudinal division of the muscular layers of the distal esophagus and the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), extending onto the gastric cardia, while carefully preserving the underlying esophageal mucosa. The fundamental goal is to relieve the functional obstruction caused by the LES's failure to relax and the absence of effective esophageal peristalsis, which are hallmarks of achalasia. By surgically weakening the hypertonic sphincter, the myotomy facilitates the passage of food and liquids from the esophagus into the stomach, thereby alleviating severe dysphagia, regurgitation, and associated symptoms, and significantly improving the patient's quality of life. Modern Heller myotomy is predominantly performed using minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques, often complemented by a partial fundoplication to mitigate postoperative gastroesophageal reflux disease.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Laparoscopic Heller Myotomy
  \item Esophagomyotomy
  \item Laparoscopic Esophagomyotomy with Fundoplication
  \item Modified Heller Procedure
  \item Extramucosal Myotomy
  \item Cardiomyotomy for Achalasia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The surgical anatomy pertinent to a Heller cardiomyotomy centers on the distal esophagus, the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ), the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), and the surrounding diaphragmatic hiatus. The esophagus is a muscular tube, approximately 25 cm long, with an outer longitudinal and an inner circular muscle layer. At its distal end, it passes through the esophageal hiatus of the diaphragm, a muscular opening formed by the right crus, before joining the stomach at the GEJ. The LES is a physiologically defined high-pressure zone, not a distinct anatomical sphincter, formed by the thickening of the circular muscle layer of the distal esophagus and augmented by the diaphragmatic crura.

Key anatomical structures and relations include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Esophagus:} The distal 6-8 cm, comprising longitudinal and circular muscle layers. The myotomy targets the circular muscle.
  \item \textbf{Gastroesophageal Junction (GEJ):} The transition point between the esophagus and the stomach, typically marked by the change from esophageal squamous epithelium to gastric columnar epithelium.
  \textbf{Diaphragmatic Hiatus:} The opening in the diaphragm through which the esophagus passes. The crura of the diaphragm contribute to LES function.
  \item \textbf{Phrenoesophageal Ligament:} A fascial condensation that anchors the esophagus to the diaphragm, forming the hiatal attachments. Division of this ligament is necessary for adequate exposure.
  \item \textbf{Vagus Nerves:} The anterior (left) and posterior (right) vagal trunks descend with the esophagus. The anterior vagus nerve typically lies on the anterior surface of the esophagus and must be carefully identified and preserved during the myotomy to avoid gastroparesis.
  \item \textbf{Arterial Supply:} The distal esophagus receives blood from esophageal branches of the left gastric artery and inferior phrenic arteries. The gastric cardia is supplied by the left gastric artery and short gastric arteries. Meticulous hemostasis is crucial.
  \item \textbf{Venous Drainage:} Venous drainage of the distal esophagus is primarily via the esophageal veins, which drain into the azygos and hemiazygos systems, and also communicate with the left gastric vein (a portal system tributary), forming a portosystemic anastomosis.
  \item \textbf{Lymphatic Drainage:} Lymphatics of the distal esophagus drain into nodes along the left gastric artery and celiac axis.
\end{itemize}
Understanding these relationships is paramount to performing a safe and effective myotomy, particularly in preserving the vagus nerves and maintaining mucosal integrity.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
Achalasia is fundamentally a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the loss of inhibitory ganglion cells within the myenteric plexus of the esophageal wall, predominantly at the lower esophageal sphincter (LES). This neuronal deficit leads to two primary pathophysiological features: a failure of the LES to relax in response to swallowing, and the absence of coordinated peristaltic contractions in the esophageal body. The resulting functional obstruction at the gastroesophageal junction causes progressive dilation of the esophagus and retention of ingested material, leading to severe dysphagia and regurgitation.

The principle of the Heller cardiomyotomy is to surgically disrupt the hypertonic circular muscle fibers of the LES, which are responsible for its sustained contraction and inability to relax. By performing an extramucosal myotomy, the surgeon longitudinally divides these muscle layers, extending from the distal esophagus across the gastroesophageal junction and onto the gastric cardia for a short distance (typically 2-3 cm). This division effectively widens the esophageal outlet, allowing gravity and the hydrostatic pressure of retained food and liquid to facilitate passage into the stomach, thereby bypassing the non-relaxing sphincter. A critical component of the modern Heller myotomy is the concomitant performance of a partial fundoplication (most commonly a Dor anterior fundoplication or a Toupet posterior fundoplication). This anti-reflux maneuver is essential because the myotomy, by compromising the LES's natural barrier function, significantly increases the risk of postoperative gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). The fundoplication wraps a portion of the gastric fundus around the distal esophagus, providing a mechanical barrier against reflux while still allowing the now-relaxed LES to open, thus achieving the technical goals of relieving obstruction and preventing reflux.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The surgical treatment of achalasia has a rich history spanning over a century, marked by significant advancements in technique and approach. The first successful surgical myotomy for achalasia was performed by Ernst Heller in 1913 in Germany. His original procedure, known as the "double Heller," involved dividing both the anterior and posterior muscle layers of the distal esophagus. While effective, this approach was associated with higher morbidity due to the extensive dissection. Over subsequent decades, the technique was refined, leading to the adoption of a single anterior myotomy as the standard, which proved equally effective with fewer complications.

Early Heller myotomies were performed via an open abdominal incision or a thoracotomy, both of which were major operations with considerable postoperative pain, prolonged recovery times, and significant scarring. A pivotal advancement occurred in the early 1990s with the advent of minimally invasive surgery. The first laparoscopic Heller myotomy was reported in 1991, quickly demonstrating superior outcomes in terms of reduced pain, shorter hospital stays, quicker return to normal activities, and improved cosmetic results compared to open approaches. This minimally invasive technique rapidly became the gold standard. Concurrently, the importance of adding an anti-reflux procedure, such as a Dor or Toupet fundoplication, was recognized to mitigate the high incidence of postoperative gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) that resulted from the compromised LES. This evolution solidified the modern laparoscopic Heller cardiomyotomy with partial fundoplication as the preferred surgical treatment for achalasia, offering both effective symptom relief and reflux prevention.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Heller cardiomyotomy is indicated for patients with confirmed achalasia who experience significant symptoms and for whom less invasive treatments have failed or are not appropriate. The primary indications include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Persistent and severe dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) for both solids and liquids, which significantly impairs nutritional intake and quality of life, despite dietary modifications.
  \item Frequent regurgitation of undigested food and saliva, leading to aspiration risk, recurrent respiratory infections, or chronic cough.
  \item Significant weight loss directly attributable to the inability to adequately consume food due to the esophageal obstruction caused by achalasia.
  \item Recurrent or severe non-cardiac chest pain that is characteristic of esophageal spasm or distension in achalasia, unresponsive to medical therapy.
  \item Failure of less invasive endoscopic treatments, such as pneumatic balloon dilation or botulinum toxin injection, to provide durable symptom relief.
  \item As a primary treatment option for younger patients with achalasia who desire a long-term, definitive solution with high success rates.
  \item When peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) is not available, contraindicated (e.g., prior esophageal surgery, severe tortuosity), or has failed to achieve adequate symptom control.
  \item Objective diagnostic findings consistent with achalasia, such as a "bird's beak" appearance on barium swallow, significant esophageal dilation, or characteristic manometric findings (e.g., absent peristalsis, incomplete LES relaxation, elevated LES pressure) according to the Chicago Classification of Esophageal Motility Disorders.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Heller cardiomyotomy is considered a primary and highly effective treatment for achalasia, particularly for patients who are good surgical candidates and desire a durable, long-term solution. It is often recommended as a first-line intervention for younger patients, those with vigorous achalasia (Type III), or individuals with significant esophageal dilation, due to its excellent long-term success rates and proven efficacy. Its role as a primary treatment is well-established, offering superior and more durable symptom relief compared to endoscopic pneumatic dilation in many patient cohorts.

However, the Heller myotomy also serves as a crucial secondary or salvage procedure. It is frequently offered to patients who have experienced failure or recurrence of symptoms after less invasive endoscopic treatments, such as pneumatic balloon dilation or botulinum toxin injection, which often provide only temporary relief. While peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) has emerged as another primary endoscopic treatment option, Heller myotomy remains a robust alternative, especially in cases where POEM is technically challenging, contraindicated (e.g., severe esophageal tortuosity, prior radiation), or when a surgical approach is preferred by the patient or surgeon due to concerns about reflux or the need for concomitant hiatal hernia repair. Its role as a secondary procedure also extends to patients who may have had an incomplete myotomy or developed recurrent symptoms after a prior surgical intervention, though re-operative myotomy can be technically more complex due to adhesions.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
The laparoscopic Heller cardiomyotomy is performed under general anesthesia, with the patient typically positioned supine in a reverse Trendelenburg position to allow gravity to retract abdominal contents inferiorly, improving exposure of the hiatus. The procedure begins with the creation of pneumoperitoneum, usually to 12-15 mmHg, using a Veress needle or an open Hasson technique, followed by the insertion of several small (5-12 mm) trocars into the upper abdomen. These ports facilitate the introduction of a laparoscope (camera) and specialized surgical instruments.

The key steps of the procedure are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Access and Exposure:} The surgeon gains access to the esophageal hiatus by dividing the gastrohepatic ligament and carefully dissecting the phrenoesophageal membrane. The distal esophagus and the gastroesophageal junction are meticulously mobilized and dissected circumferentially to expose the anterior surface of the esophagus and the gastric cardia, ensuring clear identification of the anterior vagus nerve.
  \item \textbf{Identification of the LES:} The lower esophageal sphincter is identified by its characteristic thickening of the circular muscle layer and often by the transition from the relatively thin esophageal wall to the thicker gastric wall. An intraoperative endoscope can be advanced to help delineate the GEJ.
  \item \textbf{Myotomy Initiation:} The myotomy is initiated on the anterior surface of the esophagus, approximately 2-3 cm proximal to the gastroesophageal junction. The longitudinal muscle fibers are carefully incised using electrocautery or ultrasonic shears, exposing the underlying circular muscle layer.
  \item \textbf{Extramucosal Dissection:} The circular muscle fibers are then meticulously divided longitudinally, one fiber at a time, until the submucosal layer and the intact esophageal mucosa are clearly visible. This division continues distally across the gastroesophageal junction and onto the gastric cardia for about 2-3 cm. Proximally, the myotomy extends 6-8 cm up the esophagus to ensure complete division of the hypertonic LES and any spastic esophageal muscle.
  \item \textbf{Mucosal Integrity Check:} After the myotomy is complete, the integrity of the esophageal mucosa is rigorously confirmed. This is typically done by insufflating air into the esophagus via an intraoperative endoscope while the surgical field is submerged in saline (bubble test). Any mucosal injury, even a small one, must be immediately identified and meticulously repaired with fine absorbable sutures.
  \item \textbf{Fundoplication:} A partial fundoplication is then performed to prevent postoperative gastroesophageal reflux. The most common types are the Dor fundoplication (anterior, 180-200 degrees) or the Toupet fundoplication (posterior, 270 degrees). This involves wrapping a portion of the gastric fundus around the distal esophagus and securing it with sutures to the diaphragm and the edges of the myotomy.
  \item \textbf{Closure:} The surgical field is inspected for complete hemostasis. The trocars are removed under direct vision, and the small abdominal incisions are closed in layers with absorbable sutures or surgical glue.
\end{itemize}
A nasogastric tube may be placed intraoperatively and typically removed in the PACU or within the first few hours post-op. Drains are generally not left in place unless there is a specific concern for mucosal injury or significant bleeding.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Thorough preoperative preparation is essential for a successful Heller cardiomyotomy, aiming to optimize patient condition, confirm diagnosis, and minimize risks. Patients are typically instructed to follow a clear liquid diet for 2-3 days prior to surgery to help empty the often-dilated and food-filled esophagus, reducing the risk of aspiration during induction of anesthesia and improving surgical visibility. Fasting from all food and liquids for at least 8 hours before surgery is mandatory.

Key preparatory steps include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic Confirmation:} The diagnosis of achalasia must be unequivocally confirmed through a combination of barium swallow (showing esophageal dilation, aperistalsis, and a "bird's beak" appearance of the LES) and high-resolution esophageal manometry (demonstrating absent peristalsis and incomplete LES relaxation, often with elevated LES pressure).
  \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} Upper endoscopy is crucial to rule out pseudoachalasia (e.g., due to malignancy at the GEJ) and to assess for esophagitis or other mucosal abnormalities. Biopsies may be taken if there is any suspicion of malignancy.
  \item \textbf{Medical Optimization:} A comprehensive medical evaluation, including a detailed history and physical examination, is performed. Baseline laboratory tests such as a complete blood count, electrolyte panel, renal and liver function tests, and coagulation studies are obtained. Any underlying medical conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, pulmonary disease) are optimized.
  \item \textbf{Cardiac and Anesthesia Clearance:} Patients undergo cardiac clearance, often including an electrocardiogram (ECG) and sometimes further cardiac testing (e.g., echocardiogram, stress test), to ensure they are fit for general anesthesia and the physiological demands of laparoscopic surgery. Anesthesia consultation assesses individual risks and plans for perioperative management.
  \item \textbf{Medication Review:} All medications are reviewed. Blood-thinning medications (anticoagulants and antiplatelets) are typically stopped several days to a week before surgery, as directed by the surgeon and cardiologist, to minimize bleeding risk.
  \item \textbf{Antibiotic Prophylaxis:} Intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., a cephalosporin) are administered shortly before the incision to reduce the risk of surgical site infection.
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} Detailed informed consent is obtained, ensuring the patient fully understands the procedure, its anticipated benefits, potential risks, and available alternatives.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Severe, uncorrectable cardiopulmonary disease that renders the patient an unacceptable risk for general anesthesia and major abdominal surgery (e.g., severe uncontrolled heart failure, recent myocardial infarction, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with respiratory failure).
  \item Active, uncontrolled esophageal perforation or severe esophageal infection (e.g., necrotizing esophagitis), which would preclude safe surgical manipulation and increase the risk of sepsis.
  \item Uncorrected coagulopathy or bleeding diathesis that cannot be managed preoperatively, significantly increasing the risk of intraoperative and postoperative hemorrhage.
  \item Carcinoma of the gastroesophageal junction or distal esophagus, as this requires a different and more extensive surgical approach, typically an esophagectomy, rather than a myotomy.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications \& Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Prior extensive upper abdominal surgery, especially near the hiatus, which may result in dense adhesions and significantly increase the technical difficulty, operative time, and risk of iatrogenic injury during re-operation.
  \item Severe esophageal dilation with tortuosity (sigmoid esophagus), which can make the myotomy technically challenging and may indicate a need for esophagectomy in some cases, particularly if the esophagus is severely decompensated.
  \item Advanced age or extreme frailty, where the risks of surgery and anesthesia may outweigh the potential benefits, prompting consideration of less invasive alternatives such as pneumatic dilation or POEM.
  \item Morbid obesity (BMI > 40 kg/m\textsuperscript{2}), which can increase the technical difficulty of laparoscopic surgery, prolong operative time, and elevate anesthesia risks and postoperative complications.
  \item Pregnancy, where surgery is generally deferred until after delivery unless emergent, due to potential risks to the fetus from anesthesia and surgical stress.
  \item Active peptic ulcer disease or severe esophagitis that requires medical optimization and healing before surgery to reduce the risk of complications.
  \item Significant hiatal hernia, which may require concomitant repair and careful consideration during fundoplication.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
As with any surgical procedure, Heller cardiomyotomy carries potential risks, which can be broadly categorized into general surgical complications and procedure-specific complications.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Complications:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textit{Bleeding:} Intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage, which may necessitate blood transfusion or, rarely, re-operation to control the bleeding source.
      \item \textit{Infection:} Surgical site infection (e.g., port site infection) or intra-abdominal infection (e.g., abscess), requiring antibiotic treatment or drainage.
      \item \textit{Anesthesia Complications:} Adverse reactions to general anesthesia, including respiratory depression, cardiac arrhythmias, aspiration pneumonitis, or allergic reactions to medications.
      \item \textit{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE):} Formation of blood clots in the leg veins that can dislodge and travel to the lungs, a potentially life-threatening complication. Prophylaxis is routinely employed.
      \item \textit{Injury to Adjacent Organs:} Accidental injury to nearby structures such as the spleen, liver, or stomach during laparoscopic dissection.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textit{Esophageal Perforation:} The most serious and feared complication, occurring when the esophageal mucosa is inadvertently breached during the myotomy. This requires immediate repair, may necessitate a longer hospital stay, intravenous antibiotics, and potentially a drain or even a temporary diversion.
      \item \textit{Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD):} Despite the addition of a fundoplication, some patients may develop new-onset or worsened reflux symptoms, requiring long-term acid-suppressing medication (e.g., proton pump inhibitors) or, rarely, re-operation for refractory symptoms.
      \item \textit{Incomplete Myotomy:} Insufficient division of the muscle fibers, leading to persistent or recurrent dysphagia and potentially requiring re-intervention (e.g., pneumatic dilation, POEM, or repeat surgery).
      \item \textit{Esophageal Stricture:} Rare, but can occur due to scarring at the myotomy site or chronic reflux, causing recurrent dysphagia and requiring endoscopic dilation.
      \item \textit{Vagal Nerve Injury:} Damage to the anterior or posterior vagus nerves, which can lead to delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis) or other gastrointestinal motility issues, though this is uncommon with careful dissection.
      \item \textit{Pneumothorax/Pneumomediastinum:} Air leakage into the chest cavity or mediastinum, usually self-limiting but occasionally requiring a chest tube for drainage.
      \item \textit{Port Site Hernia:} Herniation of abdominal contents through one of the small incision sites, requiring surgical repair.
      \item \textit{Recurrence of Achalasia Symptoms:} While effective, a small percentage of patients may experience a return of dysphagia over time due to disease progression or myotomy failure, necessitating further evaluation and treatment.
      \item \textit{Dysphagia to Solids/Liquids:} Can occur if the fundoplication is too tight (wrap dysphagia) or if the myotomy is inadequate.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Immediately after a laparoscopic Heller cardiomyotomy, patients are transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for close monitoring of vital signs, pain levels, and recovery from general anesthesia. Pain is typically managed with intravenous analgesics, transitioning to oral medications as tolerated. A nasogastric tube (NGT), if placed, is usually removed either in the PACU or within the first few hours post-op to minimize discomfort and aspiration risk. Early ambulation is encouraged to prevent complications such as deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.

On the first postoperative day, a contrast swallow study (e.g., gastrografin swallow) is often performed to confirm the integrity of the esophageal repair and rule out any leaks or perforations before oral intake is initiated. If the study is clear, patients are typically started on a clear liquid diet, which is gradually advanced to a full liquid diet, then soft foods, and eventually a regular diet over the next 1-2 weeks. The typical hospital stay for an uncomplicated laparoscopic Heller myotomy is 1-2 days. Upon discharge, patients receive detailed instructions on diet progression, pain management, wound care, and activity restrictions. Strenuous activities, heavy lifting (over 10-15 pounds), and vigorous exercise are usually restricted for 4-6 weeks to allow for proper healing of the abdominal wall incisions and the myotomy site. Patients are advised to eat slowly, chew food thoroughly, and drink plenty of fluids with meals to aid passage through the newly relaxed LES. Full recovery and return to normal activities typically occur within 4-6 weeks.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During a Heller cardiomyotomy, the surgeon's intraoperative findings are crucial for confirming the diagnosis and guiding the procedure. Typically, the distal esophagus may appear dilated, sometimes significantly so, reflecting the chronic obstruction. The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) region is often visibly thickened and hypertrophied, appearing as a distinct muscular band that fails to relax. There should be no evidence of an obstructing mass or tumor at the gastroesophageal junction, which would suggest pseudoachalasia and necessitate a different surgical approach. The integrity of the esophageal mucosa is meticulously confirmed after the myotomy, ensuring no iatrogenic perforation.

Regarding pathology, no tissue is typically resected from the myotomy site for routine histopathological examination during a Heller cardiomyotomy. The diagnosis of achalasia is established preoperatively through manometry and barium swallow. However, the underlying pathology of achalasia, which is the loss of inhibitory ganglion cells within the myenteric plexus of the esophageal wall, is a key component of the disease's understanding. If a biopsy of the esophageal muscle or mucosa were taken (e.g., to rule out other conditions), the pathologist would look for specific findings such as inflammation, fibrosis, or the absence of ganglion cells. In the context of a Heller myotomy, the "pathology" primarily refers to the macroscopic surgical findings consistent with achalasia and the underlying microscopic neurodegenerative process that drives the disease.

\section{Expected Postoperative Course}
A normal and successful postoperative course following a Heller cardiomyotomy is characterized by a significant and sustained improvement in the patient's quality of life and resolution of achalasia-related symptoms. Immediately after surgery, patients will experience mild to moderate incisional pain, which is well-controlled with oral analgesics. They will gradually advance their diet from clear liquids to soft foods over the first few days, with a noticeable improvement in swallowing ability. Most patients are discharged within 1-2 days. Over the first few weeks, patients should experience a progressive resolution of dysphagia, allowing them to consume a more varied and regular diet without difficulty. Regurgitation, if present preoperatively, should cease or be significantly reduced. Patients often report an increase in appetite and begin to gain weight, improving their nutritional status. While some mild reflux symptoms may occur, especially if a fundoplication was not performed or is inadequate, these are usually manageable with proton pump inhibitors. The small laparoscopic incisions heal well, with minimal scarring, and patients can typically resume light activities within a week and full activities within 4-6 weeks.

\section{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative care and follow-up are crucial to monitor recovery, assess symptom resolution, and manage any potential complications after a Heller cardiomyotomy.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Initial Clinic Visit:} Typically scheduled 2-4 weeks after surgery to assess wound healing, discuss diet progression, review symptom improvement, and address any patient concerns.
  \item \textbf{Dietary Guidance:} Patients receive continued advice on eating slowly, chewing food thoroughly, drinking plenty of fluids with meals, and avoiding very dry or sticky foods, especially during the initial healing phase.
  \item \textbf{Medication Management:} Patients are often prescribed a course of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) post-surgery, and long-term use may be necessary if reflux symptoms persist or develop, even with a fundoplication.
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Gradual return to normal activities is encouraged, with restrictions on heavy lifting (typically >10-15 lbs) and strenuous abdominal exercise for 4-6 weeks to allow for proper healing of the abdominal wall.
  \item \textbf{Symptom Monitoring:} Patients are advised to monitor for any recurrence of dysphagia, regurgitation, chest pain, or new symptoms of severe gastroesophageal reflux.
  \item \textbf{Further Diagnostics (if symptoms recur):} If symptoms return or worsen, further investigations such as a barium swallow, upper endoscopy, or repeat esophageal manometry may be performed to identify the cause (e.g., incomplete myotomy, stricture formation, severe reflux, or recurrent achalasia).
\end{itemize}
Patients should contact their surgeon immediately if they experience severe abdominal pain, fever, persistent nausea or vomiting, inability to swallow, or signs of wound infection (redness, swelling, purulent discharge).

\section*{Epidemiology}
Achalasia is a relatively rare esophageal motility disorder, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1-2 cases per 100,000 individuals per year and a prevalence of about 10-15 cases per 100,000 population worldwide. It typically affects individuals in their prime adult years, most commonly between 25 and 60 years of age, though it can occur at any age, including in children and the elderly. There is no significant sex predilection, with men and women being affected almost equally. Heller cardiomyotomy is one of the most frequently performed definitive treatments for achalasia, with thousands of procedures performed annually globally. The primary indications for the procedure are persistent dysphagia and regurgitation refractory to medical management or less invasive endoscopic therapies. While the overall mortality rate associated with laparoscopic Heller myotomy is very low (typically less than 0.1-0.2\%), morbidity rates, primarily related to esophageal perforation (1-5\%) and postoperative reflux (10-30\% requiring medication), are more significant but generally manageable. The procedure has a high success rate in relieving dysphagia, making it a cornerstone of achalasia management.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The long-term outcomes of laparoscopic Heller cardiomyotomy with partial fundoplication are generally excellent, with high rates of symptom relief and improved quality of life. Success rates, defined as significant improvement in dysphagia and absence of severe regurgitation, range from 85\% to 95\% in most large series, with sustained relief often lasting for over a decade. Functional outcomes include the ability to consume a regular diet, maintain a healthy weight, and return to normal daily activities. Survival outcomes are generally not impacted by the procedure itself, as achalasia is not a life-threatening condition, but rather by the patient's underlying health status and comorbidities.

Recurrence of dysphagia occurs in approximately 5-10\% of patients over 5-10 years, which may be due to an incomplete myotomy, progressive esophageal dysfunction, or the development of a stricture. Prognostic factors influencing success include the patient's age (younger patients often have better long-term results), the duration of symptoms, the degree of esophageal dilation prior to surgery, and the specific achalasia subtype (Type II achalasia generally has the best response). While postoperative gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a common complication, it is usually well-controlled with proton pump inhibitors. In cases of recurrent or persistent symptoms, further interventions such as repeat myotomy, pneumatic dilation, peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM), or, in rare severe cases of end-stage achalasia (sigmoid esophagus), esophagectomy may be considered. Overall, Heller cardiomyotomy provides a durable and effective solution for the vast majority of patients with achalasia, significantly improving their quality of life.
