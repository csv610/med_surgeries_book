\chapter{Cornea Transplant}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Cornea transplant, also known as keratoplasty, is a complex surgical procedure that involves the replacement of a diseased or damaged cornea with healthy donor corneal tissue. The cornea is the transparent, dome-shaped outer layer of the eye, crucial for focusing light onto the retina and protecting intraocular structures. This surgery is primarily indicated for patients suffering from corneal opacification, distortion, or thinning, which can lead to significant visual impairment or debilitating ocular pain. Corneal transplantation is a vital therapeutic option for various conditions that cannot be effectively managed through less invasive medical or surgical interventions. The procedure not only aims to restore vision but also significantly enhances the quality of life for patients afflicted with corneal blindness or severe discomfort. The success of corneal transplantation is supported by the cornea's unique immunological properties and ongoing advancements in microsurgical techniques and postoperative care protocols.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Keratoplasty
  \item Corneal Graft
  \item Full-Thickness Corneal Transplant
  \item Partial-Thickness Corneal Transplant
  \item Endothelial Keratoplasty
  \item Penetrating Keratoplasty (PKP)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The cornea is a specialized, avascular, transparent tissue that constitutes the anterior one-sixth of the fibrous tunic of the eye. It has an average diameter of approximately 11.5 mm and a central thickness of about 550 micrometers, which gradually increases towards the periphery. The cornea is composed of five primary layers: the corneal epithelium, Bowman's layer, corneal stroma, Descemet's membrane, and the corneal endothelium. A recently identified sixth layer, Dua's layer, lies between the posterior stroma and Descemet's membrane. 

The epithelium serves as a barrier against infection and provides a smooth optical surface. Bowman's layer is a strong, acellular collagenous layer that contributes to the cornea's structural integrity. The stroma, which makes up about 90\% of the cornea's thickness, consists of organized collagen lamellae and keratocytes, which are essential for maintaining corneal transparency. Descemet's membrane acts as the basement membrane for the endothelium, which is a single layer of hexagonal cells responsible for maintaining corneal deturgescence by actively pumping fluid out of the stroma.

The cornea receives nutrients from the aqueous humor and tear film, and its avascular nature contributes to its immune privilege. Sensory innervation is rich, primarily supplied by the long ciliary nerves, branches of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V1). Lymphatic drainage is virtually absent within the cornea itself, although lymphatic vessels are present at the limbus. Key surgical landmarks include the limbus, which marks the transition from cornea to sclera, and the anterior chamber, the space between the cornea and the iris/lens filled with aqueous humor. Understanding these anatomical relationships is crucial for surgical planning and execution, particularly in selecting the appropriate keratoplasty technique to target specific diseased layers while preserving healthy ones.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The primary surgical principle of corneal transplantation is the precise replacement of diseased or dysfunctional corneal tissue with healthy donor tissue to restore the cornea's essential functions: optical clarity and structural integrity. The rationale for this procedure is based on the cornea's unique biological characteristics, particularly its avascularity and the immune-privileged environment of the anterior chamber, which collectively contribute to a lower risk of allograft rejection compared to other solid organ transplants.

Modern keratoplasty techniques are tailored to the specific corneal layer affected by disease. For full-thickness pathologies such as severe scarring or corneal perforation, Penetrating Keratoplasty (PKP) is employed, replacing all corneal layers to restore both optical and structural integrity. In cases where only the anterior stromal layers are diseased, as seen in certain forms of keratoconus or superficial scars, Deep Anterior Lamellar Keratoplasty (DALK) is preferred, preserving the patient's healthy Descemet's membrane and endothelium. This approach significantly reduces the risk of endothelial graft rejection, the most common cause of graft failure. For conditions primarily affecting the corneal endothelium, such as Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy, endothelial keratoplasty techniques like Descemet's Stripping Endothelial Keratoplasty (DSEK) and Descemet's Membrane Endothelial Keratoplasty (DMEK) are utilized. These procedures selectively replace only the diseased endothelial layer and Descemet's membrane, preserving the patient's healthy anterior cornea. The rationale for these selective techniques is to minimize surgical invasiveness, accelerate visual recovery, and reduce the risk of immune rejection by transplanting less tissue.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The concept of corneal transplantation dates back to the early 19th century, with initial experimental attempts by surgeons such as Franz Reisinger in 1824, which were largely unsuccessful due to rudimentary surgical techniques and a lack of understanding of tissue rejection. A significant milestone occurred in 1905 when Austrian ophthalmologist Eduard Zirm performed the first successful full-thickness corneal transplant (PKP), restoring vision in a patient with severe corneal burns using tissue from an enucleated eye.

The mid-20th century saw refinements in PKP techniques, driven by pioneers like Ramón Castroviejo, who developed specialized trephines and suturing methods that improved graft survival and visual outcomes. The introduction of corticosteroids in the 1950s revolutionized postoperative management, helping to control inflammation and reduce graft rejection. The late 20th and early 21st centuries marked a paradigm shift with the advent of lamellar and endothelial keratoplasty techniques. Gerrit Melles introduced posterior lamellar keratoplasty in the late 1990s, which evolved into DSEK and DMEK, allowing for more selective and less invasive tissue replacement. These advancements have progressively refined corneal surgery, optimizing outcomes for specific corneal pathologies.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Corneal transplantation is indicated for a variety of corneal pathologies that result in significant visual impairment or intractable ocular pain, particularly when medical management or less invasive surgical interventions are insufficient.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Advanced keratoconus or pellucid marginal degeneration that cannot be adequately corrected with rigid gas permeable contact lenses or other interventions.
  \item Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy, leading to chronic corneal edema and bullous keratopathy.
  \item Corneal scarring from trauma, infectious keratitis, or complications from previous ocular surgeries.
  \item Pseudophakic or aphakic bullous keratopathy due to endothelial decompensation following cataract extraction.
  \item Corneal perforation or severe thinning due to infection or autoimmune diseases.
  \item Failure of a previous corneal graft necessitating re-transplantation.
  \item Severe corneal edema from congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy in infants or children.
  \item Painful bullous keratopathy in eyes with poor visual potential.
  \item Corneal dystrophies causing significant visual compromise due to stromal opacification.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Corneal transplantation can serve as both a primary and secondary treatment modality, depending on the underlying corneal pathology and the patient's overall ocular health. For conditions such as advanced Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy or severe corneal scarring, keratoplasty is often considered a primary intervention, as vision loss is significant and irreversible through medical management.

Conversely, for conditions like keratoconus, corneal transplantation is frequently a secondary procedure. Initial management typically involves non-surgical options such as rigid gas permeable contact lenses or corneal collagen cross-linking. A transplant is reserved for cases where these treatments fail or the disease progresses to a stage where contact lens wear is no longer effective. Similarly, corneal transplantation may be a salvage procedure for complications arising from previous ocular surgeries or for the failure of earlier grafts.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Corneal transplant surgery is a highly specialized microsurgical procedure typically performed under local anesthesia with intravenous sedation, although general anesthesia may be used for anxious patients or complex cases. The patient is positioned supine, and the head is stabilized. The specific operative steps vary based on the type of keratoplasty performed.

\paragraph{}
**1. Penetrating Keratoplasty (PKP) - Full-Thickness Transplant:**
\begin{enumerate}
  \item **Anesthesia and Preparation:** Local anesthetic injection or general anesthesia is administered. The eye is prepped and draped, and a lid speculum is inserted.
  \item **Recipient Cornea Trephination:** A circular trephine is used to create a full-thickness incision in the diseased cornea, removing the central corneal button.
  \item **Donor Cornea Preparation:** A healthy donor cornea is prepared, typically matched in size to ensure a tight fit.
  \item **Donor Graft Placement:** The donor corneal button is placed into the recipient bed.
  \item **Suturing:** The graft is secured with fine sutures, adjusting tension to minimize postoperative astigmatism.
  \item **Anterior Chamber Reformation:** The anterior chamber is reformed with balanced salt solution, and intraocular pressure is checked.
  \item **Closure:** Topical antibiotic and steroid are applied, and an eye shield is placed.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{}
**2. Deep Anterior Lamellar Keratoplasty (DALK) - Anterior Stromal Transplant:**
\begin{enumerate}
  \item **Anesthesia and Preparation:** Similar to PKP.
  \item **Partial-Thickness Trephination:** A partial-thickness trephination is performed, leaving Descemet's membrane intact.
  \item **Stromal Dissection (Big Bubble Technique):** Air or viscoelastic is injected to create a "big bubble" for clean dissection.
  \item **Removal of Diseased Stroma:** Diseased anterior stromal layers are dissected and removed.
  \item **Donor Cornea Preparation:** A donor corneal cap is prepared.
  \item **Donor Graft Placement and Suturing:** The donor cap is placed onto the recipient Descemet's membrane and secured.
  \item **Closure:** Topical medications and an eye shield are applied.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{}
**3. Endothelial Keratoplasty (DSEK/DMEK) - Endothelial Transplant:**
\begin{enumerate}
  \item **Anesthesia and Preparation:** Similar to PKP, often with a retrobulbar block.
  \item **Small Incision:** A small limbal incision is created.
  \item **Descemet's Membrane Stripping:** The diseased Descemet's membrane and endothelium are stripped from the recipient cornea.
  \item **Donor Tissue Preparation:** A donor corneal lamella is prepared for DSEK or a thin graft for DMEK.
  \item **Donor Tissue Insertion:** The donor tissue is folded and inserted into the anterior chamber.
  \item **Donor Tissue Unfolding and Positioning:** The donor tissue is unfolded and positioned against the recipient stroma.
  \item **Air/Gas Bubble Injection:** An air or gas bubble is injected to facilitate adhesion.
  \item **Closure:** The small incision is self-sealing or closed with a single suture. Topical medications and an eye shield are applied.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A thorough preoperative workup is essential for optimizing the success of a corneal transplant. This evaluation includes a detailed ophthalmic examination, assessing visual acuity, intraocular pressure, corneal topography, and endothelial cell density.

\paragraph{}
Key preparations include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Systemic Medical Evaluation:** Comprehensive medical history and physical examination, including routine blood tests and EKG.
  \item **Infection Screening and Treatment:** Identification and treatment of any active ocular or systemic infections.
  \item **Medication Review and Adjustment:** Review of current medications, particularly anticoagulants.
  \item **NPO Status:** Patients are instructed to be nil per os (NPO) for 6-8 hours before surgery.
  \item **Prophylactic Antibiotics:** Topical antibiotics are prescribed leading up to the surgery.
  \item **Informed Consent:** Detailed discussion regarding the procedure, risks, and expected outcomes.
  \item **Donor Tissue Procurement and Screening:** Ensuring donor corneal tissue meets safety standards.
  \item **Ocular Surface Optimization:** Treatment of pre-existing ocular surface diseases.
  \item **Patient Education:** Educating patients on postoperative care, particularly for endothelial keratoplasty.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
Identifying contraindications and precautions is crucial for ensuring patient safety and optimizing graft survival.

\paragraph{}
**Absolute Contraindications:**
\begin{itemize}
  \item Active, uncontrolled ocular infection.
  \item Uncontrolled severe glaucoma.
  \item Severe ocular surface disease that precludes proper healing.
  \item Significant systemic illness rendering the patient unfit for surgery.
  \item Lack of potential for visual improvement due to severe underlying retinal or optic nerve disease.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{}
**Relative Contraindications and Precautions:**
\begin{itemize}
  \item Advanced corneal neovascularization.
  \item History of multiple previous graft failures.
  \item Uncontrolled chronic ocular inflammation.
  \item Poor patient compliance with postoperative medication.
  \item Monocular patients where risks must be weighed against potential benefits.
  \item Pre-existing glaucoma requiring careful monitoring.
  \item History of herpes simplex keratitis necessitating prophylactic antiviral medication.
  \item Significant psychological or cognitive impairment affecting adherence to postoperative instructions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Corneal transplantation, while generally safe, carries potential risks and complications that can be categorized into general surgical complications and procedure-specific risks.

\paragraph{}
**General Surgical Complications:**
\begin{itemize}
  \item **Bleeding:** Intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage.
  \item **Infection:** Risk of endophthalmitis.
  \item **Anesthesia Complications:** Risks associated with local or general anesthesia.
  \item **Pain:** Postoperative pain, typically manageable with analgesics.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{}
**Procedure-Specific Risks:**
\begin{itemize}
  \item **Graft Rejection:** Immune response against the donor tissue.
  \item **Primary Graft Failure:** Failure of the donor cornea to function properly.
  \item **High Astigmatism:** Irregular healing or uneven suture tension.
  \item **Glaucoma:** Elevated intraocular pressure postoperatively.
  \item **Retinal Detachment:** Rare but serious complication.
  \item **Wound Leak or Dehiscence:** Inadequate wound closure leading to complications.
  \item **Cataract Formation or Progression:** Development of cataracts postoperatively.
  \item **Persistent Epithelial Defects:** Failure of the epithelium to heal.
  \item **Suture-Related Complications:** Issues with sutures causing discomfort or infection.
  \item **Dislocation of Endothelial Graft:** Detachment of the donor endothelial tissue.
  \item **Endothelial Cell Loss:** Accelerated loss of endothelial cells post-surgery.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Following corneal transplant surgery, patients are closely monitored in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) as they recover from anesthesia. An eye shield is placed over the operated eye for protection. Most patients are discharged the same day or the following morning.

\paragraph{}
During the first 24-48 hours, adherence to postoperative instructions is critical. For patients undergoing endothelial keratoplasty, specific head positioning is often required to ensure proper graft adhesion. Topical medications, including antibiotics and corticosteroids, are prescribed and must be administered diligently. Patients should avoid activities that could increase intraocular pressure. Follow-up visits are scheduled frequently to monitor graft clarity and detect early signs of complications. Over the first 1-2 weeks, steroid dosages may be tapered based on the eye's inflammatory response. Long-term, topical corticosteroids are typically continued for several months to years, particularly for PKP, to maintain immune suppression and prevent rejection. Visual recovery is gradual, often taking several weeks to months for endothelial grafts and longer for full-thickness grafts.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During the corneal transplant procedure, the surgeon documents intraoperative findings, including the dimensions of the trephination and the condition of the host cornea. The pathology report on resected tissues confirms the clinical diagnosis and provides evidence of conditions such as keratoconus or Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy. This report is vital for understanding the disease process and guiding long-term management.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course following corneal transplant is characterized by gradual improvement in visual acuity and a stable, clear corneal graft. Initially, vision may be blurry due to corneal swelling or the presence of an air/gas bubble. Pain is typically mild to moderate and resolves within days to a week. As healing progresses, vision should steadily improve, with the donor cornea remaining transparent and free from inflammation. Patients should experience a resolution of preoperative symptoms, leading to significant enhancement in quality of life and functional vision.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative care for corneal transplant patients is rigorous and requires adherence to medication regimens and a structured follow-up schedule.

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Frequent Slit-Lamp Examinations:** Initially daily or weekly, tapering to monthly and annual visits.
  \item **Intraocular Pressure (IOP) Checks:** Performed at every visit to monitor for glaucoma.
  \item **Topical Medication Adjustments:** Dosages of medications are frequently adjusted based on clinical findings.
  \item **Suture Management:** Selective suture removal may commence several months post-op.
  \item **Refraction and Corrective Lenses:** New refraction performed once the eye stabilizes.
  \item **Corneal Topography:** Periodically used to monitor corneal shape and astigmatism.
  \item **Specular Microscopy:** Monitors endothelial cell count for long-term graft health.
  \item **Rehabilitation:** Referral to vision rehabilitation specialists if needed.
\end{itemize}

Patients are educated to recognize and report warning signs such as sudden vision changes or increased redness, as these may indicate graft rejection or infection.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Corneal transplantation is one of the most frequently performed and successful forms of solid organ transplantation globally. In the United States, approximately 40,000 to 50,000 corneal transplants are performed annually. The incidence of corneal diseases necessitating transplantation varies by region and demographic factors. Corneal transplants are performed across all age groups, with leading indications evolving over time. Currently, Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy is the most common indication for endothelial keratoplasty, while keratoconus remains significant for full-thickness or deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty. The direct mortality associated with corneal transplant surgery is exceedingly low, with morbidity primarily related to complications such as graft rejection and infection.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes of corneal transplantation are generally excellent, with high success rates. One-year graft survival rates for PKP typically range from 80-90\%, while for endothelial keratoplasty techniques, they often exceed 90-95\%. Five-year survival rates are favorable, generally ranging from 60-80\% for PKP and 80-90\% for endothelial keratoplasty. Functional outcomes vary, with many patients achieving good functional vision. Recurrence of the original disease in donor tissue is rare, although conditions like herpetic keratitis may recur. Graft rejection remains a lifelong risk, necessitating long-term immunosuppression. Prognostic factors influencing outcomes include the degree of corneal vascularization, presence of ocular inflammation, and patient compliance with postoperative care.
