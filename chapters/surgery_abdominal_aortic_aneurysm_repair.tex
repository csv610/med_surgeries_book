\chapter{Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Repair}

\section*{Introduction \& Clinical Indications}
Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) repair is a critical surgical intervention aimed at addressing a pathologically dilated and weakened segment of the abdominal aorta. This procedure is primarily performed to prevent the life-threatening event of aneurysm rupture, which can lead to massive internal hemorrhage and death. The surgery typically targets aneurysms located in the infrarenal aorta, although juxtarenal or suprarenal aneurysms may require more complex surgical techniques. Open repair involves the replacement of the affected aortic segment with a synthetic graft, providing a durable solution to restore normal blood flow and prevent future complications. Despite the rise of endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) as a less invasive alternative, open AAA repair remains a definitive treatment option, particularly for patients with complex anatomical considerations, prior failed EVAR, or those presenting with ruptured aneurysms. The significance of this surgery lies in its ability to reduce mortality associated with AAA and improve long-term outcomes for patients.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Open Abdominal Aortic Aneurysmectomy
  \item Aortic Graft Placement for Aneurysm
  \item Aneurysmectomy with Graft Interposition
  \item Conventional AAA Repair
  \item Infrarenal Aortic Aneurysm Repair
  \item Aortic Bypass for Aneurysm
  \item Aortoiliac Bypass (if bifurcated graft used)
\end{itemize}

The primary surgical principle of open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair is the definitive exclusion of the aneurysmal segment from systemic circulation, thereby restoring normal aortic continuity and blood flow. This is achieved through a series of carefully orchestrated steps: the aorta is exposed, clamped above and below the aneurysm to control blood flow, and the aneurysm sac is incised. Any intraluminal thrombus is meticulously removed to ensure a clean surgical field. A synthetic graft, typically made from Dacron or PTFE, is then sewn into place, creating a new conduit for blood flow. The proximal anastomosis is performed just below the renal arteries, while the distal anastomoses are made to the common iliac arteries or the distal aorta.

The rationale behind this approach is to eliminate the risk of rupture associated with the weakened aortic wall while providing a durable pathway for blood flow. Technical goals include achieving secure and hemostatic anastomoses that can withstand systemic arterial pressure, maintaining adequate perfusion to vital organs during clamping, and ensuring long-term graft patency. The choice between a tube graft and a bifurcated graft depends on the anatomical extent of the aneurysm, with the aim of restoring normal blood flow to both lower extremities.

The surgical treatment of aortic aneurysms has evolved significantly since the early 20th century. Initial attempts at managing AAAs involved ligation or wrapping of the aneurysm, often with poor outcomes. A landmark achievement occurred in 1951 when Dr. Charles Dubost performed the first successful resection of an abdominal aortic aneurysm, replacing it with a homograft. This pioneering work laid the foundation for direct surgical repair.

In the mid-1950s, the development of synthetic graft materials, particularly Dacron, by Dr. Arthur Voorhees and Dr. Arthur Blakemore revolutionized vascular surgery. These materials provided a durable and biocompatible option for grafting, overcoming the limitations of homografts.

Throughout the latter half of the 20th century, open AAA repair became a standardized and effective procedure, with advancements in surgical techniques, anesthetic management, and perioperative care leading to improved patient outcomes. The introduction of endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) in the 1990s by Dr. Juan Parodi offered a less invasive alternative, but open repair remains a vital option for complex cases, ruptured aneurysms, or when EVAR is unsuitable.

Open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair is indicated in several clinical scenarios to prevent the life-threatening complication of aneurysm rupture or to address existing complications. Key indications include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Aneurysm size: Typically indicated for infrarenal or juxtarenal AAAs measuring 5.5 cm or larger in men, or 5.0 cm or larger in women, due to increased rupture risk.
  \item Rapid aneurysm expansion: An increase in diameter of 0.5 cm or more within a 6-month period, indicating an unstable aneurysm.
  \item Symptomatic aneurysm: Presence of new or worsening abdominal, back, or flank pain, which may suggest impending rupture or rapid expansion.
  \item Aneurysm rupture: An emergent procedure performed immediately when an aneurysm has ruptured, presenting with severe pain, hypotension, and shock.
  \item Distal embolization: Evidence of thrombus within the aneurysm sac causing emboli to lower extremities or other organs, leading to acute limb ischemia.
  \item Anatomical unsuitability for EVAR: When aneurysm morphology precludes safe and effective endovascular repair.
  \item Infected (mycotic) aneurysm: Open repair is preferred for infected aneurysms due to the high risk of graft infection with endovascular approaches.
  \item Failed previous EVAR: Indicated for patients with complications after EVAR requiring conversion to open repair.
\end{itemize}

Open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair serves as both a primary and secondary definitive treatment for AAA, depending on the clinical context. Historically, it was the only definitive surgical treatment available. With the advent of EVAR, the role of open repair has evolved but remains indispensable.

As a primary procedure, open repair is chosen when a patient meets the size or symptomatic criteria for intervention and is a suitable surgical candidate, but their aneurysm anatomy is not amenable to EVAR. This includes cases with a very short or absent infrarenal neck, severe neck angulation, or extensive iliac artery disease.

As a secondary procedure, open repair is indicated for patients who have previously undergone EVAR but developed complications such as persistent or enlarging endoleaks, graft infection, or rupture. In emergent situations like ruptured AAA, open repair remains a critical option, especially if EVAR is technically challenging or equipment is unavailable.

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The abdominal aorta is the largest artery in the abdomen, originating from the diaphragm and bifurcating into the common iliac arteries at the level of the fourth lumbar vertebra (L4). The most common site for an abdominal aortic aneurysm is the infrarenal segment, which is located below the renal arteries. Understanding the relevant anatomy is crucial for successful surgical intervention.

Key anatomical structures involved in AAA repair include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Abdominal Aorta:** The primary vessel of concern, often affected by atherosclerosis, which leads to dilation. It lies anterior to the lumbar vertebrae and is covered by the posterior peritoneum.
  \item **Renal Arteries:** These arise laterally from the aorta at the level of L1-L2 and must be preserved during surgery to maintain renal perfusion.
  \item \textbf{Inferior Mesenteric Artery (IMA):} This artery supplies the distal colon. Its ostium is assessed, but routine preservation or reimplantation is not required; reimplantation is considered when collateral perfusion is inadequate or colonic ischemia is a concern.
  \item **Lumbar Arteries:** These paired arteries supply the posterior abdominal wall and are typically ligated during the procedure.
  \item **Common Iliac Arteries:** The terminal branches of the aorta, which further divide into internal and external iliac arteries. Anastomoses for bifurcated grafts are made to these arteries.
  \item **Left Renal Vein:** This vein crosses anterior to the aorta and must be carefully mobilized during dissection.
  \item **Duodenum:** The third part of the duodenum lies anterior to the aorta and must be mobilized to access the infrarenal aorta.
  \item **Ureters:** These structures lie laterally to the aorta and must be identified and protected during dissection.
  \item **Sympathetic Nerves:** Plexuses of sympathetic nerves are located around the aorta, and injury to these nerves can lead to complications such as erectile dysfunction.
\end{itemize}

The retroperitoneal space, where the aorta resides, is accessed via a transperitoneal approach, necessitating careful mobilization of overlying viscera.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A comprehensive preoperative workup is essential for patients undergoing open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair to optimize their condition and minimize risks. This evaluation aims to identify and mitigate cardiac, pulmonary, and renal comorbidities.

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Comprehensive Medical Evaluation:**
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Cardiac Assessment:} ECG and further cardiac testing are guided by symptoms, functional capacity, and comorbidity; routine stress testing is not required for every patient.
      \item **Pulmonary Assessment:** PFTs for patients with a history of smoking or lung disease; smoking cessation is encouraged.
      \item **Renal Assessment:** Serum creatinine, BUN, and eGFR to assess kidney function, as renal complications are a significant risk.
      \item **Hematologic Assessment:** CBC, coagulation profile, and blood type and cross-match for several units of blood.
    \end{itemize}
  \item **Imaging:** CTA of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast to map the aneurysm's anatomy and its relation to surrounding structures.
      \item \textbf{Medication Management:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Initiate or continue beta-blockers to reduce cardiac risk.
      \item Manage anticoagulants and antiplatelet medications with the prescribing and vascular teams; routine interruption is not appropriate for every drug or indication.
      \item Tight control of blood glucose levels.
    \end{itemize}
  \item **Bowel Preparation:** Some surgeons may order mechanical bowel preparation to reduce infection risk.
  \item **NPO Status:** Patients must be NPO for at least 6-8 hours prior to surgery.
  \item **Antibiotic Prophylaxis:** Administer intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics shortly before incision.
  \item **Informed Consent:** Discuss the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives with the patient and family.
\end{itemize}

Open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair is a major surgery with significant physiological demands, necessitating careful patient selection.

\textbf{Factors requiring optimization or a change in strategy:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Active infection or sepsis:} Requires urgent multidisciplinary management; infection is not an automatic reason to withhold repair when the aneurysm itself is infected or ruptured.
  \item \textbf{Severe, uncorrectable coagulopathy:} Requires correction when possible and individualized operative planning.
  \item \textbf{Prohibitive operative risk or limited life expectancy:} The balance of repair, surveillance, EVAR, palliation, and patient goals must be considered.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative risks and precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Severe cardiopulmonary disease:} Increases perioperative risk and may favor EVAR or nonoperative management after shared decision-making.
  \item **Extreme Age or Frailty:** Diminished physiological reserve complicates recovery.
  \item **Hostile Abdomen:** Previous surgeries leading to dense adhesions can complicate access.
  \item **Morbid Obesity:** Increases risks of wound complications and respiratory issues.
  \item **Renal Insufficiency:** Higher risk for acute kidney injury postoperatively.
  \item **Connective Tissue Disorders:** Fragile aortic tissue increases surgical challenges.
  \item \textbf{Inflammatory aneurysm:} Requires careful dissection and specialist planning because of dense fibrosis.
  \item \textbf{Horseshoe kidney:} Can complicate proximal clamping and renal or accessory-artery preservation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair is performed under general anesthesia, often supplemented with epidural anesthesia for postoperative pain control.

The patient is positioned supine on the operating table. A long midline incision is made from the xiphoid process to the pubis, or a transverse incision may be used. The small bowel is retracted to the right side of the abdomen using a self-retaining retractor, exposing the retroperitoneum. The posterior peritoneum overlying the aorta is incised, and the duodenum is mobilized to access the infrarenal aorta. The aorta is carefully dissected free from surrounding tissues, ensuring adequate length for clamping.

Control of the aorta is achieved by placing vascular clamps: one proximally, just below the renal arteries, and two distally on the common iliac arteries. Heparin is administered intravenously before clamping to prevent thrombus formation.

Once the aorta is clamped, the aneurysm sac is incised longitudinally, and any intraluminal thrombus is meticulously removed. The lumbar arteries are identified and ligated to prevent back-bleeding. The IMA ostium is assessed; if patent, it may be reimplanted into the graft to prevent ischemia.

A synthetic graft, either a tube or bifurcated graft, is selected based on the aneurysm's extent. The proximal anastomosis is performed first, sewing the graft to the healthy aorta just below the renal arteries. The distal anastomoses are then completed, connecting the graft limbs to the common iliac arteries or the distal aorta. Clamps are briefly released to flush out any air or debris before finalizing the anastomoses.

Hemostasis is meticulously achieved, and the native aneurysm sac is closed over the graft to prevent aortoenteric fistula formation. The posterior peritoneum is closed, and the abdominal wall is closed in layers, often with retention sutures in high-risk patients. Drains may be placed in the retroperitoneum or abdominal cavity before closure.

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair carries significant risks, both general surgical risks and those specific to aortic surgery.

\begin{itemize}
  \item **General Surgical Risks:**
    \begin{itemize}
      \item **Bleeding:** Significant intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage.
      \item **Infection:** Surgical site infection, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, or graft infection.
      \item **Anesthesia Complications:** Myocardial infarction, stroke, adverse drug reactions, aspiration pneumonia, DVT leading to PE.
      \item **Pain:** Significant postoperative pain requiring intensive analgesia.
    \end{itemize}
  \item **Procedure-Specific Risks:**
    \begin{itemize}
      \item **Renal Failure:** Acute kidney injury, especially with suprarenal clamping.
      \item **Bowel Ischemia:** Insufficient blood supply to the colon, potentially requiring colectomy.
      \item **Spinal Cord Ischemia:** Rare interruption of blood flow leading to neurological deficits.
      \item **Graft Infection:** A rare but devastating complication that can lead to sepsis.
      \item **Anastomotic Pseudoaneurysm:** Weakness at the suture line leading to contained rupture.
      \item **Distal Embolization:** Release of plaque or thrombus causing ischemia in lower extremities.
      \item **Lymphatic Leak/Lymphocele:** Injury to lymphatic channels leading to fluid collection.
      \item **Erectile Dysfunction/Ejaculatory Dysfunction:** Injury to sympathetic nerves during dissection.
      \item **Incisional Hernia:** Weakness in abdominal wall closure leading to hernia formation.
      \item **Aortoenteric Fistula:** Rare connection between the graft and gastrointestinal tract leading to bleeding.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Recovery}
Following open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair, patients are typically transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring. This includes continuous monitoring of vital signs, cardiac rhythm, and urine output. Mechanical ventilation may be required initially, with extubation usually occurring within 12-24 hours.

Pain management is prioritized, often utilizing epidural analgesia or patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). Fluid balance is carefully managed to maintain adequate hydration and renal perfusion, and blood transfusions may be necessary. Early ambulation is encouraged within 24-48 hours to prevent complications such as DVT and pulmonary embolism.

Over the next several days, the patient's condition is monitored closely. Bowel function, often temporarily impaired due to surgery, is observed, with a clear liquid diet initiated once bowel sounds return. Surgical drains are typically removed within 2-5 days. The hospital stay for open AAA repair generally ranges from 4 to 10 days, depending on recovery and complications. Upon discharge, patients receive instructions on wound care, activity restrictions, and medication management. Full recovery can take several weeks to months, with gradual return to normal activities.

During open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair, the surgeon documents several key findings, including the location and extent of the aneurysm, maximum diameter, presence of intraluminal thrombus, and degree of calcification. The quality of the aortic neck and distal vessels for anastomosis is also noted, along with any inflammatory changes or adherence to adjacent structures.

The pathology report on the resected aneurysm sac typically confirms the diagnosis of AAA, describing severe atherosclerosis with degeneration of the media and chronic inflammation. It rules out other pathologies such as inflammatory or mycotic aneurysms, which may influence long-term management. The presence of intraluminal thrombus is also documented.

A normal postoperative course following open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair is characterized by a progressive recovery, leading to a significant reduction in the risk of aneurysm rupture. Patients can expect initial discomfort, which should be well-managed with analgesia. Bowel function typically returns within 3-5 days, allowing for a gradual advancement of diet. The surgical incision should heal without signs of infection, and mobility will gradually improve.

Vital signs should remain stable, and urine output should be adequate, indicating good renal function. There should be no signs of major complications such as persistent bleeding or graft infection. Patients may experience fatigue for several weeks, but the ultimate goal is to return to baseline functional status, free from aneurysm-related symptoms.

Long-term follow-up is essential after open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair to monitor for potential complications and manage cardiovascular risk factors.

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Postoperative Clinic Visits:** Initial follow-up visits are typically scheduled at 2-4 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months post-surgery.
  \item **Suture/Staple Removal:** Non-absorbable sutures or staples are removed around 10-14 days post-op.
  \item **Imaging Surveillance:**
    \begin{itemize}
      \item An initial CT scan is often performed 3-6 months post-surgery to confirm graft integrity.
      \item Subsequent surveillance typically involves annual or biennial ultrasound or CT scans.
    \end{itemize}
  \item **Cardiovascular Risk Factor Management:** Lifelong management of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes is critical.
  \item **Physical Therapy/Rehabilitation:** Some patients may benefit from structured rehabilitation programs.
  \item **Activity Resumption:** Guidelines for gradually resuming physical activities are provided, typically avoiding heavy lifting for several weeks.
  \item **Warning Signs:** Patients are educated on warning signs that require immediate medical attention.
\end{itemize}

Adherence to these follow-up recommendations is crucial for optimizing long-term outcomes and detecting potential complications early.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) are a significant public health concern, with open repair being a critical intervention. The prevalence of AAA varies, affecting approximately 4-8\% of men over 65 years of age and 0.5-1.5\% of women in the same age group. The incidence increases with age, peaking in the seventh and eighth decades of life. Men are 4-6 times more likely to develop AAAs than women, although women tend to rupture at smaller diameters and have a higher mortality rate from rupture. Major risk factors include smoking, advanced age, male sex, family history, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and coronary artery disease. The primary indications for open repair include large aneurysm size, rapid expansion, or symptoms. Elective open AAA repair has a reported mortality rate of 2-5\%, while emergent repair of a ruptured AAA carries a mortality rate of 50-80\%.

The outcomes and prognosis following elective open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair are generally excellent, particularly in carefully selected patients. The 5-year survival rate for elective open AAA repair typically ranges from 70-85\%, with most late deaths attributable to cardiovascular events rather than aneurysm-related issues. Functional outcomes are generally good, with most patients returning to baseline activity levels within a few months. Recurrence of the aneurysm in the treated segment is not possible due to graft replacement, but patients remain at risk for developing new aneurysms in other locations. Prognostic factors for favorable outcomes include younger age, fewer comorbidities, and elective presentation. Conversely, patients undergoing emergent repair for ruptured AAA face a significantly poorer prognosis, with high mortality rates despite successful surgery.
