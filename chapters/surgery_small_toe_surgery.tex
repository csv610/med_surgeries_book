\chapter{Surgery for Problems of the Small Toes}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Surgery for problems of the small toes, commonly referred to as lesser toe surgery, encompasses a variety of orthopedic procedures aimed at correcting painful deformities of the second, third, fourth, and fifth toes. These deformities include conditions such as hammer toe, claw toe, and mallet toe, which are characterized by abnormal flexion or extension at the metatarsophalangeal (MTP), proximal interphalangeal (PIP), or distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints. The surgical interventions are designed to restore proper anatomical alignment, alleviate pain, eliminate pressure points that lead to corns and calluses, and enhance the patient's ability to wear shoes comfortably and ambulate without discomfort. The procedures typically involve a combination of soft tissue releases, joint resections (arthroplasty), joint fusions (arthrodesis), and bone shortening or realignment osteotomies. This surgical approach is essential for patients whose quality of life is significantly affected by chronic foot pain and functional limitations, particularly when conservative management strategies have failed.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lesser Toe Deformity Correction
  \item Digital Arthroplasty
  \item Digital Arthrodesis
  \item Hammertoe Repair
  \item Clawtoe Correction
  \item Mallet Toe Reconstruction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
A comprehensive understanding of the anatomy of the lesser toes and the surrounding structures is critical for successful surgical intervention. The lesser toes consist of three phalanges (proximal, middle, and distal) for the second through fifth toes, while the great toe (hallux) has only two phalanges. The metatarsal heads articulate with the proximal phalanges at the MTP joints, which are crucial for toe movement and stability. The plantar plate, a fibrocartilaginous structure located on the plantar aspect of the MTP joint, plays a vital role in joint stability and preventing hyperextension. 

Key anatomical features include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Bony Structures:** The metatarsal heads, proximal phalanges, and the distal phalanges.
  \item **Soft Tissue Structures:** The extensor digitorum longus and brevis tendons extend the toes, while the flexor digitorum longus and brevis tendons facilitate toe flexion. 
  \item **Neurovascular Supply:** Each toe receives innervation and blood supply from the dorsal and plantar digital nerves and arteries, which run along the sides of the phalanges.
  \item **Surgical Landmarks:** Key landmarks include the palpable joint lines of the MTP, PIP, and DIP joints, and the dorsal aspect of the toe where incisions are typically made.
\end{itemize}

Understanding these anatomical relationships is essential for avoiding complications such as nerve injury or vascular compromise during surgical procedures.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The primary surgical principle for correcting lesser toe deformities is to restore normal biomechanical alignment and balance the forces acting across the affected joints. This is achieved by addressing both soft tissue contractures and bony malalignment. The rationale for surgical intervention stems from the recognition that these deformities often arise from a combination of intrinsic muscle imbalances, extrinsic tendon contractures, and bony malalignment, frequently exacerbated by ill-fitting footwear.

For conditions such as hammertoe, characterized by hyperextension at the MTP joint and flexion at the PIP joint, the surgical goal is to release contracted extensor tendons and the dorsal capsule at the MTP joint, followed by straightening the PIP joint. This can be accomplished through either a PIP joint arthroplasty, which involves resection of the proximal phalanx head, or an arthrodesis, which fuses the PIP joint for a stable correction. 

The technical objectives include achieving a straight, stable, and plantigrade toe that rests flat on the ground, eliminating painful pressure points, and improving the patient's ability to wear conventional footwear comfortably. For claw toe, the approach may involve flexor tenotomy or transfer, while mallet toe is typically corrected with a DIP joint arthroplasty or arthrodesis. Internal fixation, often using Kirschner wires (K-wires) or small implants, is employed to maintain the corrected position during the healing phase.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The surgical correction of toe deformities has evolved significantly over the past century. Early procedures, described in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, were often rudimentary, focusing on simple resections of bony prominences or partial amputations, which provided symptomatic relief but frequently resulted in unstable or flail toes. Pioneers such as Robert Jones in the UK laid the groundwork for hammertoe correction in the early 1900s.

A pivotal advancement occurred in the mid-20th century when surgeons like DuVries popularized proximal interphalangeal joint arthroplasty, which involved resection of the proximal phalanx head. This technique became a cornerstone of hammertoe correction due to its simplicity and effectiveness. The introduction of Kirschner wires for internal fixation further revolutionized these procedures, allowing for more stable and predictable outcomes.

In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, advancements in understanding the biomechanics of the forefoot led to more sophisticated techniques, including tendon balancing, addressing MTP joint pathology, and the development of minimally invasive surgical approaches. The use of absorbable implants and specialized instrumentation continues to refine the precision and outcomes of lesser toe surgery.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Surgery for problems of the small toes is indicated in several clinical scenarios, particularly when conservative management has failed. The following are clear indications for surgical intervention:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Persistent pain in the affected toe or forefoot that significantly interferes with daily activities and ambulation.
  \item Development of painful corns or calluses due to chronic friction and pressure on the toe joints.
  \item Significant difficulty wearing conventional footwear, leading to limitations in lifestyle and mobility.
  \item Progressive deformity that is becoming rigid or causing secondary problems such as MTP joint subluxation.
  \item Recurrent ulceration or skin breakdown over bony prominences, especially in patients with diabetes or neuropathy.
  \item Functional impairment, such as instability during walking or altered gait mechanics.
  \item Cosmetic concerns when the deformity is severe and causes significant psychological distress.
\end{itemize}

These indications highlight the importance of addressing both functional and aesthetic concerns in patients suffering from lesser toe deformities.

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Lesser toe surgery is generally considered a primary definitive treatment for symptomatic lesser toe deformities that have not responded to conservative measures. Conservative management typically includes wider shoes, custom orthotics, padding, activity modification, and anti-inflammatory medications, which are attempted for several months. If these interventions fail to alleviate pain or improve function, surgery becomes the primary option to correct the underlying structural issues.

In certain cases, lesser toe surgery may also serve as a secondary or salvage procedure, particularly when previous surgeries have failed, resulting in recurrence of the deformity or new complications. In such instances, revision surgery is often more complex and requires careful planning. Additionally, it may be performed in conjunction with other forefoot surgeries to achieve overall balance and optimize long-term outcomes.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Lesser toe surgery is typically performed on an outpatient basis under regional anesthesia (e.g., ankle block) or general anesthesia. The patient is positioned supine, and a tourniquet is applied to provide a bloodless surgical field. The specific steps of the procedure may vary based on the type and severity of the deformity but generally follow this structured approach:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Incision and Exposure:} A small dorsal longitudinal or transverse incision is made over the affected joint. For multiple toes, separate incisions are preferred to enhance exposure.
  
  \item \textbf{Soft Tissue Release:} The extensor tendon is often lengthened or released, and the dorsal joint capsule may be incised to reduce hyperextension and decompress the joint.
  
  \item \textbf{Joint Arthroplasty (Resection):} For flexible or semi-rigid hammertoe and mallet toe, the head of the proximal or distal phalanx is resected to create a space that allows the toe to straighten.
  
  \item \textbf{Joint Arthrodesis (Fusion):} For rigid deformities, the articular surfaces of the PIP or DIP joint are resected, and the joint is fused in a corrected position for stability.
  
  \item \textbf{Bone Shortening/Osteotomy:} In cases of MTP joint subluxation, a shortening osteotomy of the metatarsal may be performed to restore proper length relationships.
  
  \item \textbf{Flexor Tendon Transfer:} For claw toe deformities, the flexor digitorum longus tendon may be transferred dorsally to correct MTP joint hyperextension.
  
  \item \textbf{Fixation:} The toe is stabilized with a Kirschner wire or small absorbable pins to maintain the corrected position during healing.
  
  \item \textbf{Closure:} The incision is closed in layers with absorbable sutures for deeper tissues and non-absorbable sutures for the skin. A sterile dressing is applied.
\end{itemize}

This structured approach ensures that the surgical goals of alignment, stability, and function are achieved.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A thorough preoperative workup is essential for optimizing surgical outcomes. Patients undergo a detailed medical history and physical examination, including a neurovascular assessment of the foot. Preoperative imaging, typically weight-bearing X-rays, is performed to assess the specific deformity and any associated pathologies.

Key components of preoperative preparation include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Smoking Cessation:** Patients are advised to stop smoking several weeks prior to surgery to enhance wound healing.
  
  \item **Medication Management:** Blood-thinning medications must be adjusted as directed to minimize bleeding risk.
  
  \item **Diabetes Control:** Patients with diabetes should achieve optimal glycemic control to reduce infection risk.
  
  \item **Anesthesia Evaluation:** A preoperative visit with the anesthesia team assesses overall health and fitness for anesthesia.
  
  \item **NPO Instructions:** Patients are instructed to be NPO for a specified duration before surgery.
  
  \item **Prophylactic Antibiotics:** Administered intravenously shortly before the incision to reduce the risk of infection.
  
  \item **Informed Consent:** Comprehensive consent is obtained, ensuring the patient understands the procedure and its risks.
\end{itemize}

This thorough preparation is vital for minimizing complications and ensuring a successful surgical outcome.

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Active infection in the surgical site or systemic sepsis.
  \item Severe peripheral vascular disease compromising blood supply to the foot.
  \item Uncontrolled diabetes mellitus with severe neuropathy or active Charcot neuroarthropathy.
  \item Non-ambulatory patients with no functional benefit expected from the procedure.
  \item Unrealistic patient expectations regarding surgical outcomes.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications \& Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Poor nutritional status or significant immunosuppression.
  \item Significant obesity (BMI >35-40).
  \item Smoking, which increases the risk of complications.
  \item Mild or asymptomatic toe deformities that do not cause pain or functional limitations.
  \item Certain rheumatologic conditions affecting bone quality and healing.
  \item Previous failed toe surgery requiring a more complex revision approach.
  \item Allergy to materials used for fixation.
\end{itemize}

Awareness of these contraindications is essential for ensuring patient safety and optimizing surgical outcomes.

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Lesser toe surgery carries potential risks, which can be categorized into general surgical risks and procedure-specific complications.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Minor hematoma formation is common; significant hemorrhage is rare.
      \item \textbf{Infection:} Superficial wound infections may occur, requiring antibiotics or further intervention.
      \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications:} Nausea, allergic reactions, or respiratory issues may arise.
      \item \textbf{Blood Clots:} Deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, though rare, can occur.
    \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Stiffness of the Toe or Adjacent Joints:} Arthrofibrosis can limit motion postoperatively.
      \item \textbf{Recurrence of the Deformity:} The toe may revert to its deformed position if underlying issues are not addressed.
      \item \textbf{Malunion or Nonunion:} Improper healing of fused joints may necessitate revision surgery.
      \item \textbf{Floating Toe:} The corrected toe may not contact the ground, leading to new pain.
      \item \textbf{Nerve Injury:} Damage to digital nerves can result in persistent sensory deficits.
      \item \textbf{Vascular Compromise:} Rarely, injury to blood vessels can lead to tissue loss.
      \item \textbf{Hardware Complications:} K-wires or implants may migrate or require removal.
      \item \textbf{Prolonged Swelling:} Swelling can persist for weeks to months postoperatively.
      \item \textbf{Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS):} A rare chronic pain condition may develop.
      \item \textbf{Transfer Metatarsalgia:} Increased pressure under adjacent metatarsals may occur.
      \item \textbf{Unsatisfactory Cosmetic Result:} The toe may not appear perfectly straight post-surgery.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Understanding these risks is crucial for informed consent and patient education.

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Following surgery, patients are monitored in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for vital signs, pain control, and neurovascular checks. The foot is typically elevated to minimize swelling, and ice packs are applied. Most patients are discharged the same day with prescriptions for pain management and instructions for wound care.

In the first 24-48 hours, strict elevation and ice application are critical for managing swelling and pain. A postoperative shoe or boot is worn to protect the surgical site and allow for protected weight-bearing. Stitches are usually removed at a follow-up visit around 10-14 days post-op. If K-wires were used, they are typically removed in the clinic between 3 to 6 weeks.

Patients can expect a gradual return to normal activities, with full recovery often taking 3-6 months. Physical therapy may be recommended to restore range of motion and strength, particularly if stiffness persists. Residual swelling may last for several months, but overall, patients should experience significant improvement in function and comfort.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During surgery, the surgeon documents intraoperative findings, including the type and rigidity of the deformity, the degree of MTP joint hyperextension, and the condition of the plantar plate and extensor tendons. Any resected bone or soft tissue is sent for pathological examination, which typically confirms degenerative changes or chronic inflammation. The pathology report may also rule out unexpected findings, ensuring that the surgical approach is appropriate and effective.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course is characterized by a gradual reduction in pain and swelling, leading to improved function. Patients will initially experience moderate pain, which should decrease over the first few weeks. The surgical incision typically heals cleanly within 2-3 weeks, and once any temporary fixation is removed, the toe should maintain its corrected position.

Patients can expect a gradual return to protected weight-bearing and increasing activity levels. The ultimate goal is the resolution of preoperative symptoms, including the absence of painful corns or calluses, and the ability to wear a wider variety of shoes comfortably, leading to enhanced mobility and improved quality of life.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative care is essential for monitoring healing and addressing potential complications. Follow-up visits typically occur at 10-14 days for wound inspection and suture removal. If K-wires were used, they are usually removed between 3 to 6 weeks post-surgery.

Key components of postoperative care include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wound Checks:} Regular inspections to ensure proper healing and detect any signs of infection.
  
  \item \textbf{Imaging:} Post-operative X-rays may be taken to confirm alignment and assess bone healing.
  
  \item \textbf{Physical Therapy/Rehabilitation:} May be recommended to improve range of motion and strength.
  
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Gradual return to normal activities, typically over several months.
  
  \item \textbf{Footwear Advice:} Guidance on appropriate footwear to prevent recurrence and maintain comfort.
\end{itemize}

Patients are instructed to contact their surgeon if they experience increased pain, redness, swelling, or any changes in sensation, as these may indicate complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lesser toe deformities are prevalent orthopedic conditions, particularly among older adults. Hammertoe is the most common deformity, often affecting women due to the use of narrow, high-heeled shoes. The incidence of these deformities is estimated to be as high as 20-30\% in the adult population, with a significant subset requiring surgical intervention. These conditions are frequently associated with other forefoot pathologies, such as hallux valgus and metatarsalgia, which contribute to altered biomechanics. While mortality related to elective lesser toe surgery is rare, morbidity can include recurrence rates of 10-20\% and infection rates of 1-5\%.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes for surgery for problems of the small toes are generally favorable, with high rates of patient satisfaction. Success rates, defined by significant pain relief and improved function, typically range from 80\% to 90\%. Most patients return to their desired activity levels within 3-6 months, although residual swelling may persist longer. Recurrence of the deformity is the most common long-term complication, occurring in approximately 10-20\% of cases. Prognostic factors influencing outcomes include the severity of the initial deformity, the presence of associated pathologies, and patient compliance with postoperative instructions.
