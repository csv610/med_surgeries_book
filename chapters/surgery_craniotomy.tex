\chapter{Craniotomy}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
A craniotomy is a significant neurosurgical intervention that involves the temporary removal of a portion of the skull, referred to as a bone flap, to provide direct access to the brain and its surrounding structures. This procedure is essential for diagnosing and treating a variety of intracranial conditions, including traumatic brain injuries, vascular malformations, and both benign and malignant tumors. The primary goal of a craniotomy is to safely expose the affected area of the brain, allowing for precise surgical intervention under direct visualization, often enhanced by the use of advanced imaging techniques such as intraoperative MRI or neuronavigation. After the surgical procedure is completed, the bone flap is carefully reattached to restore the protective integrity of the cranial vault. Craniotomy plays a crucial role in alleviating intracranial pressure, excising pathological tissues, repairing vascular anomalies, and draining fluid collections that may compromise neurological function and overall survival.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Open Brain Surgery
  \item Intracranial Surgery
  \item Skull Opening Procedure
  \item Bone Flap Craniotomy
  \item Cranial Trephination (historically)
  \item Specific approaches: Pterional Craniotomy, Suboccipital Craniotomy, Frontotemporal Craniotomy, Orbitozygomatic Craniotomy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
A comprehensive understanding of cranial and intracranial anatomy is vital for the safe execution of a craniotomy. The skull, composed of several fused bones (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, sphenoid, and ethmoid), serves as a rigid protective casing for the brain. Key surgical landmarks include the pterion, a weak point where the frontal, parietal, temporal, and sphenoid bones converge, often targeted for access to the middle cranial fossa. The asterion marks the junction of the parietal, temporal, and occipital bones, while the superior temporal line serves as an important reference for muscle attachment.

Beneath the skull lies the meninges, which consist of three layers: the dura mater (the tough outer layer), the arachnoid mater (the delicate middle layer), and the pia mater (the thin layer closely adhering to the brain). The subarachnoid space, located between the arachnoid and pia mater, contains cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and major cerebral blood vessels.

The brain is divided into three main regions: the cerebrum, cerebellum, and brainstem, each with distinct functions and vascular territories. The cerebrum is further divided into lobes—frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital—housing critical functional areas such as the motor cortex, sensory cortex, and language centers. The arterial supply to the brain is primarily derived from the internal carotid and vertebral arteries, which form the Circle of Willis at the base of the brain. Key vessels encountered during craniotomy include the Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA), Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA), and Posterior Cerebral Artery (PCA). Venous drainage occurs through superficial cortical veins into the superior sagittal sinus and other major sinuses, ultimately draining into the internal jugular veins. Preservation of cranial nerves, which emerge from the brainstem and base of the skull, is critical during surgery, as specific nerves may be at risk depending on the surgical approach and pathology location.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Major arteries: Internal carotid artery, vertebral artery, MCA, ACA, PCA.
  \item Major veins: Superior sagittal sinus, transverse sinuses, sigmoid sinuses.
  \item Cranial nerves: Optic (II), oculomotor (III), trigeminal (V), facial (VII), vestibulocochlear (VIII).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The fundamental principle of craniotomy is to create a controlled, temporary opening in the skull to allow direct surgical access to intracranial contents while minimizing trauma to surrounding healthy brain tissue. This is achieved by carefully elevating a section of the skull bone, known as the bone flap, which is then replaced at the end of the procedure. The rationale behind this approach is to provide a sufficiently wide surgical corridor for the neurosurgeon to safely manipulate delicate brain structures, resect lesions, clip aneurysms, or evacuate hematomas, often under high magnification using a surgical microscope or endoscope.

The technical goals of a craniotomy are multifaceted. Firstly, it aims to achieve adequate exposure of the pathology while protecting eloquent brain regions and critical neurovascular structures. Secondly, meticulous hemostasis is essential throughout the procedure to prevent intraoperative and postoperative hemorrhage. Thirdly, the specific surgical task—whether it be tumor resection, aneurysm clipping, or hematoma evacuation—must be performed with maximal efficacy and minimal collateral damage. Finally, restoring the protective barrier of the skull and achieving a watertight dural closure is crucial to prevent cerebrospinal fluid leakage and infection, thereby facilitating optimal neurological recovery.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The practice of opening the skull has ancient origins, with evidence of trepanation dating back thousands of years across various cultures. Early trepanations were often performed for ritualistic purposes or to relieve headaches, but they frequently resulted in high mortality rates. Hippocrates, in ancient Greece, documented trephination for head injuries, indicating an early understanding of the brain's role in health.

Modern craniotomy began to take shape in the late 19th century with the advent of antiseptic surgical techniques pioneered by Joseph Lister, which drastically reduced infection rates. Key figures in the development of neurosurgery include William Macewen, a Scottish surgeon who successfully drained brain abscesses and removed tumors in the 1880s, demonstrating the feasibility of intracranial surgery. Harvey Cushing, often regarded as the father of modern neurosurgery, revolutionized the field in the early 20th century by emphasizing meticulous hemostasis, developing specialized instruments, and establishing the first neurosurgical residency program. His contributions significantly reduced the mortality and morbidity associated with brain surgery, transforming it into a viable therapeutic option. Subsequent advancements in imaging techniques, microsurgical methods, neuroanesthesia, intraoperative monitoring, and neuronavigation have further refined craniotomy, making it safer and more precise for a wider range of complex intracranial pathologies.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Craniotomy is indicated for a variety of critical neurological conditions that necessitate direct surgical intervention. Specific indications include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Resection of primary or metastatic brain tumors, including gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary adenomas, to achieve gross total resection or debulking.
  \item Clipping of cerebral aneurysms, both ruptured and unruptured, to prevent re-bleeding and manage subarachnoid hemorrhage.
  \item Excision of arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) and cavernous malformations to prevent hemorrhage or control medically refractory seizures.
  \item Evacuation of traumatic intracranial hematomas, such as epidural or subdural hematomas, to relieve mass effect and brain compression.
  \item Drainage and excision of brain abscesses or other intracranial infections that are refractory to antibiotic therapy.
  \item Decompression of cranial nerves in cases of trigeminal neuralgia or hemifacial spasm through microvascular decompression.
  \item Resection of epileptogenic foci in patients with medically refractory epilepsy, such as temporal lobectomy.
  \item Repair of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks that are resistant to conservative management.
  \item Biopsy of deep-seated brain lesions that cannot be diagnosed through less invasive means.
  \item Evacuation of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, particularly large clots causing significant neurological deficits.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Craniotomy serves as a primary, definitive operative procedure for many acute and chronic intracranial diseases. In life-threatening conditions such as ruptured cerebral aneurysms or large traumatic hematomas, craniotomy is often the first-line treatment to preserve life and neurological function. The immediate access to the pathology provided by craniotomy is crucial, as delays can lead to irreversible brain damage or death.

Conversely, craniotomy can also be a secondary or salvage procedure when less invasive treatments, such as endovascular coiling or stereotactic radiosurgery, have failed or are not feasible. For instance, a craniotomy may be performed to clip an aneurysm that could not be successfully coiled or to resect a tumor that recurred after radiation therapy. The decision to perform a craniotomy, and its role as primary or secondary, depends on the specific pathology, its location, the patient's clinical status, and the overall risk-benefit profile.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Craniotomy is performed under general anesthesia, ensuring the patient is completely unconscious and pain-free. Patient positioning is critical and often involves the use of a skull clamp to secure the head in a specific orientation that optimizes surgical access. The exact position (supine, lateral, or prone) depends on the location of the lesion and the chosen surgical approach. The scalp is prepared by clipping or shaving hair and thoroughly cleansing with antiseptic agents.

The surgical steps typically include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scalp Incision and Flap Creation:} A curvilinear or horseshoe-shaped incision is made in the scalp, designed to maximize exposure while preserving blood supply. The scalp and underlying muscle layers are dissected and reflected to expose the skull.
  
  \item \textbf{Burr Hole Placement:} Multiple small burr holes are drilled into the skull using a high-speed drill, defining the perimeter of the bone flap, often guided by neuronavigation systems.
  
  \item \textbf{Bone Flap Creation:} A craniotome is used to connect the burr holes, creating a precise cut through the skull bone. The bone flap is then carefully elevated or completely removed and placed in a sterile basin.
  
  \item \textbf{Dural Opening:} The dura mater is meticulously incised and reflected, requiring care to avoid injury to the underlying brain or bridging veins.
  
  \item \textbf{Intracranial Work:} Under microscopic guidance, the neurosurgeon performs the specific procedure, such as tumor resection or aneurysm clipping. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring may be utilized to protect eloquent brain regions.
  
  \item \textbf{Dural Closure:} After completing the intracranial work, meticulous hemostasis is achieved, and the dura mater is closed in a watertight fashion to prevent CSF leakage.
  
  \item \textbf{Bone Flap Replacement and Fixation:} The bone flap is repositioned and secured using titanium plates and screws to ensure stability and proper alignment.
  
  \item \textbf{Scalp Closure:} The muscle and scalp layers are closed in layers with absorbable and non-absorbable sutures. A subgaleal drain may be placed to prevent fluid accumulation, and a sterile dressing is applied.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Thorough preoperative preparation is essential for optimizing patient safety and surgical outcomes in craniotomy. Key preparatory steps include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Detailed Neurological Examination:} Establishing a baseline of neurological function and identifying any existing deficits.
  
  \item \textbf{Laboratory Tests:} Complete blood count (CBC), electrolyte panel, renal and hepatic function tests, coagulation studies, and blood type and cross-match.
  
  \item \textbf{Advanced Imaging:} High-resolution MRI and CT scans to localize the lesion and characterize its nature. Functional MRI may be used to map critical brain areas.
  
  \item \textbf{Medical Clearance:} Comprehensive cardiac and pulmonary evaluations to ensure the patient is fit for surgery and anesthesia.
  
  \item \textbf{Medication Management:} Discontinuation of anticoagulants and initiation of steroids or anticonvulsants as needed.
  
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients are instructed to be NPO for a specified period before surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} A detailed discussion regarding the procedure, risks, benefits, and expected recovery is conducted.
  
  \item \textbf{Preoperative Antibiotics:} Intravenous antibiotics are administered shortly before the incision to minimize the risk of infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Severe, Uncontrolled Coagulopathy:} Significant bleeding disorders pose an unacceptable risk of intracranial hemorrhage.
  
  \item \textbf{Active Systemic Infection or Sepsis:} Elective craniotomy in the presence of widespread infection increases the risk of complications.
  
  \item \textbf{Irreversible Brain Damage or Futility:} Cases of severe brain injury with no expectation of recovery may be deemed futile for surgical intervention.
  
  \item \textbf{Severe, Uncorrectable Medical Comorbidities:} Patients with end-stage organ failure may not tolerate the physiological stress of surgery.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications and Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Extreme Advanced Age with Multiple Comorbidities:} Very elderly patients with significant medical burdens may have increased surgical risks.
  
  \item \textbf{Prior Radiation to the Surgical Field:} Previous radiation therapy can complicate subsequent surgery due to altered tissue planes.
  
  \item \textbf{Severe Cerebral Edema with Impending Herniation:} In some cases, a decompressive craniectomy may be a safer initial approach.
  
  \item \textbf{Poor Neurological Status with Borderline Prognosis:} The potential benefits of surgery must be weighed against the risks in patients with poor preoperative function.
  
  \item \textbf{Patient Refusal:} A competent patient's refusal of surgery is a contraindication.
  
  \item \textbf{Uncontrolled Hypertension or Diabetes:} These conditions must be optimized preoperatively to minimize complications.
  
  \item \textbf{Active Substance Abuse:} May complicate anesthesia and postoperative recovery.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Craniotomy carries inherent risks, both general and specific to the procedure.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage can lead to hematoma formation requiring urgent reoperation.
      \item \textbf{Infection:} Surgical site infections, meningitis, or brain abscess can occur and may be life-threatening.
      \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications:} Allergic reactions, cardiac events, respiratory depression, and pulmonary embolism are potential risks.
    \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Neurological Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Neurologic Deficit:} New or worsened neurological deficits may occur depending on the area involved.
      \item \textbf{Seizures:} Postoperative seizures may require long-term anticonvulsant therapy.
      \item \textbf{Cerebral Edema:} Brain swelling can increase intracranial pressure and may require further intervention.
      \item \textbf{CSF Leak:} Leakage can increase the risk of meningitis and may necessitate surgical repair.
      \item \textbf{Stroke:} Ischemic or hemorrhagic strokes can occur during or after surgery.
      \item \textbf{Hydrocephalus:} Accumulation of CSF may require shunt placement.
      \item \textbf{Cranial Nerve Injury:} Damage to cranial nerves can lead to various deficits.
      \item \textbf{Venous Air Embolism:} Air entering the venous system can be life-threatening.
      \item \textbf{Bone Flap Complications:} Infection or malposition of the bone flap may require revision surgery.
      \item \textbf{Vascular Injury:} Damage to major vessels can lead to significant complications.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Following a craniotomy, the patient is transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for initial recovery. Vital signs are closely monitored, and neurological checks are performed to detect any immediate complications such as hemorrhage or worsening edema. Pain management is initiated with intravenous analgesics, and antiemetics are administered to prevent nausea.

Once stable, the patient is typically moved to a neurosurgical intensive care unit (NICU) for continued monitoring. In the NICU, attention is given to intracranial pressure management, blood pressure control, and fluid balance. Early mobilization is encouraged, often starting with sitting up and ambulation within 24-48 hours. The hospital stay usually ranges from 5 to 10 days, depending on the complexity of the surgery and the patient's recovery trajectory. Long-term recovery may involve extensive rehabilitation, taking several weeks to months for full functional improvement.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During a craniotomy, the surgeon documents intraoperative findings, including the appearance of brain tissue, the nature and extent of lesions, and their relationship to neurovascular structures. Tissue removed is sent for pathological examination, which provides definitive diagnoses, including histological type and grade of tumors, and assesses resection margins. These findings are crucial for guiding further treatment decisions and determining prognosis.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course following craniotomy is characterized by resolution of the underlying pathology and stabilization or improvement of neurological status. Patients typically experience initial pain and discomfort, which is managed effectively. Neurological function should either remain stable or show progressive improvement. The surgical wound should heal cleanly, and patients are encouraged to gradually increase activity levels, with the ultimate goal of returning to pre-illness functional status.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Post-craniotomy follow-up is essential for monitoring recovery and managing long-term care.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Neurosurgical Clinic Visits:} Regular appointments are scheduled to monitor neurological recovery and assess wound healing.
  
  \item \textbf{Suture/Staple Removal:} Scalp sutures or staples are typically removed 7-14 days post-surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Serial Neuroimaging:} Follow-up imaging is performed at scheduled intervals to monitor for recurrence or other changes.
  
  \item \textbf{Neurological Rehabilitation:} Therapy may be continued for weeks to months to address any deficits.
  
  \item \textbf{Medication Management:} Anticonvulsants may be continued as needed, and steroids are tapered over time.
  
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Gradual resumption of normal activities is advised, with specific restrictions as necessary.
  
  \item \textbf{Oncology Follow-up:} For patients with malignant tumors, follow-up with a neuro-oncologist is crucial for ongoing management.
\end{itemize}

Patients are educated on warning signs such as worsening headache, fever, new neurological deficits, or wound changes, and instructed to seek immediate medical attention if these occur.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of craniotomy is closely linked to the incidence of the underlying neurological conditions it addresses. Approximately 86,000 new cases of primary brain tumors are diagnosed annually in the United States, with metastatic brain tumors occurring in 20-40\% of cancer patients. Cerebral aneurysms are found in about 3-5\% of the general population, with roughly 30,000 ruptures occurring each year. Traumatic brain injury is a leading cause of craniotomy, with millions of emergency department visits annually. The age and sex distribution for craniotomy vary widely depending on the specific pathology, with gliomas affecting older adults and AVMs often diagnosed in younger individuals. Mortality and morbidity rates can vary significantly, ranging from low for elective procedures on benign lesions to higher rates for emergency interventions.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Outcomes following craniotomy are influenced by the underlying pathology, the patient's preoperative neurological status, and the occurrence of complications. For acute conditions like traumatic hematomas, timely craniotomy can be life-saving. In brain tumor surgery, the extent of resection is a critical prognostic factor, with gross total resection often leading to improved survival. Studies have shown the benefit of adjuvant therapies following resection of malignant tumors. Functional outcomes can vary widely, with some patients experiencing full recovery while others may have persistent deficits. Overall, advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative care have improved the safety and efficacy of craniotomy, leading to better survival rates and functional outcomes.
