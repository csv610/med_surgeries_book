\chapter{Myringotomy and Tube Insertion}

\section*{Introduction \& Clinical Indications}
Myringotomy with tympanostomy tube insertion is a common surgical procedure that involves creating a small incision in the tympanic membrane (eardrum) to allow for the drainage of fluid from the middle ear and the placement of a ventilation tube. This tube, often referred to as a grommet or pressure equalization (PE) tube, serves to maintain the incision's patency, facilitating ventilation of the middle ear space and equalizing pressure across the tympanic membrane. The procedure is particularly significant in the pediatric population, where it is primarily indicated for recurrent acute otitis media (AOM) and chronic otitis media with effusion (OME). These conditions can lead to hearing impairment, speech and language delays, and other developmental issues. By restoring normal middle ear function, myringotomy and tube insertion play a crucial role in improving quality of life and developmental outcomes for affected children.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Ear tube surgery
  \item Tympanostomy tube placement
  \item Grommet insertion
  \item Ventilation tube insertion
  \item Pressure equalization (PE) tube insertion
  \item Myringotomy with PE tube
  \item Bilateral myringotomy and tubes (BMT)
\end{itemize}

The primary surgical principle of myringotomy and tympanostomy tube insertion is to create an artificial opening in the tympanic membrane to facilitate ventilation of the middle ear and drainage of fluid. The Eustachian tube's dysfunction often leads to negative pressure in the middle ear, resulting in fluid accumulation and potential infection. By performing a myringotomy, the surgeon can immediately relieve pressure and aspirate any fluid present in the middle ear.

The insertion of a tympanostomy tube maintains this opening, allowing for continuous ventilation and preventing fluid re-accumulation. This intervention not only alleviates symptoms but also promotes the healing of the middle ear mucosa, reducing the frequency of acute infections. The tube acts as a temporary substitute for the Eustachian tube, allowing for natural function to recover over time. The technical goals of the procedure include ensuring proper placement of the tube, minimizing trauma to surrounding structures, and achieving optimal drainage and ventilation of the middle ear.

The practice of myringotomy can be traced back to the early 19th century, with Sir Astley Cooper being one of the first to document the procedure in 1801. Initially, myringotomy was performed to relieve pressure and drain fluid, but it lacked a mechanism to keep the incision open, leading to recurrent symptoms. Over the years, various attempts were made to maintain the patency of the incision, including the use of small metal or rubber grommets, but these methods were not widely adopted.

A significant advancement occurred in 1954 when Dr. Beverly Armstrong introduced the concept of inserting a small plastic tube into the myringotomy incision. This innovation, known as the "collar button" tube, revolutionized the management of chronic middle ear effusions and recurrent otitis media. Since then, numerous tube designs and materials have been developed, including silicone and fluoroplastic options, each aiming to optimize retention time and minimize complications. Today, myringotomy with tympanostomy tube insertion is one of the most frequently performed surgical procedures in pediatric otolaryngology, reflecting its enduring efficacy and safety.

Myringotomy and tympanostomy tube insertion are indicated for several clinical scenarios, particularly when conservative management has failed or is inappropriate. The following are key indications for the procedure:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Recurrent acute otitis media (AOM), defined as three or more episodes within six months or four episodes within 12 months, with at least one episode occurring in the last six months.
  \item Chronic otitis media with effusion (OME) lasting three months or longer, especially with documented bilateral hearing loss of 20 dB or greater.
  \item OME associated with significant speech or language delays, balance disorders, or learning difficulties attributable to hearing impairment.
  \item Severe tympanic membrane retraction or adhesive otitis media, which can predispose to cholesteatoma formation.
  \item Eustachian tube dysfunction causing persistent discomfort or recurrent barotrauma, particularly in adults.
  \item Prevention of recurrent otitis media in children with craniofacial anomalies or other conditions that increase the risk of chronic middle ear disease.
  \item Complications of otitis media, such as impending mastoiditis or facial nerve paralysis, requiring urgent intervention.
  \item Diagnostic or therapeutic measures for specific middle ear conditions, such as perilymphatic fistula or Meniere's disease.
\end{itemize}

Myringotomy and tympanostomy tube insertion is primarily considered a first-line surgical intervention for recurrent AOM and chronic OME after a period of watchful waiting or ineffective medical therapy. In cases of recurrent AOM, the procedure is typically performed once the patient meets established frequency criteria, often following multiple unsuccessful antibiotic treatments. For OME, it is indicated when the effusion persists for an extended duration and is associated with significant hearing loss or developmental concerns.

While antibiotics are the primary treatment for acute infections, tube insertion serves as a proactive measure to prevent future episodes and resolve chronic effusions. In certain cases, such as severe tympanic membrane retraction or specific craniofacial syndromes, tube placement may be considered a primary intervention to avert serious complications. It is rarely a salvage procedure but rather a strategic approach to mitigate the long-term effects of chronic middle ear disease.

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
A comprehensive understanding of the anatomy of the ear is essential for the successful execution of myringotomy and tube insertion. The tympanic membrane (TM) separates the external auditory canal (EAC) from the middle ear and consists of three layers: an outer epidermal layer, a middle fibrous layer, and an inner mucosal layer. Key anatomical landmarks on the TM include the umbo, where the malleus attaches, and the cone of light, which is typically observed in a healthy TM. The TM is divided into quadrants, with the anterior-inferior quadrant being the preferred site for incision due to its relative avascularity and distance from critical structures.

The middle ear cavity houses the ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes), which are vital for sound transmission. The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the nasopharynx, playing a crucial role in pressure regulation and drainage. Important nearby structures include the facial nerve, which runs posteriorly, and the jugular bulb, located inferiorly. The arterial supply to the tympanic membrane and middle ear is primarily from branches of the maxillary artery and the posterior auricular artery, while venous drainage follows similar pathways. Lymphatic drainage typically follows the external jugular chain.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Tympanic membrane (TM)
  \item Ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes)
  \item Eustachian tube
  \item Facial nerve
  \item Jugular bulb
  \item Arterial supply (maxillary artery, posterior auricular artery)
  \item Venous drainage (similar to arterial supply)
  \item Lymphatic drainage (external jugular chain)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Preoperative preparation for myringotomy and tube insertion is essential to ensure patient safety and optimize surgical outcomes. Key steps in the preoperative workup include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Medical History and Physical Examination:} A thorough review of the patient's medical history, including medications and allergies, is conducted, along with a focused physical examination to confirm surgical indications.
  
  \item \textbf{Audiometry and Tympanometry:} Preoperative audiometry is performed to document hearing loss, and tympanometry is used to confirm the presence of middle ear effusion.
  
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Clearance:} A preoperative assessment by an anesthesiologist is mandatory for patients undergoing general anesthesia, including necessary blood tests and cardiac evaluations.
  
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients are instructed to be NPO for a specified period prior to anesthesia to reduce the risk of aspiration.
  
  \item \textbf{Medication Adjustments:} Instructions regarding regular medications, particularly anticoagulants, are provided to minimize bleeding risk.
  
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} A detailed discussion regarding the procedure, benefits, risks, and expected postoperative course is conducted, and written informed consent is obtained.
  
  \item \textbf{Prophylactic Antibiotics:} Some surgeons may prescribe a short course of antibiotics before or after the procedure, especially if there is evidence of active infection.
\end{itemize}

While myringotomy and tube insertion is a common procedure, certain contraindications and precautions must be considered.

\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Active cholesteatoma, which requires a more extensive surgical approach.
  \item Existing tympanic membrane perforation, as the middle ear is already ventilated.
  \item Severe, uncorrectable bleeding diathesis that poses an unacceptable surgical risk.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications \& Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Coagulopathy or anticoagulant use requires careful evaluation and management.
  \item Immunocompromised patients may need enhanced prophylactic measures.
  \item Anatomical abnormalities of the ear may complicate the procedure.
  \item Uncontrolled systemic illnesses should be optimized before surgery.
  \item Patient or parent non-compliance with postoperative care can increase complication risks.
  \item Water precautions should be advised to prevent complications related to tube placement.
  \item Flying and diving should be discussed with patients to avoid potential issues.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Myringotomy with tympanostomy tube insertion is typically performed as an outpatient procedure, lasting approximately 10 to 15 minutes per ear. General anesthesia is commonly used in pediatric patients, while cooperative adults may undergo the procedure under local anesthesia.

The general steps of the procedure are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia and Patient Positioning:} General anesthesia is induced for children, and the patient is positioned supine with the head turned to expose the operative ear. An operating microscope is utilized for enhanced visualization.
  
  \item \textbf{Ear Canal Preparation and Visualization:} The external ear canal is cleaned with an antiseptic solution, and any cerumen or debris is removed to ensure a clear view of the tympanic membrane.
  
  \item \textbf{Myringotomy Incision:} A small, radial incision (2-3 mm) is made in the anterior-inferior quadrant of the tympanic membrane using a specialized myringotomy knife, minimizing the risk of injury to surrounding structures.
  
  \item \textbf{Fluid Aspiration:} Any middle ear fluid is aspirated using a small suction catheter, and its characteristics are noted. A sample may be collected for culture if infection is suspected.
  
  \item \textbf{Tube Insertion:} A tympanostomy tube of appropriate size is inserted through the myringotomy incision, ensuring that the flanges rest on either side of the tympanic membrane for secure placement.
  
  \item \textbf{Post-Insertion Check and Antibiotic Instillation:} The tube's position and patency are confirmed, and topical antibiotic ear drops may be instilled to prevent infection.
  
  \item \textbf{Closure:} No sutures are required for the myringotomy incision, as it is designed to self-seal around the tube. The patient is then awakened from anesthesia and monitored in the recovery area.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Myringotomy with tube insertion is generally safe, but potential complications can arise, categorized into general surgical risks and procedure-specific risks.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Adverse reactions to anesthetic agents, including nausea and respiratory complications.
      \item Cardiovascular events, though rare in healthy pediatric patients.
    \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Bleeding, usually minimal but occasionally requiring intervention.
      \item Infection, including postoperative otitis externa or otitis media.
      \item Pain, typically mild and manageable with over-the-counter analgesics.
    \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Otorrhea, occurring in 10-20\% of patients, often due to infection.
      \item Premature extrusion of the tympanostomy tube, leading to recurrence of symptoms.
      \item Tube obstruction by blood clots or cerumen, affecting ventilation.
      \item Persistent perforation of the tympanic membrane, occurring in 1-3\% of cases.
      \item Tympanosclerosis, which may affect hearing if extensive.
      \item Granulation tissue formation around the tube insertion site.
      \item Rarely, cholesteatoma formation requiring more extensive surgery.
      \item Hearing changes, which are typically temporary but can occur.
      \item Retained tubes that may require surgical removal.
      \item Displacement of ossicles, although extremely rare.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Recovery}
Following myringotomy and tube insertion, patients are monitored in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for recovery from anesthesia. Initial symptoms may include drowsiness or mild irritability, but pain is generally minimal and manageable with analgesics. Most patients are discharged within a few hours.

During the first 1-2 weeks postoperatively, patients are advised to avoid strenuous activities and water exposure to the ear. Clear or blood-tinged drainage from the ear canal is common and typically resolves within 48 hours. Hearing improvement is often immediate, as fluid is removed and the middle ear is ventilated. Regular follow-up appointments are essential to monitor tube placement and assess hearing.

The tympanostomy tubes usually remain in place for 6 to 18 months, depending on the type used, before spontaneously extruding. The myringotomy site typically heals completely after tube extrusion, with minimal or no residual scarring.

During the procedure, the surgeon documents several key findings, including the appearance of the tympanic membrane, the presence and characteristics of middle ear fluid, and the specifics of the myringotomy incision. The type and size of the tympanostomy tube inserted are also recorded. While routine histopathological examination is not typically performed, any aspirated fluid may be sent for microbiological culture to identify pathogens and guide treatment.

A successful postoperative course following myringotomy and tube insertion is characterized by rapid symptom resolution. Patients typically experience immediate hearing improvement, with a significant reduction in ear pain and pressure. The frequency of acute ear infections should decrease substantially, leading to fewer antibiotic prescriptions. Mild drainage may occur for a short period, but persistent or purulent discharge is not expected and should prompt medical evaluation. The tympanostomy tubes are expected to remain patent, allowing for continuous ventilation until they extrude naturally, at which point the tympanic membrane should heal without complications.

Comprehensive postoperative care is crucial for ensuring the success of myringotomy and tube insertion. Key components of postoperative care include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Postoperative Visits:} Initial follow-up is typically scheduled 2-4 weeks after surgery to confirm tube placement and patency, with subsequent visits every 3-6 months.
  
  \item \textbf{Otoscopic Examination:} Regular otoscopic examinations are performed to verify tube position and assess the middle ear for fluid.
  
  \item \textbf{Audiometry and Tympanometry:} Repeat audiometry may be conducted to monitor hearing improvement, especially if there were concerns preoperatively.
  
  \item \textbf{Management of Otorrhea:} If drainage occurs, the physician will assess for infection and may prescribe topical or oral antibiotics as needed.
  
  \item \textbf{Tube Extrusion Monitoring:} The natural extrusion of tubes is monitored, with surgical removal considered if a tube remains in place for an extended period.
  
  \item \textbf{Repeat Tube Placement:} If symptoms recur after tube extrusion, a second set of tubes may be indicated.
  
  \item \textbf{Adenoidectomy Consideration:} In cases of recurrent AOM, adenoidectomy may be considered to address contributing factors.
  
  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients should be advised to contact their physician if they experience persistent drainage, significant pain, fever, or sudden hearing changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Myringotomy with tympanostomy tube insertion is the most common ambulatory surgical procedure performed on children in the United States, with an estimated 600,000 to 700,000 procedures annually. The peak incidence occurs in children aged 1 to 3 years, reflecting the higher prevalence of otitis media in this age group. Boys and girls are affected relatively equally. The primary indications for the procedure are recurrent AOM and chronic OME, with OME accounting for a significant proportion of cases. The procedure has a low mortality rate, but morbidity is associated with complications such as otorrhea, premature tube extrusion, and persistent tympanic membrane perforation. The public health impact is substantial, given the high incidence of otitis media and its effects on childhood development and healthcare utilization.

The outcomes of myringotomy and tympanostomy tube insertion are generally excellent, with over 90\% success in resolving middle ear effusions and improving hearing. The procedure significantly reduces the frequency of recurrent AOM, often by 50-70\% during the period the tubes are in place. Most children experience resolution of their middle ear issues as they grow older, with only 20-30\% requiring a second set of tubes. Long-term studies indicate that while tympanosclerosis is common after tube extrusion, it rarely leads to significant hearing impairment. Persistent tympanic membrane perforation requiring repair occurs in a small minority of patients. Overall, the procedure positively impacts speech and language development, academic performance, and quality of life, while reducing the need for repeated antibiotic courses. Prognostic factors for recurrence include younger age at initial surgery and the presence of craniofacial anomalies.
