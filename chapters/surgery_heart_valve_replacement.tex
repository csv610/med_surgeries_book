\chapter{Heart Valve Replacement}

\section*{Introduction \& Clinical Indications}
Heart valve replacement is a critical cardiac surgical procedure that involves the removal of a severely diseased or dysfunctional heart valve and its replacement with a prosthetic valve. This surgery is primarily indicated for patients suffering from significant valvular stenosis, characterized by the narrowing of the valve opening, or regurgitation, where the valve fails to close properly, leading to backward blood flow. The most commonly affected valves are the aortic and mitral valves, although the tricuspid and pulmonary valves may also require replacement in specific clinical scenarios. The primary objective of this surgery is to restore normal hemodynamics within the heart, alleviate symptoms of heart failure, and improve overall cardiac function, thereby enhancing the patient's quality of life and survival rates. The choice between mechanical and bioprosthetic valves is influenced by various factors, including the patient's age, lifestyle, and the need for long-term anticoagulation therapy.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Surgical Valve Replacement
  \item Aortic Valve Replacement (AVR)
  \item Mitral Valve Replacement (MVR)
  \item Tricuspid Valve Replacement (TVR)
  \item Pulmonary Valve Replacement (PVR)
  \item Open Heart Valve Surgery
  \item Valve Replacement Surgery
\end{itemize}

The primary principle of heart valve replacement is to correct severe valvular dysfunction that significantly impairs cardiac output and leads to debilitating symptoms of heart failure or irreversible myocardial damage. Stenotic valves obstruct forward blood flow, forcing the heart to exert greater pressure to eject blood, which can lead to myocardial hypertrophy and eventual heart failure. Conversely, regurgitant valves allow backward flow of blood, increasing the workload on the heart and reducing effective forward flow.

The surgical intervention involves the meticulous excision of the diseased valve leaflets and annulus, often calcified or fibrotic, followed by the implantation of a prosthetic valve. This prosthetic valve, whether mechanical or bioprosthetic, is designed to ensure competent, unidirectional blood flow, normalizing intracardiac pressures and reducing cardiac workload. Key technical goals include achieving a secure, leak-free anastomosis between the prosthesis and the native annulus, optimizing valve size to prevent patient-prosthesis mismatch, and preserving surrounding cardiac structures, such as the mitral subvalvular apparatus, to maintain ventricular geometry and function.

The evolution of heart valve replacement is a remarkable journey in cardiac surgery, beginning in the mid-20th century. A significant milestone was the development of cardiopulmonary bypass (the heart-lung machine) in the early 1950s by Dr. John Gibbon, which allowed surgeons to operate on a still, bloodless heart. In 1960, Dr. Albert Starr and Lowell Edwards achieved a breakthrough by successfully implanting the first clinically viable caged-ball valve prosthesis in a human patient, revolutionizing the treatment of valvular heart disease.

The 1970s saw the introduction of bioprosthetic valves derived from porcine or bovine tissues, providing alternatives for patients who could not tolerate lifelong anticoagulation. The 21st century brought minimally invasive surgical approaches, such as ministernotomy and thoracotomy, aimed at reducing surgical trauma. A landmark moment occurred in 2002 with the first human transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) performed by Dr. Alain Cribier, which transformed the treatment of high-risk patients with severe aortic stenosis, allowing valve replacement without open-heart surgery.

Heart valve replacement is indicated for patients with severe valvular heart disease who are symptomatic or show objective evidence of progressive cardiac dysfunction. Specific clinical indications include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, characterized by angina, syncope, or dyspnea on exertion.
  \item Severe asymptomatic aortic stenosis with left ventricular dysfunction (ejection fraction <50\%).
  \item Severe symptomatic aortic regurgitation with left ventricular dilation or dysfunction.
  \item Severe symptomatic mitral stenosis presenting with dyspnea or pulmonary hypertension.
  \item Severe symptomatic primary mitral regurgitation with left ventricular dysfunction.
  \item Severe secondary mitral regurgitation refractory to medical therapy.
  \item Infective endocarditis causing severe valvular destruction or persistent infection.
  \item Valvular dysfunction from congenital heart disease or traumatic injury.
\end{itemize}

Heart valve replacement is typically considered a primary and definitive treatment for severe symptomatic valvular heart disease. It directly addresses the underlying structural pathology of the valve, aiming to restore normal cardiac function and prevent irreversible myocardial damage. In cases of severe aortic stenosis or regurgitation, surgical valve replacement is often the first-line intervention when medical management is insufficient.

In complex scenarios, valve replacement may serve as a secondary or adjunct procedure. For example, in severe secondary mitral regurgitation, valve replacement may be considered after optimal medical therapy has failed. Additionally, valve replacement may be performed alongside other cardiac surgeries, such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), when significant valvular disease coexists with coronary artery disease.

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
A thorough understanding of the cardiac anatomy is essential for successful heart valve replacement. The heart consists of four chambers: two atria and two ventricles, separated by a septum. Blood flow is regulated by four valves: the tricuspid and mitral (atrioventricular) valves, and the pulmonary and aortic (semilunar) valves. Each valve comprises leaflets or cusps anchored to a fibrous annulus, which provides structural support. 

The mitral valve, located between the left atrium and left ventricle, typically has two leaflets (anterior and posterior) connected to the papillary muscles via chordae tendineae. The aortic valve, situated between the left ventricle and the aorta, usually has three cusps (right, left, and non-coronary) that open into the ascending aorta, just distal to which lie the coronary arteries.

Key anatomical landmarks include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item **McBurney's Point:** Not directly related to heart surgery but important for understanding anatomical references.
  \item **Calot's Triangle:** Important in biliary surgery but not applicable here; however, understanding anatomical triangles can aid in spatial orientation.
  \item **Atrioventricular Node:** Located near the tricuspid valve, this structure is critical for maintaining cardiac rhythm and is at risk during valve surgery.
\end{itemize}

The proximity of the cardiac conduction system, particularly the atrioventricular (AV) node and the Bundle of His, is crucial during valve surgery, as injury to these structures can lead to heart block. Additionally, the great vessels, including the ascending aorta and pulmonary artery, are vital for cannulation during cardiopulmonary bypass. The pericardium, which encloses the heart, is incised to access the heart and is typically closed at the end of the procedure. Understanding these anatomical relationships, along with the arterial supply (coronary arteries), venous drainage (cardiac veins), and lymphatic pathways, is fundamental for minimizing complications and achieving optimal surgical outcomes.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A thorough preoperative evaluation is essential for optimizing patient outcomes. This process typically involves:

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Medical History and Physical Examination:** Assess overall health and identify comorbidities.
  
  \item **Laboratory Tests:** Including complete blood count, coagulation profile, renal and liver function tests, and blood type.
  
  \item **Cardiac Imaging:** Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography to confirm valvular pathology and assess ventricular function.
  
  \item **Dental Clearance:** To identify and treat any active dental infections.
  
  \item **Pulmonary Function Tests:** For patients with respiratory disease history.
  
  \item **Anesthesia and Cardiac Clearance:** Evaluation by anesthesiologist and cardiologist to assess surgical risk.
  
  \item **Medication Adjustments:** Discontinuation of anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents prior to surgery.
  
  \item **NPO Status:** Patients are instructed to be nil per os (NPO) for at least 6-8 hours before surgery.
  
  \item **Preoperative Antibiotics:** Administered intravenously within one hour prior to incision.
  
  \item **Informed Consent:** Detailed discussion regarding the procedure, risks, and expected recovery.
\end{itemize}

**Absolute Contraindications:**

\begin{itemize}
  \item Irreversible neurological damage or severe cognitive impairment.
  \item Severe uncorrectable comorbidities with high operative mortality.
  \item Active systemic infection or sepsis.
  \item Inability to tolerate cardiopulmonary bypass.
  \item Severe porcelain aorta.
\end{itemize}

**Relative Contraindications:**

\begin{itemize}
  \item Advanced age and frailty.
  \item Significant renal insufficiency.
  \item Severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
  \item Previous cardiac surgery.
  \item Coagulopathy or bleeding diathesis.
  \item Patient refusal or lack of understanding of the procedure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Heart valve replacement is a complex open-heart procedure requiring meticulous surgical technique. The procedure typically begins with general anesthesia, followed by patient positioning, usually supine, with arms tucked for access. A median sternotomy incision is made to provide direct access to the heart.

The patient is placed on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), which involves cannulation of the aorta and vena cavae to divert blood for oxygenation. Myocardial protection is achieved by administering a cold cardioplegic solution into the coronary arteries to minimize ischemic damage.

Key operative steps include:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item **Initiation of Cardiopulmonary Bypass:** After heparinization, the patient is cannulated, and CPB is initiated. The ascending aorta is cross-clamped, and cardioplegia is delivered.
  
  \item **Valve Exposure:** For aortic valve replacement, an aortotomy is made above the aortic root. For mitral valve replacement, a left atriotomy is performed.
  
  \item **Excision of Diseased Valve:** The native valve leaflets are excised, and any calcification or fibrotic tissue is debrided from the annulus.
  
  \item **Valve Sizing and Prosthesis Selection:** The annulus is measured to determine the optimal prosthetic valve size, considering patient factors.
  
  \item **Prosthesis Implantation:** The prosthetic valve is sutured into the annulus using non-absorbable sutures, ensuring a secure, leak-free seal.
  
  \item **De-airing and Bypass Weaning:** The heart chambers are de-aired, and the patient is gradually weaned off CPB with inotropic support.
  
  \item **Closure:** The aortotomy or atriotomy is closed, and the sternum is reapproximated. Chest tubes are placed for drainage, and pacing wires may be inserted.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Heart valve replacement carries inherent risks, categorized into general surgical complications and procedure-specific risks.

\begin{itemize}
  \item **General Surgical Risks:**
  \begin{itemize}
    \item **Bleeding:** Intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage.
    \item **Infection:** Surgical site infection, pneumonia, or systemic sepsis.
    \item **Anesthesia Complications:** Adverse reactions or prolonged intubation.
    \item **Pain:** Postoperative pain management challenges.
  \end{itemize}
  
  \item **Procedure-Specific Risks:**
  \begin{itemize}
    \item **Stroke:** Due to embolization during surgery.
    \item **Heart Block:** Damage to the conduction system.
    \item **Myocardial Infarction:** Injury to the heart muscle.
    \item **Acute Kidney Injury (AKI):** Related to cardiopulmonary bypass.
    \item **Respiratory Failure:** Prolonged mechanical ventilation.
    \item **Paravalvular Leak (PVL):** Incomplete sealing of the prosthetic valve.
    \item **Prosthetic Valve Thrombosis:** Clot formation on the valve.
    \item **Prosthetic Valve Degeneration:** Structural deterioration over time.
    \item **Hemolysis:** Destruction of red blood cells.
    \item **Arrhythmias:** New-onset atrial fibrillation or other dysrhythmias.
    \item **Death:** Small but significant mortality risk.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Recovery}
Postoperative recovery following heart valve replacement typically involves several phases. Immediately after surgery, patients are transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) for close monitoring. During the first 24-48 hours, vital signs, cardiac rhythm, urine output, and chest tube drainage are continuously assessed. Pain management is initiated with intravenous analgesics, and patients are usually extubated within hours if stable.

Early mobilization is encouraged to prevent complications such as deep vein thrombosis and pneumonia. Over the next 1-2 weeks, patients are gradually weaned off intravenous medications, chest tubes are removed, and they are transferred to a general ward. Physical and occupational therapy begins to help regain strength and independence.

Patients are typically discharged within 5-10 days, depending on their recovery. Long-term recovery involves continued cardiac rehabilitation, adherence to medical regimens, and a gradual return to normal activities, with full recovery often taking 3-6 months.

During heart valve replacement, the surgeon documents the appearance of the excised native valve and surrounding structures. Common findings include calcification in aortic stenosis, myxomatous degeneration in mitral regurgitation, and signs of infective endocarditis. The excised valve tissue is sent for histopathological examination, providing a definitive diagnosis and correlating with surgical observations. 

Pathology reports may reveal calcium deposition in calcific aortic stenosis, chronic inflammation in rheumatic disease, or bacterial colonies in endocarditis. These findings are crucial for understanding the disease process and guiding long-term management.

A successful postoperative course is characterized by a gradual improvement in the patient's clinical status. Patients typically experience a reduction in preoperative symptoms such as dyspnea and angina as the new valve restores normal hemodynamics. Cardiac rhythm should stabilize, and wound healing should progress without complications. Over time, improvements in left ventricular function and favorable cardiac remodeling are expected, leading to enhanced exercise tolerance and quality of life.

For patients with mechanical valves, maintaining a stable international normalized ratio (INR) is critical for preventing thromboembolic events.

Postoperative care is essential for optimal outcomes. Follow-up typically includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item **Cardiology Clinic Visits:** Scheduled at 1-2 weeks post-discharge and then at regular intervals.
  \item **Suture/Staple Removal:** Usually done at the first post-op visit.
  \item **Echocardiography:** Baseline TTE before discharge, with repeat studies as needed.
  \item **INR Monitoring:** Frequent checks for patients with mechanical valves.
  \item **Blood Tests:** Routine monitoring of renal function and complete blood counts.
  \item **Cardiac Rehabilitation:** Recommended to aid recovery and provide education.
  \item **Activity Resumption:** Gradual increase in physical activity with specific guidance.
  \item **Warning Signs:** Patients should seek immediate medical attention for concerning symptoms.
\end{itemize}

Lifelong adherence to medical therapy, including anticoagulation for mechanical valves, is crucial for long-term success.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Valvular heart disease (VHD) is a significant global health issue, with its prevalence increasing with age. Degenerative calcific aortic stenosis is the most common indication for valve replacement, particularly affecting older adults. The incidence of severe aortic stenosis reaches 3-5\% in individuals over 75 years of age. Mitral regurgitation is also prevalent, often due to myxomatous degeneration or ischemic heart disease. Surgical heart valve replacement procedures are performed in hundreds of thousands of patients annually. The advent of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has significantly changed the landscape of treatment, especially for high-risk patients. Mortality rates for isolated surgical valve replacement have declined, typically ranging from 1-5\% for elective procedures, influenced by patient comorbidities.

The outcomes of heart valve replacement are generally favorable, with significant improvements in symptoms and long-term survival for appropriately selected patients. For surgical aortic valve replacement, 10-year survival rates range from 60-80\%, depending on various factors. Mitral valve replacement also shows favorable long-term survival, although operative risks may be higher due to patient complexity.

The choice between mechanical and bioprosthetic valves impacts long-term prognosis. Mechanical valves offer durability but require lifelong anticoagulation, while bioprosthetic valves avoid chronic anticoagulation but may degenerate over time. Prognostic factors include patient age, preoperative left ventricular function, and the urgency of the procedure. Landmark clinical trials have refined prognostic considerations, demonstrating the effectiveness of transcatheter approaches in specific populations.
