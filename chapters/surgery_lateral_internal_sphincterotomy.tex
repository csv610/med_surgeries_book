\chapter{Lateral Internal Sphincterotomy}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Lateral internal sphincterotomy (LIS) is a highly effective surgical procedure involving the controlled, partial division of the internal anal sphincter muscle. This intervention is primarily undertaken to definitively treat chronic anal fissures, which are painful, linear tears or ulcers in the anoderm, typically located in the posterior midline of the anal canal. The fundamental aim of LIS is to reduce the abnormally elevated resting anal pressure, a key pathophysiological factor that contributes to the development, persistence, and impaired healing of chronic anal fissures. By alleviating the hypertonicity of the internal sphincter, the procedure significantly improves local blood supply to the anoderm, thereby facilitating fissure healing and providing profound, lasting pain relief. It is widely regarded as the gold standard surgical treatment for chronic anal fissures that have proven refractory to comprehensive non-surgical medical management.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Internal Sphincterotomy
  \item Anal Fissure Surgery
  \item Lateral Sphincterotomy
  \item Open Lateral Internal Sphincterotomy (OLSI)
  \item Closed Lateral Internal Sphincterotomy (CLSI)
  \item Subcutaneous Lateral Internal Sphincterotomy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
A thorough understanding of the anorectal anatomy is paramount for safe and effective lateral internal sphincterotomy. The anal canal, approximately 3-4 cm in length, extends from the anorectal ring to the anal verge. It is lined proximally by columnar epithelium, transitioning to specialized anoderm distally, which is highly innervated and sensitive. The dentate line, a crucial anatomical landmark, demarcates the transition from the rectal mucosa to the anoderm and is the site of the anal crypts and papillae.

The continence mechanism relies heavily on the anal sphincters. The internal anal sphincter (IAS) is an involuntary, smooth muscle continuation of the circular muscle layer of the rectum, responsible for 70-85\% of the resting anal tone. It is approximately 2.5-4 cm long and 2-3 mm thick. Superficial to the IAS lies the external anal sphincter (EAS), a voluntary, striated muscle that is part of the pelvic floor musculature, responsible for squeeze pressure and conscious continence. The intersphincteric groove, a palpable depression, separates the distal ends of the IAS and EAS and is a key surgical landmark for LIS. The arterial supply to the anal canal is primarily from the inferior rectal arteries (branches of the internal pudendal artery), with venous drainage via the inferior rectal veins into the internal pudendal veins. Nerve supply to the EAS and anoderm is from the inferior rectal nerves (branches of the pudendal nerve), while the IAS is under autonomic control. Lymphatic drainage follows the inferior rectal vessels to the inguinal lymph nodes.

\section{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The fundamental surgical principle behind lateral internal sphincterotomy is the precise and controlled reduction of internal anal sphincter hypertonicity, which is recognized as the primary underlying pathophysiological factor in the development and persistence of chronic anal fissures. The internal anal sphincter, an involuntary smooth muscle, maintains the majority of the resting anal tone. In patients afflicted with chronic anal fissures, this muscle often exhibits abnormally high resting pressure, leading to localized ischemia of the anoderm, particularly at the posterior midline where the blood supply is relatively tenuous. This chronic ischemia impairs the natural healing process and perpetuates a vicious cycle of pain, muscle spasm, and further ischemia.

The operative concept involves the surgical division of a carefully measured portion of the internal anal sphincter muscle, typically at the 3 o'clock or 9 o'clock lateral position. This targeted incision directly addresses the hypertonicity by permanently lowering the resting anal pressure, usually by 30-50\%. The reduction in pressure subsequently improves local blood flow to the anal canal and the fissure site, thereby promoting granulation tissue formation, epithelialization, and ultimately, complete fissure healing. The technical goals of the procedure are meticulously defined and include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Achieving an adequate and sustained reduction in resting anal pressure to facilitate robust fissure healing.
  \item Meticulously preserving the integrity and function of the external anal sphincter to prevent iatrogenic fecal incontinence.
  \item Minimizing trauma to the anoderm, anal mucosa, and surrounding perianal tissues to reduce postoperative pain and accelerate recovery.
  \item Ensuring complete division of the targeted internal sphincter fibers without excessive resection, which could compromise continence.
\item Avoiding injury to the dentate line and anal glands to prevent fistula formation.
\end{itemize}

\section{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The understanding and treatment of anal fissures have undergone significant evolution over centuries. Early medical texts describe various painful and often ineffective remedies, including caustic agents, cauterization, and wide excisions, which frequently resulted in significant morbidity and poor outcomes. The concept of addressing anal sphincter spasm as a cause of fissure began to emerge in the mid-20th century.

In 1951, Eisenhammer introduced the technique of posterior internal sphincterotomy, which involved dividing the internal sphincter through the fissure itself. While this approach demonstrated efficacy in fissure healing, it was associated with a higher incidence of "keyhole" deformity, delayed wound healing, and a greater risk of infection due to its proximity to the contaminated fissure bed. A pivotal advancement occurred in 1967 when Sir Alan Parks pioneered the lateral internal sphincterotomy. Parks advocated for a lateral approach, away from the primary fissure, to minimize disruption of the anoderm and reduce the risk of wound complications and keyhole deformity. This technique, performed either openly or subcutaneously, rapidly gained acceptance due to its superior success rates, lower morbidity, and reduced risk of incontinence compared to previous methods. It quickly became the preferred surgical method and remains the gold standard for chronic anal fissures, with subsequent refinements focusing on precise muscle division and minimal tissue dissection.

\section{Clinical Indications}
Lateral internal sphincterotomy is indicated for patients who meet specific clinical criteria, primarily related to chronic anal fissures that have failed conservative management.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Persistent, severe anal pain, often excruciating during and after defecation, which significantly impairs the patient's quality of life and daily activities.
  \item Chronic anal fissures, defined as those present for more than 6-8 weeks, that have failed to heal despite a comprehensive trial of non-surgical management.
  \item Fissures that have not responded to at least one, and often multiple, trials of topical pharmacological agents such as nitroglycerin ointment, diltiazem cream, or nifedipine cream, typically over a period of 6-8 weeks.
  \item Recurrent anal fissures despite previous conservative treatments, indicating an underlying and persistent internal sphincter hypertonicity.
  \item Presence of associated chronic changes such as a sentinel pile (a hypertrophied skin tag at the distal end of the fissure) or a hypertrophied anal papilla (at the proximal end), which often accompany chronic fissures and can hinder healing.
  \item Patients who have failed or are unsuitable for botulinum toxin injections into the internal anal sphincter due to contraindications, lack of efficacy, or patient preference.
  \item Objective evidence of high resting anal canal pressure on anorectal manometry, although this specialized test is not always routinely performed prior to surgery for typical chronic fissures.
  \item Fissures associated with significant bleeding that is refractory to conservative measures.
\end{itemize}

\section{Clinical Positioning}
Lateral internal sphincterotomy is unequivocally considered the primary surgical treatment for chronic anal fissures. It is not typically the initial intervention for an acute anal fissure, which often resolves spontaneously or with simple conservative measures such as increased dietary fiber, stool softeners, adequate hydration, sitz baths, and topical agents like lidocaine or hydrocortisone. These non-surgical approaches are the first-line management for acute fissures, aiming to break the pain-spasm cycle.

However, once an anal fissure becomes chronic, characterized by its persistence beyond 6-8 weeks, the development of fibrotic edges, a sentinel pile, or a hypertrophied anal papilla, LIS assumes its role as the most effective and definitive treatment option. It is generally reserved for cases where multiple trials of non-surgical therapies, including topical nitrates, calcium channel blockers, and botulinum toxin injections, have failed to achieve complete healing or provide lasting symptom relief. In this context, LIS serves as the primary surgical intervention, offering superior healing rates and long-term efficacy compared to continued medical management. It is rarely a secondary or salvage procedure, as its high success rates and relatively low complication profile, when performed correctly, position it as the definitive step after conservative measures have been exhausted.

\section{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Lateral internal sphincterotomy is typically performed as an outpatient procedure, allowing patients to return home the same day. The choice of anesthesia varies but commonly includes general anesthesia, spinal anesthesia, or local anesthesia with intravenous sedation. Patient positioning is crucial for optimal exposure, usually the prone jackknife position with buttocks taped apart, or the lithotomy position. The perianal area is meticulously prepped with an antiseptic solution and draped in a sterile fashion. A gentle digital rectal examination is performed to assess anal tone and identify the intersphincteric groove.

The procedure can be executed using either an open or a closed (subcutaneous) technique, with the choice often depending on surgeon preference and experience.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Open Technique (Parks' Technique):}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item A small, 1-2 cm transverse or oblique incision is made in the perianal skin, typically at the 3 o'clock or 9 o'clock position (lateral to the anal verge), avoiding the posterior midline.
      \item The anoderm and subcutaneous tissue are carefully dissected with fine scissors or electrocautery to expose the intersphincteric groove, which is the plane between the internal and external anal sphincters.
      \item The internal anal sphincter is identified as a pale, glistening, fibrous band of smooth muscle, distinct from the more reddish, coarser fibers of the external sphincter.
      \item A small, controlled incision is made through the internal anal sphincter, extending from just below the dentate line (to avoid mucosal injury) to its distal end, typically dividing approximately 50-75\% of its thickness. The external sphincter is meticulously avoided to prevent incontinence.
      \item Hemostasis is achieved using gentle electrocautery.
      \item The skin incision is usually left open to heal by secondary intention, or it may be loosely closed with one or two absorbable sutures to minimize infection risk and promote drainage.
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Closed (Subcutaneous) Technique (Notaras' Technique):}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item A very small stab incision (approximately 3-5 mm) is made in the perianal skin at the 3 o'clock or 9 o'clock position.
      \item A fine, curved hemostat (e.g., a blunt-tipped artery forceps) or a narrow scalpel blade is inserted through this incision into the intersphincteric groove, carefully tunneling beneath the anoderm.
      \item The internal anal sphincter is identified, hooked by the instrument, and elevated.
      \item The internal sphincter is then divided subcutaneously by either cutting it with the scalpel blade or by crushing it with the hemostat, ensuring the external sphincter is not injured. The division is confirmed by palpation.
      \item The small skin incision is typically left open to heal.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
Both techniques aim to achieve the same goal of partial internal sphincter division while minimizing trauma. No drains or foreign body implants are typically used. The procedure usually takes 10-20 minutes.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Prior to lateral internal sphincterotomy, a comprehensive preoperative evaluation is essential to ensure patient safety, optimize surgical outcomes, and minimize potential risks. This typically commences with a detailed medical history, focusing on bowel habits, previous anorectal surgeries, any history of fecal incontinence, and current medications. A thorough physical examination, including a digital rectal examination and anoscopy, is performed to confirm the diagnosis of a chronic anal fissure, assess anal tone, and rule out other pathologies.

Routine laboratory tests are usually ordered, including a complete blood count (CBC), basic metabolic panel, and coagulation profile (PT/INR, PTT) to assess overall health and bleeding risk. For patients with significant comorbidities (e.g., cardiac disease, pulmonary issues), medical clearance from their primary care physician or a specialist (e.g., cardiologist, pulmonologist) may be required.

Specific preparations for the day of surgery include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients are strictly instructed to be NPO (nil per os, nothing by mouth) for a specified period, typically 6-8 hours, before surgery to prevent the risk of aspiration during anesthesia.
  \item \textbf{Bowel Preparation:} A mild laxative (e.g., magnesium citrate) or a cleansing enema may be prescribed for the evening before or morning of surgery to ensure the rectum is empty. This reduces the risk of fecal contamination during the procedure and improves surgical visualization.
  \item \textbf{Medication Review:} All current medications must be reviewed with the surgeon and anesthesiologist. Anticoagulants (e.g., warfarin, novel oral anticoagulants) or antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel) may need to be temporarily discontinued or adjusted according to established guidelines to minimize bleeding risk.
  \item \textbf{Prophylactic Antibiotics:} Although not universally mandated for all LIS cases, prophylactic intravenous antibiotics (e.g., a single dose of a cephalosporin) are often administered prior to incision, particularly in cases with higher infection risk or if the patient has specific risk factors.
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} A detailed discussion of the procedure, its anticipated benefits, potential risks (especially incontinence), alternative treatments, and expected postoperative course is conducted. Comprehensive informed consent is then obtained from the patient.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
While lateral internal sphincterotomy is a highly effective and generally safe procedure, certain conditions may contraindicate its use or necessitate extreme caution to prevent adverse outcomes.

\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pre-existing significant fecal incontinence (to solid, liquid, or even gas), as further division of the internal sphincter could irreversibly worsen symptoms and severely impact quality of life.
  \item Active inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) affecting the anal canal, such as Crohn's disease, due to a substantially increased risk of poor wound healing, chronic non-healing wounds, and complex fistula formation.
  \item Active perianal sepsis, including an acute perianal abscess or a symptomatic fistula-in-ano, which must be adequately treated and resolved prior to considering sphincterotomy.
  \item Uncorrected coagulopathy or severe bleeding disorders, which significantly increase the risk of uncontrolled intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage.
  \item Suspected or confirmed anal canal malignancy, which requires a completely different diagnostic and therapeutic approach.
  \item Very low resting anal pressure on anorectal manometry, as further sphincter division would almost certainly lead to incontinence.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications and Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Advanced age, particularly in multiparous women, due to a higher baseline risk of occult sphincter weakness from obstetric trauma or age-related muscle degeneration, increasing the risk of postoperative incontinence.
  \item Prior anal surgery, especially previous sphincterotomy, extensive hemorrhoidectomy, or complex fistula surgery, which may have already compromised sphincter function or altered anatomical planes.
  \item Complex or atypical anal fissures (e.g., anterior fissure in men, multiple fissures, or fissures not in the posterior/anterior midline), which may suggest an underlying systemic disease (e.g., tuberculosis, syphilis, HIV) or require further investigation to rule out secondary causes.
  \item Immunocompromised states (e.g., HIV/AIDS, organ transplant recipients on immunosuppressants, patients on high-dose corticosteroids), which can significantly impair wound healing and increase the risk of infection.
  \item Pregnancy, where non-surgical management is always preferred due to potential risks to the fetus and the transient nature of many pregnancy-related fissures.
  \item Patients with a history of obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI), even if asymptomatic, warrant careful preoperative assessment and counseling regarding increased incontinence risk.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
While lateral internal sphincterotomy is generally considered a safe procedure with a low complication rate, patients must be thoroughly informed of potential risks.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Minor bleeding or spotting from the incision site is common in the immediate postoperative period. Significant hemorrhage requiring re-intervention or blood transfusion is rare but possible.
      \item \textbf{Infection:} Wound infection, though uncommon given the clean-contaminated nature of the field, can occur and may manifest as redness, swelling, pain, or purulent discharge, potentially requiring antibiotics or drainage.
      \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications:} Risks associated with general or regional anesthesia include nausea, vomiting, allergic reactions to medications, respiratory or cardiac complications, and, rarely, nerve damage or spinal headache (with spinal anesthesia).
      \item \textbf{Urinary Retention:} Temporary difficulty or inability to urinate (urinary retention) can occur, especially after spinal anesthesia, and may necessitate temporary catheterization.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Fecal Incontinence:} This is the most significant and feared complication. It can range from minor incontinence to flatus (gas) or liquid stool, to, rarely, incontinence to solid stool. The risk is higher in older patients, females (especially multiparous), and those with pre-existing or occult sphincter weakness. It may be temporary or, in rare cases, permanent.
      \item \textbf{Fissure Non-Healing or Recurrence:} Despite the high success rate, a small percentage of fissures (2-10\%) may fail to heal completely or may recur over time, potentially requiring further medical or surgical treatment.
      \item \textbf{Anal Fistula or Abscess Formation:} Though rare, injury to the anal glands or crypts during dissection, or inadequate hemostasis leading to hematoma, can predispose to perianal abscess or fistula formation.
      \item \textbf{Persistent Pain:} While LIS is highly effective for pain relief, some patients may experience persistent or chronic anal pain, though this is uncommon.
      \item \textbf{Hematoma Formation:} A collection of blood under the skin incision can occur, leading to swelling and discomfort, usually resolving spontaneously.
      \item \textbf{Keyhole Deformity:} More commonly associated with posterior sphincterotomy, but can rarely occur with lateral approaches if too much tissue is excised or the wound heals poorly, leading to a persistent opening at the anal verge that can cause minor soiling.
      \item \textbf{Perianal Edema or Swelling:} Common in the immediate postoperative period, usually resolving within days to weeks.
      \item \textbf{Mucosal Injury:} Accidental incision of the anal mucosa during the procedure, which typically heals without specific intervention but can increase the risk of infection.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Immediately following lateral internal sphincterotomy, patients are transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for close monitoring as they recover from anesthesia. Pain management is promptly initiated, typically with oral analgesics, often including a combination of acetaminophen and a mild opioid for breakthrough pain. Most patients are discharged home on the same day, once they are fully awake, alert, hemodynamically stable, and able to void spontaneously. Patients are generally advised to avoid strenuous activities, heavy lifting, and prolonged sitting for the first few days.

Over the first 24-48 hours, mild to moderate pain, discomfort, and some serosanguinous drainage from the wound are common and expected. Patients are strongly encouraged to resume a high-fiber diet and use stool softeners (e.g., docusate sodium, psyllium husk) to ensure soft, easy bowel movements, thereby preventing straining that could disrupt the healing site. Sitz baths (soaking the perianal area in warm water for 15-20 minutes) are highly recommended several times a day, especially after bowel movements, to promote hygiene, reduce swelling, alleviate discomfort, and encourage wound healing. Significant pain relief is often experienced within the first week, and the fissure itself typically shows signs of healing within 2-3 weeks. Complete epithelialization and closure of the fissure usually take 4-6 weeks, with the incision site healing by secondary intention over a similar period. Long-term, the vast majority of patients experience complete resolution of their symptoms and return to normal bowel function without pain or significant continence issues.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During a lateral internal sphincterotomy, the surgeon's primary findings relate to the macroscopic appearance of the anal canal and the characteristics of the internal sphincter. The chronic anal fissure itself is typically observed as a deep, linear ulcer with indurated, fibrotic edges, often accompanied by a hypertrophied anal papilla proximally and a sentinel pile (skin tag) distally. The internal anal sphincter is palpated and visually confirmed to be hypertonic and thickened. Upon division, the muscle fibers are seen to retract, indicating successful release of tension. The absence of other perianal pathology, such as fistulas or abscesses, is also noted.

For lateral internal sphincterotomy, there is typically no resected tissue sent for pathological examination, as the procedure involves incision rather than excision of the sphincter muscle. However, if a concomitant sentinel pile or hypertrophied anal papilla is excised (which is sometimes done to improve hygiene or remove a symptomatic tag), these specimens would be sent to pathology. The pathologist would then describe the excised tissue, confirming it as benign squamous epithelium with underlying fibrous tissue for a sentinel pile, or hypertrophied anal papilla tissue, ruling out any malignancy or unusual inflammatory processes. In rare cases of atypical fissures, a biopsy of the fissure edge might be taken to exclude granulomatous disease (e.g., Crohn's) or malignancy, and the pathology report would detail these findings.

\section{Expected Postoperative Course}
A normal and successful postoperative course following lateral internal sphincterotomy is characterized by a predictable progression of symptom resolution and healing. Patients typically experience immediate, albeit often partial, relief from the severe, chronic anal pain that prompted the surgery. This pain relief usually becomes substantial within the first few days to a week, allowing for a significant reduction in analgesic requirements. The first bowel movement post-surgery, while potentially anxiety-provoking, is generally less painful than pre-operative defecation, especially with diligent use of stool softeners and a high-fiber diet.

The fissure itself begins to heal rapidly due to the improved blood flow and reduced spasm. Patients should notice a cessation of bleeding with bowel movements within the first week or two. The incision site, whether open or loosely closed, will heal over 4-6 weeks, often with minimal scarring. Minor discomfort, swelling, and occasional serosanguinous discharge are normal during this period. The goal is to restore normal, pain-free bowel function and a return to all usual activities without the debilitating symptoms of a chronic anal fissure, which is achieved in the vast majority of patients. Any minor flatus incontinence experienced initially typically resolves as the tissues heal and the external sphincter adapts.

\section{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative care after lateral internal sphincterotomy is crucial for monitoring healing, preventing complications, and ensuring optimal long-term outcomes.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Follow-up Visits:} Patients typically have a scheduled follow-up appointment with the surgeon within 2-4 weeks after the procedure. This visit allows the surgeon to assess wound healing, confirm fissure closure, evaluate symptom resolution, and address any patient concerns. Further visits may be scheduled as needed, particularly if healing is delayed or complications arise.
  \item \textbf{Wound Care \& Hygiene:} If the incision was left open, patients are instructed on proper perianal hygiene, including regular sitz baths (2-3 times daily and after bowel movements) to keep the area clean, reduce inflammation, and promote healing by secondary intention. Absorbable sutures are often used if the wound is closed, negating the need for suture removal.
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Gradual return to normal daily activities is encouraged. Strenuous exercise, heavy lifting, and prolonged sitting are typically restricted for 2-4 weeks to prevent strain on the healing tissues.
  \item \textbf{Dietary and Bowel Management:} Continued emphasis on a high-fiber diet (25-30g/day), adequate fluid intake (8-10 glasses of water daily), and the use of stool softeners or bulk-forming agents is vital to prevent constipation and straining, which could impede healing or cause recurrence.
  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients are thoroughly educated on warning signs that warrant immediate medical attention. These include increasing or severe pain unresponsive to analgesics, fever (above 101.5$^{\circ}$F or 38.6$^{\circ}$C), significant or persistent bleeding (more than a few drops), purulent discharge from the wound, or new-onset or worsening fecal incontinence.
\end{itemize}
Routine imaging or laboratory tests are generally not required unless complications are suspected or the fissure fails to heal. Physical therapy or specialized rehabilitation is typically not necessary for this procedure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anal fissures are a highly prevalent anorectal condition, affecting a substantial portion of the population, with an estimated lifetime incidence of approximately 1 in 10 individuals. The annual incidence is reported to be between 1 and 2 per 1,000 population. While acute fissures can occur at any age, chronic anal fissures, for which lateral internal sphincterotomy is the definitive treatment, most commonly affect young to middle-aged adults, with a peak incidence observed between 30 and 50 years of age. There is no significant gender predilection, although some studies suggest a slight female predominance, potentially linked to factors such as childbirth and obstetric trauma. The primary indication for LIS is chronic anal fissure refractory to conservative medical management, which accounts for a significant proportion of all chronic fissure cases, estimated to be around 30-50\%. Mortality directly attributable to lateral internal sphincterotomy is exceedingly rare, virtually zero, reflecting its safety profile. Morbidity is primarily related to the risk of fecal incontinence, which varies depending on the definition and assessment method, but typically ranges from 0-15\% for minor incontinence (e.g., to flatus or minor soiling) and less than 1-2\% for major incontinence (e.g., to solid stool). Risk factors for anal fissures include chronic constipation, prolonged diarrhea, and previous anal trauma.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Lateral internal sphincterotomy consistently demonstrates excellent outcomes and a highly favorable prognosis for patients suffering from chronic anal fissures. Healing rates are remarkably high, typically ranging from 90\% to 98\%, establishing LIS as the most effective and definitive treatment for this debilitating condition. Most patients experience rapid and significant pain relief, often within days to weeks following the procedure, leading to a substantial improvement in their quality of life. Functional outcomes are generally very good, with the vast majority of patients returning to normal bowel function and experiencing sustained symptom resolution.

The recurrence rate after a successful LIS is notably low, usually less than 5\%, which is significantly lower compared to non-surgical treatments or botulinum toxin injections. Prognostic factors for successful outcomes include proper patient selection, meticulous surgical technique ensuring adequate but not excessive sphincter division, and diligent adherence to postoperative care instructions. While the primary goal is fissure healing and pain relief, long-term follow-up studies confirm sustained symptom resolution in most individuals, with a low incidence of persistent or severe incontinence. The procedure's high efficacy, low recurrence, and acceptable complication profile solidify its position as the gold standard surgical intervention for chronic anal fissures refractory to conservative management.
