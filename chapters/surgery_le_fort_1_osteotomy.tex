\chapter{Le Fort I Osteotomy (Maxillary Osteotomy)}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Le Fort I osteotomy is a foundational orthognathic surgical procedure meticulously designed to correct skeletal discrepancies of the upper jaw, or maxilla. This precise operation involves surgically dividing the maxilla from the skull base and facial skeleton, typically above the roots of the upper teeth, to allow for its three-dimensional repositioning. The maxilla can be moved forward (advancement), backward (setback), upward (impaction), downward (downfracture), or rotated to achieve optimal dental occlusion, improve facial balance, and enhance functional aspects such as chewing, speech, and nasal breathing. It is a highly specialized procedure performed under general anesthesia by an oral and maxillofacial surgeon, often in conjunction with comprehensive orthodontic treatment. The repositioned bone segment is then stabilized using rigid internal fixation with biocompatible titanium plates and screws, facilitating predictable bone healing and long-term stability of the corrected jaw position.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Maxillary osteotomy
  \item Upper jaw surgery
  \item Le Fort I advancement
  \item Maxillary impaction
  \item Orthognathic surgery of the maxilla
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The Le Fort I osteotomy primarily involves the maxilla, a paired bone forming the upper jaw, central face, and parts of the orbital floor, nasal cavity, and hard palate. Key anatomical structures and their relationships are critical for safe and effective surgery. The osteotomy line typically extends from the piriform aperture anteriorly, across the lateral maxillary wall superior to the tooth apices, and posteriorly to the pterygomaxillary junction. The **infraorbital nerve**, a branch of the trigeminal nerve (V2), exits the infraorbital foramen approximately 5-10 mm below the orbital rim and supplies sensation to the lower eyelid, side of the nose, upper lip, and anterior cheek; it must be protected during dissection and osteotomy. The **greater palatine artery**, a major blood supply to the maxilla, descends from the pterygopalatine fossa through the greater palatine canal and runs anteriorly along the hard palate; its preservation is crucial for maxillary vascularity.

The **maxillary sinuses**, large air-filled cavities within the maxilla, are inevitably entered during the osteotomy. Their integrity and drainage pathways are important considerations. The **nasal septum** and **lateral nasal walls** are also involved, requiring osteotomies to fully mobilize the maxilla. The **pterygomaxillary junction**, where the maxilla articulates with the pterygoid plates of the sphenoid bone, is a critical posterior osteotomy site, requiring careful separation to avoid injury to the pterygoid plexus of veins and the internal maxillary artery. Lymphatic drainage of the maxilla primarily flows to the submandibular and deep cervical lymph nodes. Understanding these intricate relationships is paramount for minimizing complications and ensuring successful outcomes.

\section{*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}}
The fundamental principle of the Le Fort I osteotomy is the surgical correction of dentofacial deformities that arise from an improperly positioned maxilla relative to the mandible and cranial base. Malocclusion, characterized by an incorrect bite, and facial imbalance often result when the upper and lower jaws do not articulate harmoniously. By performing a controlled, horizontal osteotomy, the surgeon gains the ability to precisely mobilize the entire maxilla as a single unit or in segments. This allows for its repositioning into a predetermined, ideal anatomical and functional relationship with the mandible and the rest of the facial skeleton, guided by pre-surgical planning.

The rationale for this procedure is to restore optimal oral function, improve facial aesthetics, and alleviate associated symptoms. Technical goals include achieving a stable Class I occlusal relationship, improving facial aesthetics by correcting midface projection or vertical dimension, and optimizing functional parameters such as mastication, speech articulation, and nasal airway patency. Once the maxilla is moved to its planned position, guided by pre-surgical virtual planning and intraoperative splints, it is secured with small, biocompatible titanium plates and screws. This rigid internal fixation provides immediate stability, promoting primary bone healing and fusion (osteosynthesis) in the new, corrected position, thereby preventing relapse and ensuring long-term stability of the surgical outcome.

\section{*{History \& Surgical Evolution}}
The Le Fort I osteotomy is named after the pioneering French surgeon René Le Fort, who, in 1901, meticulously described characteristic patterns of midfacial fractures based on cadaveric studies. While Le Fort's work was crucial for understanding facial trauma, it was not until much later that his classification was adapted for elective osteotomies. Early attempts at maxillary repositioning in the mid-20th century were often fraught with complications due to inadequate fixation methods and a poor understanding of maxillary vascularity.

Significant advancements in the surgical approach for repositioning the maxilla were made by surgeons like Hugo Obwegeser in the 1960s, who refined the intraoral approach, minimizing external scarring, and developing techniques for controlled downfracture of the maxilla. William Bell's extensive research in the 1970s on blood supply to the maxilla and the development of rigid internal fixation with plates and screws revolutionized the procedure, making it safer, more predictable, and significantly reducing post-operative morbidity and relapse. Today, with the advent of three-dimensional imaging, virtual surgical planning, and custom surgical guides, the Le Fort I osteotomy has evolved into a highly precise, standard, and reliable technique in orthognathic surgery, offering excellent functional and aesthetic outcomes with enhanced predictability.

\section{*{Clinical Indications}}
The Le Fort I osteotomy is indicated to address a range of skeletal and functional issues related to maxillary position, typically after skeletal growth has ceased.

\begin{itemize}
  \item To correct severe malocclusion, such as an anterior open bite, deep overbite, or Class II/Class III skeletal discrepancies, when orthodontics alone cannot achieve proper dental alignment.
  \item To treat midface deficiency or hypoplasia, where the upper jaw sits too far back, resulting in a concave facial profile and often an underbite.
  \item To improve facial aesthetics and harmony by correcting vertical maxillary excess (long face syndrome) through impaction, or vertical maxillary deficiency through downfracture and bone grafting.
  \item To enhance chewing efficiency and masticatory function, which may be severely compromised by an unstable or incorrect bite.
  \item To improve speech articulation, particularly in cases where jaw position affects tongue placement or airflow.
  \item To address certain cases of obstructive sleep apnea where maxillary advancement can enlarge the posterior airway space, often in conjunction with mandibular advancement.
  \item To correct facial asymmetry involving the maxilla, often in conjunction with other orthognathic procedures.
  \item To manage sequelae of cleft lip and palate, where maxillary growth is often restricted, leading to midface retrusion and malocclusion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
The Le Fort I osteotomy is considered a primary surgical procedure for the definitive correction of skeletal maxillary discrepancies. However, within the broader context of orthognathic treatment, it functions as a secondary, yet essential, step in a multi-phase therapeutic plan. The overall treatment typically commences with a period of pre-surgical orthodontics, which is a primary intervention aimed at aligning the teeth within each arch. This initial orthodontic phase is crucial because it "decompensates" the dental arches, positioning the teeth correctly over their respective basal bone, thereby allowing the jaws to be surgically repositioned into their ideal skeletal relationship without dental interferences.

Therefore, while the surgery itself is the primary method for skeletal correction, it is secondary to the initial orthodontic preparation. In rare instances, such as severe trauma reconstruction or immediate correction of certain congenital deformities, the Le Fort I osteotomy might be performed as a more immediate primary intervention. However, for elective correction of dentofacial deformities, it is almost invariably integrated into a comprehensive treatment sequence that includes pre-surgical orthodontics, the surgical phase, and post-surgical orthodontic finishing to fine-tune the bite and ensure long-term stability.

\section{*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}}
The Le Fort I osteotomy is performed under general anesthesia, typically with nasotracheal intubation to allow for unimpeded access to the oral cavity and facilitate intraoperative occlusal adjustments. The patient is positioned supine, and the surgical field is prepped and draped in a sterile fashion.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Incision and Exposure:} An intraoral incision is made along the gum line (vestibular incision) inside the upper lip, extending from the first molar region on one side to the other. A subperiosteal dissection then exposes the anterior and lateral walls of the maxilla, the zygomatic buttresses, and the piriform rims of the nasal aperture, carefully protecting the infraorbital nerves.
  \item \textbf{Osteotomy Cuts:} Using an oscillating saw, precise horizontal osteotomy cuts are made through the lateral maxillary walls, extending posteriorly to the pterygomaxillary junction. Care is taken to remain above the apices of the maxillary teeth and below the infraorbital foramen. The nasal septum is separated from the maxilla, and osteotomies are made through the lateral nasal walls to fully mobilize the maxilla.
  \item \textbf{Downfracture and Mobilization:} The maxilla is then "downfractured" and carefully mobilized from the cranial base and pterygoid plates using specialized osteotomes. This allows the surgeon to visualize the nasal floor and posterior maxilla, enabling precise removal or addition of bone as needed for impaction or downfracture.
  \item \textbf{Repositioning and Occlusal Guidance:} The mobilized maxilla is then moved into its planned three-dimensional position. This precise repositioning is guided by pre-fabricated surgical splints (wafers) that dictate the new occlusal relationship between the upper and lower teeth, ensuring the planned bite is achieved.
  \item \textbf{Rigid Internal Fixation:} Once the maxilla is in the ideal position, it is secured to the stable facial skeleton using small titanium plates and screws. These plates are typically placed at the piriform rims and zygomatic buttresses, providing strong, stable fixation. Bone grafts, often harvested from the maxilla itself or synthetic materials, may be placed in gaps created by advancement or impaction to promote healing.
  \item \textbf{Closure:} The intraoral incisions are meticulously closed with dissolving sutures. A nasal pack may be placed temporarily to control bleeding, and light intermaxillary fixation (IMF) with elastic bands may be applied to guide the bite during initial healing and promote muscle memory.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Comprehensive preoperative workup and preparation are paramount for a successful Le Fort I osteotomy, involving a multidisciplinary team including the oral and maxillofacial surgeon, orthodontist, and anesthesiologist.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic Records:} Extensive pre-operative records are gathered, including panoramic and cephalometric X-rays, dental study models, intraoral and extraoral photographs, and often a cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan. These records are crucial for detailed analysis of the skeletal and dental relationships, airway assessment, and identification of critical anatomical structures.
  \item \textbf{Virtual Surgical Planning:} Modern planning often involves 3D virtual surgical planning using specialized software. This allows the surgeon to simulate the osteotomy and repositioning on a virtual patient model, design custom surgical guides, and fabricate precise occlusal splints that are used intraoperatively to guide the maxillary movement.
  \item \textbf{Orthodontic Preparation:} The patient typically undergoes a period of pre-surgical orthodontics (braces) to align the teeth within each arch, ensuring they will fit together correctly after the jaw repositioning. The orthodontist and surgeon coordinate closely on the timing of surgery, ensuring dental decompensation is complete.
  \item \textbf{Medical Clearance:} A thorough medical evaluation is performed by the surgeon and often by the patient's primary care physician or an anesthesiologist to ensure the patient is fit for general anesthesia and surgery. This includes blood tests (e.g., complete blood count, coagulation profile), electrocardiogram (ECG), and other studies as indicated by the patient's health status and comorbidities.
  \item \textbf{Medication Review:} Patients are instructed to stop certain medications, such as blood thinners (e.g., aspirin, NSAIDs, warfarin) and herbal supplements (e.g., ginkgo biloba, ginseng), typically 1-2 weeks before surgery to minimize bleeding risk. Specific instructions are given for managing chronic medications.
  \item \textbf{Smoking Cessation:} Patients are strongly advised to stop smoking several weeks prior to surgery, as smoking significantly impairs bone healing, increases the risk of infection, and can compromise vascularity.
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients must adhere to "nil per os" (NPO) instructions, meaning no food or drink for a specified period (usually 6-8 hours) before surgery to prevent aspiration during anesthesia.
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} A detailed discussion of the procedure, its goals, potential risks, benefits, alternatives, and expected recovery is conducted, and the patient provides informed consent.
  \item \textbf{Pre-operative Antibiotics:} Prophylactic antibiotics are typically administered intravenously just before the incision to reduce the risk of surgical site infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Uncontrolled systemic medical conditions (e.g., severe uncontrolled diabetes, severe cardiovascular disease, bleeding disorders, active autoimmune disease) that significantly increase anesthetic or surgical risk.
  \item Active infection in the teeth, sinuses, or gums that has not been adequately treated prior to surgery.
  \item Unrealistic patient expectations or significant psychological instability that may compromise post-operative compliance or satisfaction.
  \item Incomplete skeletal growth in adolescents, as surgery before growth cessation can lead to unpredictable outcomes and relapse.
  \item Active malignancy in the surgical field or adjacent structures.
  \item Severe, uncorrectable coagulopathy.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications and Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Smoking, which significantly delays bone healing (non-union) and increases the risk of infection and other complications. Patients are strongly advised to cease smoking for several weeks pre- and post-operatively.
  \item Poor oral hygiene or untreated periodontal disease, which increases the risk of post-operative infection. Intensive pre-surgical dental cleaning and treatment are required.
  \item Certain medications, such as bisphosphonates or other anti-resorptive agents, which can impair bone healing and increase the risk of osteonecrosis of the jaw. Careful risk-benefit assessment is necessary.
  \item Previous radiation therapy to the head and neck region, which can compromise tissue vascularity and healing, increasing complication rates.
  \item Significant temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction, which may require pre-surgical management or influence surgical planning to avoid exacerbation.
  \item Pregnancy, due to potential risks of anesthesia and medications to the fetus. Elective surgery is typically deferred until after delivery.
  \item Patients with severe obstructive sleep apnea, where the procedure may be part of a comprehensive treatment plan but requires careful airway management.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
While the Le Fort I osteotomy is generally safe, like any major surgical procedure, it carries potential risks and complications. These can be broadly categorized as general surgical risks and procedure-specific risks.

\textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications:} Reactions to anesthetic agents, respiratory problems (e.g., aspiration, airway obstruction), nausea, vomiting, or, rarely, more severe cardiovascular events (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke) or malignant hyperthermia.
  \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Intraoperative or post-operative hemorrhage, which may necessitate blood transfusion in rare cases. Hematoma formation can also occur.
  \item \textbf{Infection:} Infection of the surgical site, bone (osteomyelitis), or hardware, potentially requiring prolonged antibiotic treatment, surgical debridement, or removal of plates/screws.
  \item \textbf{Swelling and Bruising:} Significant facial swelling and bruising are common post-operatively and typically resolve over several weeks, but can be extensive.
  \item \textbf{Pain:} Post-operative pain is expected and managed with analgesics, but chronic pain is rare.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nerve Injury:} Temporary or, rarely, permanent numbness or altered sensation (paresthesia/dysesthesia) of the cheeks, upper lip, gums, and nose due to injury to the infraorbital nerves or other sensory nerves. Complete loss of sensation is rare.
  \item \textbf{Dental Injury:} Damage to tooth roots, devitalization of teeth (requiring root canal treatment), or loosening of teeth adjacent to the osteotomy cuts, potentially requiring endodontic treatment or extraction.
  \item \textbf{Relapse:} The jaw may gradually shift back toward its original position, requiring further orthodontic treatment or, in rare cases, revision surgery. This is minimized with rigid fixation.
  \item \textbf{Non-union or Malunion:} Failure of the bone segments to heal together (non-union) or healing in an incorrect position (malunion), which may necessitate further surgical intervention.
  \item \textbf{Sinus Complications:} Communication with the maxillary sinuses is inherent to the procedure; potential risks include acute or chronic sinusitis, chronic nasal drainage, or oroantral fistula.
  \item \textbf{Nasal Changes:} Subtle changes in nasal shape, width, or tip projection can occur due to the osteotomy and maxillary repositioning, sometimes requiring secondary rhinoplasty for correction.
  \item \textbf{Hardware Complications:} Exposure of plates or screws through the oral mucosa, requiring removal, or allergic reaction to titanium.
  \item \textbf{Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ) Dysfunction:} New onset or worsening of TMJ pain, clicking, or limited jaw movement, though sometimes TMJ symptoms can improve.
  \item \textbf{Speech Changes:} Temporary changes in speech, particularly nasal resonance or articulation, which usually resolve as swelling subsides and adaptation occurs.
  \item \textbf{Vision Changes:} Extremely rare, but potential for orbital hematoma or injury to orbital contents during superior dissection, leading to temporary or permanent vision changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Immediately after the Le Fort I osteotomy, the patient is transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for close monitoring of vital signs, airway patency, pain, and nausea. Significant facial swelling and discomfort are expected, and pain is managed with intravenous or oral analgesics, while anti-nausea medications are administered as needed. Patients typically remain hospitalized for one to two days. During this initial period, intravenous fluids are given, and a liquid or very soft, non-chewing diet is initiated. The upper jaw may be temporarily secured to the lower jaw with light elastic bands (intermaxillary fixation) to guide the bite and promote initial stability, though rigid fixation often allows for immediate limited jaw movement. Oral hygiene is critical, often involving gentle rinsing with antiseptic mouthwash.

Over the first 1-2 weeks, swelling gradually subsides, though it can take several months for all residual swelling to resolve completely. Patients are restricted to a non-chewing, soft diet for 4-6 weeks to allow for initial bone healing and consolidation. Meticulous oral hygiene, often involving gentle rinsing with antiseptic mouthwash and soft toothbrushes, is crucial to prevent infection. Physical activity is restricted, and patients are advised to avoid strenuous exercise, heavy lifting, and contact sports for several months (typically 3-6 months) to protect the healing bone. Regular follow-up appointments with the surgeon monitor healing progress, and orthodontic treatment typically resumes a few weeks post-surgery to fine-tune the occlusal relationship. Full return to normal activities and diet is gradual and guided by the surgeon's instructions, with complete bone healing taking up to a year.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During a Le Fort I osteotomy, the primary surgical findings relate to the pre-existing skeletal deformity of the maxilla. The surgeon documents the specific malposition of the maxilla relative to the cranial base and mandible, such as maxillary retrusion, advancement, vertical excess or deficiency, or asymmetry. These findings are typically quantified by the amount of movement required to achieve the planned occlusal and facial aesthetic goals. For instance, the surgeon will note the precise millimeters of advancement, impaction, or setback performed. The integrity of the infraorbital nerves, greater palatine arteries, and maxillary sinus lining are also key intraoperative findings. In the context of elective orthognathic surgery, there is typically no "pathology" report in the traditional sense, as no diseased tissue is resected. However, if bone grafts are harvested (e.g., from the maxilla itself or iliac crest) or if any suspicious lesions are encountered incidentally, these would be sent for histopathological examination. The primary "pathology" addressed is the skeletal malformation itself, which is corrected by the surgical repositioning and stabilized by rigid internal fixation.

\section{*{Expected Postoperative Course}}
A normal and successful postoperative course following a Le Fort I osteotomy is characterized by a predictable progression of healing and functional recovery. Initially, patients experience significant facial swelling and discomfort, which gradually diminish over the first few weeks. Pain is typically well-controlled with prescribed analgesics and resolves within 1-2 weeks. Sensory changes, such as numbness or altered sensation in the upper lip and cheeks, are common but usually improve significantly over several months, with many patients regaining near-normal sensation. The diet progresses from liquids to soft foods over 4-6 weeks, after which a gradual return to a normal diet is expected as bone healing advances. The bite should feel stable and improved, guided by orthodontic elastics and subsequent adjustments. Patients typically resume light activities within a few weeks, with a full return to strenuous activities and contact sports delayed until complete bone consolidation, usually 3-6 months. The ultimate goal is a stable, functional occlusion and improved facial aesthetics, with minimal long-term complications.

\section{*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}}
Postoperative care and follow-up are critical to monitor healing, manage any complications, and ensure the long-term stability of the surgical outcome.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Surgical Follow-up Visits:} Initial visits are typically scheduled at 1 week, 2-4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year post-surgery. These visits allow the surgeon to assess wound healing, swelling resolution, occlusal stability, and overall recovery.
  \item \textbf{Orthodontic Adjustments:} Orthodontic treatment usually resumes a few weeks after surgery to fine-tune the bite, close any remaining spaces, and ensure optimal intercuspation. This phase can last for several months to a year.
  \item \textbf{Imaging Studies:} Panoramic X-rays are often taken at 3 and 6 months to confirm bony union and assess the integrity of the fixation hardware. A final cephalometric X-ray may be taken at the completion of treatment to document the final skeletal and dental relationships.
  \item \textbf{Elastic Wear:} Patients are often instructed to wear light elastic bands between the upper and lower jaws for several weeks or months to guide the bite and promote muscle memory.
  \item \textbf{Oral Hygiene Instruction:} Reinforcement of meticulous oral hygiene techniques is provided to prevent infection and maintain periodontal health, especially around orthodontic appliances.
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Gradual return to normal activities is advised, with specific instructions on avoiding contact sports or activities that could impact the healing jaw for several months.
  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients are educated on warning signs to report to their surgeon, such as persistent fever, severe or worsening pain, pus drainage, increasing swelling, significant changes in sensation or bite, or difficulty breathing.
\end{itemize}
Completion of orthodontic treatment, typically involving removal of braces and retention with retainers, marks the final step in the overall orthognathic treatment plan, ensuring long-term stability.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Orthognathic surgery, including the Le Fort I osteotomy, is performed worldwide to correct a variety of dentofacial deformities. While precise incidence rates vary by region and population, skeletal malocclusions requiring surgical intervention are relatively common, affecting a significant portion of the population, with estimates ranging from 5\% to 15\% of individuals exhibiting a skeletal discrepancy. The typical demographic for Le Fort I osteotomy patients are adolescents and young adults, generally after the cessation of skeletal growth, which usually occurs in the mid-to-late teens (around 16 for females, 18 for males). There is no significant sex predilection for the procedure itself, though specific types of malocclusion may show slight variations. Common indications include Class II malocclusion with maxillary retrusion, Class III malocclusion with maxillary deficiency, and vertical maxillary excess or deficiency. The overall morbidity associated with Le Fort I osteotomy is generally low, with serious complications being rare (e.g., severe hemorrhage <1\%, permanent nerve injury <5\%). Mortality is exceedingly rare, primarily associated with general anesthesia risks, estimated at less than 0.01\%. Minor complications such as temporary nerve paresthesia, swelling, and temporary occlusal instability are more frequent but typically resolve or are manageable.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes of Le Fort I osteotomy are generally excellent, with high success rates for achieving functional and aesthetic improvements. Studies consistently report high patient satisfaction regarding improved facial appearance, chewing ability, speech, and overall quality of life, often exceeding 90\%. The prognosis for long-term stability of the maxillary position is very good, particularly with the use of rigid internal fixation and diligent post-surgical orthodontic management. Relapse rates, defined as a significant return of the maxilla to its original position, are low, typically ranging from 5\% to 15\%, and are often minor, manageable with orthodontic adjustments. Factors influencing prognosis include the magnitude of the surgical movement, the stability of fixation, patient compliance with post-operative instructions (e.g., elastic wear, diet), and the quality of orthodontic finishing. While some degree of sensory alteration can persist, it rarely significantly impacts daily function or patient satisfaction. Overall, the Le Fort I osteotomy offers a predictable and durable solution for correcting complex maxillary skeletal discrepancies, leading to substantial improvements in both oral function and facial aesthetics for appropriately selected patients, as supported by numerous long-term follow-up studies in the orthognathic literature.
