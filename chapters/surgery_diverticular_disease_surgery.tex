\chapter{Diverticular Disease Surgery}

\section*{Introduction \& Clinical Indications}
Diverticular disease surgery encompasses a range of surgical interventions aimed at addressing complications arising from diverticular disease, most commonly through a procedure known as sigmoid colectomy. This surgery is primarily indicated for patients suffering from complicated diverticulitis, which may manifest as abscess formation, perforation, fistula development, or bowel obstruction. The clinical significance of this surgery lies in its ability to alleviate acute symptoms, prevent recurrent episodes of diverticulitis, and restore normal bowel function. The procedure involves the resection of the affected segment of the colon, typically the sigmoid colon, and may include techniques such as primary anastomosis or the creation of a stoma in cases of severe inflammation or contamination. Ultimately, the goal of diverticular disease surgery is to eliminate the diseased tissue and restore the patient's quality of life.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Sigmoid Colectomy
  \item Resection for Diverticulitis
  \item Diverticular Resection
  \item Hartmann's Procedure (for emergent cases)
  \item Laparoscopic Colectomy for Diverticulitis
  \item Open Colectomy for Diverticulitis
  \item Left Hemicolectomy (if disease extends proximally)
\end{itemize}

The primary surgical principle in diverticular disease surgery is the complete excision of the affected segment of the colon, which is typically the sigmoid colon. This excision aims to remove the source of ongoing inflammation, infection, or obstruction, thereby resolving acute complications and preventing future episodes of diverticulitis. The rationale for this approach extends beyond mere excision; it also involves restoring bowel continuity and function while minimizing postoperative morbidity.

Technical goals include achieving a tension-free, well-perfused anastomosis between the remaining healthy bowel segments, ensuring adequate margins of resection to encompass all diseased diverticula, and meticulously controlling intra-abdominal contamination, particularly in emergent situations. In cases of severe inflammation or patient instability, a primary anastomosis may be deemed unsafe, leading to the performance of a Hartmann's procedure, which allows for the resolution of the acute process with a planned reversal of the colostomy at a later date.

The surgical management of diverticular disease has evolved significantly over the past century. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, diverticulitis was first recognized as a distinct clinical entity. Early surgical approaches were often multi-stage procedures, reflecting the high morbidity and mortality associated with direct resection and primary anastomosis in the presence of acute inflammation. Surgeons such as Mikulicz and Paul developed techniques involving exteriorization of the diseased bowel, followed by delayed resection.

The mid-20th century marked a pivotal shift towards single-stage resections with primary anastomosis, largely due to advancements in surgical techniques and the introduction of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Surgeons like O.H. Wangensteen championed more aggressive resections. The advent of laparoscopic surgery in the 1990s further revolutionized the field, offering minimally invasive options that reduced postoperative pain and recovery times. Today, laparoscopic sigmoid colectomy with primary anastomosis is the preferred approach for most elective cases, with robotic assistance also gaining traction.

The clinical indications for diverticular disease surgery include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Complicated diverticulitis with large or persistent abscesses that fail percutaneous drainage.
  \item Free perforation with generalized peritonitis, requiring emergent surgical intervention.
  \item Diverticular fistula formation causing debilitating symptoms.
  \item Bowel obstruction due to chronic inflammation or stricture unresponsive to conservative measures.
  \item Recurrent episodes of acute diverticulitis after two or more severe attacks.
  \item Uncontrolled diverticular bleeding that persists despite endoscopic interventions.
  \item Inability to rule out colorectal malignancy when diverticular mass mimics cancer.
  \item Chronic diverticulitis with persistent abdominal pain refractory to medical management.
\end{itemize}

Diverticular disease surgery is primarily a first-line intervention for patients presenting with acute, complicated diverticulitis. In these cases, surgery is often the most definitive treatment to resolve life-threatening complications. For patients with recurrent, uncomplicated diverticulitis, surgery is typically a secondary intervention pursued after medical management has failed to control symptoms. This approach allows for improved quality of life and prevention of future complications.

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The surgical anatomy relevant to diverticular disease surgery primarily involves the sigmoid colon, which is the most common site for diverticula formation due to its anatomical characteristics. The sigmoid colon is an S-shaped segment of the large intestine, typically measuring 25-40 cm in length, extending from the descending colon at the pelvic brim to the rectum at the level of S3. Its mesentery, known as the sigmoid mesocolon, provides vascular supply and lymphatic drainage while allowing for significant mobility.

Key anatomical structures and relations include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Vascular Supply:} The sigmoid colon receives blood supply primarily from the sigmoid arteries, branches of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA). The IMA also gives rise to the left colic artery and the superior rectal artery. The marginal artery of Drummond is crucial for collateral circulation.
  
  \item \textbf{Venous Drainage:} Venous drainage follows the arterial supply, with sigmoid veins draining into the inferior mesenteric vein (IMV), which typically empties into the splenic vein.
  
  \item \textbf{Lymphatic Drainage:} Lymphatic vessels accompany the arterial supply, draining into lymph nodes along the sigmoid arteries and ultimately to para-aortic nodes.
  
  \item \textbf{Nerve Relations:} The autonomic nervous system, including the superior and inferior hypogastric plexuses, is located in the retroperitoneum and pelvis. Injury to these nerves during surgery can result in bladder or sexual dysfunction.
  
  \item \textbf{Adjacent Structures:} The left ureter lies retroperitoneally and crosses the common iliac artery, often running close to the root of the sigmoid mesentery. Other adjacent structures include the bladder anteriorly and, in females, the uterus and left ovary.
  
  \item \textbf{Surgical Landmarks:} The sacral promontory marks the transition from the descending colon to the sigmoid colon, and the peritoneal reflections around the sigmoid mesocolon are important for mobilization.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Preoperative preparation for diverticular disease surgery is essential for optimizing outcomes. This includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Comprehensive medical history and physical examination.
  \item Routine laboratory tests, including CBC, electrolyte panel, and coagulation studies.
  \item Imaging studies, typically a CT scan, to assess the extent of disease.
  \item Anesthesia consultation to evaluate fitness for surgery.
  \item Mechanical bowel preparation and oral antibiotics for elective cases.
  \item NPO status for a specified period before surgery.
  \item Administration of prophylactic antibiotics prior to incision.
  \item Informed consent detailing the procedure and potential risks.
\end{itemize}

For emergent cases, preparation focuses on rapid resuscitation and immediate surgical intervention.

\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Uncontrolled severe sepsis or septic shock.
  \item Uncorrectable coagulopathy or severe bleeding diathesis.
  \item Severe, uncompensated end-stage organ failure.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications \& Precautions:}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Significant comorbidities, such as severe cardiac disease or uncontrolled diabetes.
  \item Extensive intra-abdominal adhesions from previous surgeries.
  \item Active inflammatory bowel disease coexisting with diverticular disease.
  \item Malnutrition or severe immunosuppression.
  \item Pregnancy, especially in the first and third trimesters.
  \item Morbid obesity, which can complicate laparoscopic access.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Diverticular disease surgery is performed under general anesthesia, with the patient positioned supine. For laparoscopic approaches, the patient may be placed in a steep Trendelenburg position with a left tilt.

The procedure generally involves the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia and Access:} General anesthesia is induced, and monitoring is established. For laparoscopic surgery, small incisions are made for ports; for open surgery, a midline or left lower quadrant incision is made.
  
  \item \textbf{Exploration and Mobilization:} The abdomen is explored to assess the extent of diverticular disease. The left colon is mobilized from its retroperitoneal attachments, ensuring identification and protection of the left ureter.
  
  \item \textbf{Identification of Diseased Segment:} The affected segment of the colon is identified, ensuring the proximal margin is healthy and pliable.
  
  \item \textbf{Vascular Ligation:} The vascular supply to the diseased segment is ligated and divided, preserving the marginal artery of Drummond.
  
  \item \textbf{Resection:} The diseased segment is resected using surgical staplers or hand-sewn techniques, and the specimen is extracted.
  
  \item \textbf{Anastomosis or Stoma Creation:} In elective cases, a primary anastomosis is performed. In emergent cases, a Hartmann's procedure may be necessary.
  
  \item \textbf{Leak Test, Irrigation, and Hemostasis:} A leak test is conducted if an anastomosis is performed, followed by thorough irrigation and hemostasis.
  
  \item \textbf{Closure:} A drain may be placed if there was significant contamination, and incisions are closed in layers.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Complications of diverticular disease surgery can be categorized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Complications:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Bleeding, requiring transfusion or reoperation.
      \item Infection, including surgical site infection or intra-abdominal abscess.
      \item Anesthesia complications, such as respiratory depression or cardiac events.
      \item Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
      \item Ileus, delaying return of bowel function.
    \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Anastomotic leak, leading to peritonitis or sepsis.
      \item Bowel obstruction due to adhesions or stricture.
      \item Injury to adjacent organs, such as the ureters or bladder.
      \item Stoma complications, including prolapse or retraction.
      \item Incisional hernia at the surgical site.
      \item Persistent diverticular symptoms if not all diseased tissue is removed.
      \item Fistula formation between bowel and adjacent organs.
      \item Sexual dysfunction due to nerve injury.
      \item Urinary dysfunction from pelvic nerve injury.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Recovery}
Postoperative recovery following diverticular disease surgery typically involves several stages. Initially, patients are monitored in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for vital signs and pain management. Pain control is prioritized, often utilizing intravenous analgesics transitioning to oral medications.

Within the first 24-48 hours, the patient's diet is advanced from clear liquids to a soft, low-residue diet as bowel function returns. Intravenous fluids are continued until oral intake is adequate. If a drain was placed, its output is monitored, and it is typically removed when drainage is minimal. Hospital discharge usually occurs within 3-7 days, depending on recovery progress.

Long-term recovery involves a gradual return to normal activities over several weeks, with full recovery often taking 4-8 weeks. If a temporary stoma was created, a second surgery for stoma reversal is typically planned 3-6 months later.

During diverticular disease surgery, the surgeon documents intraoperative findings, including the location and extent of diverticula, inflammation, abscesses, and the condition of adjacent organs. The operative report specifies the type of procedure performed and any complications encountered.

The resected colonic segment is sent for pathological examination, which confirms the diagnosis of diverticular disease and assesses the severity of inflammation. The pathology report is crucial for ruling out malignancy and may describe chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and other changes indicative of diverticular disease.

A successful postoperative course following diverticular disease surgery is characterized by a gradual resolution of symptoms and a return to normal bowel function. Patients can expect a decrease in pain, with bowel function typically returning within 2-5 days. The surgical incision should heal cleanly, and if a stoma was created, it should function appropriately. Overall, patients should experience a significant improvement in their quality of life, with a cessation of recurrent diverticulitis attacks and the ability to resume normal activities.

Postoperative care includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Postoperative Clinic Visits:} Scheduled 2-4 weeks after discharge to assess recovery and pathology results.
  \item \textbf{Suture/Staple Removal:} If non-absorbable sutures or staples were used, they are removed at the first follow-up visit.
  \item \textbf{Stoma Care Education:} Provided for patients with a temporary colostomy, discussing stoma management and reversal planning.
  \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} Recommended 6-12 weeks post-surgery to screen for polyps or malignancy.
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Gradual return to normal activities, avoiding heavy lifting for 6-8 weeks.
  \item \textbf{Dietary Advice:} Encouragement of a high-fiber diet for regular bowel habits.
  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients should contact their surgeon if they experience fever, worsening pain, persistent nausea, or signs of infection.
\end{itemize}

These follow-up measures ensure complete recovery and monitor for recurrence or new pathology.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Diverticular disease is prevalent in Western populations, with incidence increasing with age. Approximately 5\% of individuals under 40 are affected, rising to over 50\% in those over 60. While diverticulosis is common, only 10-25\% will develop diverticulitis, and a smaller proportion will require surgery. Emergency surgery for complicated diverticulitis carries higher morbidity and mortality rates compared to elective surgery. There is no significant sex predilection, though some studies suggest a slight male predominance in younger patients.

Outcomes following diverticular disease surgery are generally favorable, particularly for elective procedures. Success rates for elective sigmoid colectomy with primary anastomosis exceed 90-95\%, with significant improvements in quality of life. Recurrence of diverticulitis after resection is rare, estimated at 5-10\% over 5-10 years. Prognostic factors influencing outcomes include age, overall health, and whether surgery is performed electively or emergently. Emergency surgery carries higher risks but remains a life-saving intervention. For patients undergoing a Hartmann's procedure, stoma reversal success rates are high, though it carries its own risks.
