\chapter{Carpal Tunnel Release}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Carpal tunnel release is a surgical procedure designed to relieve the symptoms associated with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), a condition characterized by the compression of the median nerve as it passes through the carpal tunnel at the wrist. This tunnel is a narrow passageway formed by the carpal bones and the transverse carpal ligament, which serves as the roof. Symptoms of CTS include pain, numbness, tingling, and weakness in the hand and fingers, particularly affecting the thumb, index, middle, and radial half of the ring finger. The surgery aims to alleviate these symptoms by surgically dividing the transverse carpal ligament, thereby increasing the space within the tunnel and relieving pressure on the median nerve. This intervention is particularly significant for patients who have not responded to conservative treatments, such as splinting or corticosteroid injections, and for those who exhibit signs of severe nerve compression, which can lead to irreversible damage if left untreated.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Carpal Tunnel Decompression
  \item Median Nerve Decompression
  \item Transverse Carpal Ligament Release
  \item Open Carpal Tunnel Release (OCTR)
  \item Endoscopic Carpal Tunnel Release (ECTR)
  \item Wrist Decompression Surgery for CTS
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The carpal tunnel is a vital anatomical structure located at the wrist, serving as a conduit for the median nerve and the flexor tendons. It is bounded by the carpal bones, which form a concave arch, and the transverse carpal ligament, which forms the roof. The ligament extends from the scaphoid and trapezium bones on the radial side to the pisiform and hook of the hamate on the ulnar side. Within this confined space, the median nerve lies superficially, making it susceptible to compression. 

The nine flexor tendons that traverse the carpal tunnel include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Four tendons of the flexor digitorum superficialis
  \item Four tendons of the flexor digitorum profundus
  \item One tendon of the flexor pollicis longus
\end{itemize}

The median nerve provides motor innervation to the thenar muscles via its recurrent motor branch, which typically arises distal to the transverse carpal ligament. Sensory innervation includes the palmar aspect of the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and the radial half of the ring finger. The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve, which supplies sensation to the proximal palm, usually arises proximal to the carpal tunnel and is spared during surgery. Important surgical landmarks include the thenar crease, the distal wrist crease, and the palpable hook of the hamate, which aids in identifying the extent of the transverse carpal ligament.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The primary surgical principle of carpal tunnel release is to alleviate the compression of the median nerve by enlarging the carpal tunnel. This is accomplished through the division of the transverse carpal ligament, which serves as the rigid roof of the tunnel. In carpal tunnel syndrome, increased pressure within this confined space can lead to ischemia and demyelination of the median nerve, resulting in pain and sensory deficits. 

The rationale for surgical intervention is to provide immediate relief from this extrinsic compression. By transecting the transverse carpal ligament, the pressure on the median nerve is reduced, allowing for improved blood flow and resolution of nerve edema. The gap created by the division of the ligament typically fills with less restrictive scar tissue, maintaining the enlarged tunnel volume. The technical goals of the procedure include the complete and safe division of the transverse carpal ligament while protecting the median nerve and its branches from injury.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The recognition of median nerve compression at the wrist dates back to 1854 when James Paget first described the symptoms associated with distal radius fractures. However, it was not until 1913 that Pierre Marie and Charles Foix accurately characterized carpal tunnel syndrome and its relationship with median nerve compression. 

A significant advancement in the surgical treatment of CTS occurred in the 1950s when George S. Phalen popularized the open surgical technique for dividing the transverse carpal ligament. His contributions established the open approach as the gold standard for treating CTS. The evolution of this technique continued with the introduction of endoscopic carpal tunnel release in the 1980s by Okutsu and others, which provided a less invasive alternative. This method utilizes small incisions and an endoscope for visualization, promising quicker recovery times and reduced postoperative discomfort. Both open and endoscopic techniques have undergone refinements, with ongoing research aimed at improving patient outcomes and minimizing complications.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Carpal tunnel release is indicated for patients with significant median nerve compression symptoms that impact their daily activities or show signs of progressive nerve damage. 

\begin{itemize}
  \item Persistent or worsening symptoms of carpal tunnel syndrome, including numbness, tingling, and pain in the median nerve distribution, particularly if nocturnal.
  \item Failure of conservative management, such as wrist splinting, activity modification, NSAIDs, and corticosteroid injections, after an adequate trial period of 3-6 months.
  \item Objective evidence of median nerve compression on electrodiagnostic studies, confirming the diagnosis and assessing severity.
  \item Presence of thenar muscle weakness or atrophy, indicating severe or long-standing median nerve compression.
  \item Constant or nocturnal pain that disrupts sleep or daily activities, despite non-surgical interventions.
  \item Progressive sensory loss or motor deficits, such as dropping objects or difficulty with fine motor tasks.
  \item Acute carpal tunnel syndrome due to trauma, infection, or hemorrhage, requiring urgent decompression.
  \item Recurrence of symptoms after previous non-surgical treatment or incomplete initial surgical release.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Carpal tunnel release is primarily considered a definitive treatment for carpal tunnel syndrome when conservative measures have failed or when there is evidence of severe nerve compromise. It is generally not the first-line intervention for newly diagnosed, mild cases, where non-surgical treatments are preferred. However, in cases of significant motor weakness, thenar muscle atrophy, or severe sensory deficits, surgical decompression may be warranted to prevent irreversible nerve damage. The decision to proceed with surgery is individualized, taking into account the severity and duration of symptoms, electrodiagnostic findings, and patient preferences.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Carpal tunnel release can be performed as an outpatient procedure under local anesthesia, often with intravenous sedation for patient comfort. The patient's arm is positioned on a hand table, and a tourniquet is applied to enhance visualization.

For the \textbf{Open Carpal Tunnel Release (OCTR)}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia \& Positioning:} Local anesthetic is infiltrated into the palm, and the arm is supinated on a hand table.
  \item \textbf{Incision:} A longitudinal incision, typically 2-4 cm, is made along the ulnar border of the thenar crease.
  \item \textbf{Dissection:} The incision is deepened through the skin and subcutaneous tissue, protecting superficial nerves and vessels.
  \item \textbf{Ligament Identification:} The palmar fascia is incised to expose the transverse carpal ligament.
  \item \textbf{Ligament Division:} The ligament is divided longitudinally from its distal to proximal attachment, ensuring complete release while protecting the median nerve.
  \item \textbf{Confirmation:} Adequate decompression of the median nerve is visually confirmed.
  \item \textbf{Closure:} The skin incision is closed with non-absorbable sutures or staples, and a sterile dressing is applied.
\end{itemize}

For the \textbf{Endoscopic Carpal Tunnel Release (ECTR)}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia \& Positioning:} Similar to OCTR, local anesthesia with sedation is used.
  \item \textbf{Incision(s):} One or two small incisions (less than 1 cm) are made, typically at the distal wrist crease or proximal palm.
  \item \textbf{Endoscope Insertion:} An endoscope is inserted through the portal for visualization.
  \item \textbf{Instrument Insertion:} A specialized cutting instrument is introduced under endoscopic guidance.
  \item \textbf{Ligament Division:} The transverse carpal ligament is divided while protecting the median nerve.
  \item \textbf{Confirmation:} Complete division of the ligament is confirmed through visualization.
  \item \textbf{Closure:} The small incisions are closed with sutures or adhesive strips, and a sterile dressing is applied.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A thorough preoperative workup is essential to confirm the diagnosis and assess the patient's overall health.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diagnosis Confirmation:} Electrodiagnostic studies (NCS and EMG) are performed to confirm carpal tunnel syndrome and assess severity.
  \item \textbf{Medical Clearance:} Patients with significant comorbidities may require medical clearance from their primary care physician or specialists.
  \item \textbf{Medication Review:} Patients are instructed to discontinue blood-thinning medications prior to surgery.
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients must adhere to NPO guidelines if sedation or general anesthesia is planned.
  \item \textbf{Prophylactic Antibiotics:} Intravenous antibiotics are administered within 60 minutes prior to incision.
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} A detailed discussion regarding the procedure, risks, benefits, and expected outcomes is conducted to obtain informed consent.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Active infection in the surgical field or systemic sepsis.
  \item Uncontrolled bleeding disorder or inability to safely discontinue anticoagulants.
  \item Patient refusal or inability to provide informed consent.
  \item Severe, uncorrectable medical comorbidities that pose excessive surgical risks.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications and Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Mild symptoms responsive to conservative management.
  \item Coexisting peripheral neuropathy that complicates diagnosis.
  \item Prior surgery in the same carpal tunnel leading to altered anatomy.
  \item Significant anatomical variations or space-occupying lesions.
  \item Severe thenar muscle atrophy with limited potential for recovery.
  \item Pregnancy, where surgery is often deferred.
  \item Inexperience of the surgeon with endoscopic techniques.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
While carpal tunnel release is generally safe, potential risks and complications include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding: Minor bruising is common; significant hemorrhage is rare.
    \item Infection: Superficial or deep infections can occur.
    \item Anesthesia complications: Reactions to anesthetics or sedation.
    \item Scarring: Visible scars may form postoperatively.
  \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Nerve injury: Risk of median nerve or its branches being damaged.
    \item Incomplete release: Persistent symptoms due to inadequate ligament division.
    \item Pillar pain: Pain in the thenar and hypothenar eminences post-surgery.
    \item Reflex Sympathetic Dystrophy (RSD) / Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS): Rare chronic pain condition.
    \item Tendon injury: Accidental damage to flexor tendons.
    \item Vascular injury: Damage to small vessels leading to hematoma.
    \item Recurrence of symptoms: Symptoms may return years after surgery.
    \item Stiffness: Temporary stiffness of fingers or wrist.
    \item Bowstringing of flexor tendons: Rare complication affecting tendon function.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Following carpal tunnel release, patients are monitored in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) before being discharged home. The first 24-48 hours involve keeping the hand elevated to minimize swelling and managing pain with prescribed medications. Patients are encouraged to gently move their fingers to prevent stiffness.

Within the first 1-2 weeks, sutures are typically removed, and patients are advised to begin light hand exercises to restore range of motion. Many experience relief from nocturnal pain and paresthesias within days. Long-term recovery involves a gradual return to full activity over several weeks to months, with continued improvement in strength and sensation. Physical or occupational therapy may be recommended for those with significant preoperative deficits. Full recovery can take up to a year, particularly in cases with severe preoperative nerve damage.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During carpal tunnel release, the surgeon typically observes a thickened and taut transverse carpal ligament compressing the median nerve. The nerve may appear flattened or edematous, indicating chronic compression. Upon division of the ligament, the median nerve often expands, confirming successful decompression. The flexor tendons may show signs of tenosynovitis. While routine cases do not involve sending tissue for pathological examination, any incidental findings, such as ganglion cysts, may warrant biopsy or excision.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course is characterized by progressive improvement in symptoms and hand function. Patients usually experience prompt relief of nocturnal pain and paresthesias within days to weeks. Incisional pain and pillar pain may occur but should improve over time. Patients can typically resume light daily activities within 1-2 weeks and gradually return to more strenuous activities over 6-12 weeks. The goal is to restore functional use of the hand with minimal residual symptoms. Complete resolution of symptoms may take several months, with continuous improvement expected.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative care is essential for optimal recovery following carpal tunnel release.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wound Care:} The initial dressing is removed within 24-48 hours, and patients are instructed to keep the wound clean and dry.
  \item \textbf{Suture Removal:} Follow-up visits occur 10-14 days post-surgery for suture removal.
  \item \textbf{Clinical Reassessment:} Subsequent visits assess symptom resolution and hand function.
  \item \textbf{Physical/Occupational Therapy:} Referral to a hand therapist may be made for patients with significant deficits.
  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Patients are gradually cleared to resume normal activities, with guidance on avoiding heavy lifting and repetitive tasks.
  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients should contact their surgeon if they experience signs of infection, severe pain, or new neurological symptoms.
\end{itemize}

Repeat electrodiagnostic studies are generally not performed unless symptoms persist or recur.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common peripheral nerve entrapment neuropathy, affecting a significant portion of the adult population. Its estimated incidence ranges from 1 to 3 cases per 1,000 persons annually, with prevalence rates between 3\% and 6\%. Women are more frequently affected than men, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 3:1 to 5:1. The peak incidence typically occurs between 40 and 60 years of age, although it can manifest at any age. Common indications for surgical intervention include idiopathic CTS, often exacerbated by repetitive hand movements, and CTS associated with systemic conditions such as diabetes, hypothyroidism, and pregnancy. Carpal tunnel release is consistently among the most frequently performed hand surgeries globally, with low mortality and morbidity rates.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
Carpal tunnel release is recognized as a highly effective surgical intervention, with success rates for significant symptom relief ranging from 90\% to 95\%. Most patients experience substantial improvement in pain, numbness, and tingling within days to weeks postoperatively. Functional outcomes, including grip and pinch strength, typically improve progressively over several months. The long-term prognosis is generally excellent, with a low recurrence rate of 1-5\% over 5-10 years. Prognostic factors influencing outcomes include the severity and duration of preoperative symptoms, with milder symptoms correlating with faster recovery. Co-morbidities such as diabetes may also affect recovery rates. Overall, carpal tunnel release significantly enhances quality of life and functional independence for affected individuals.
