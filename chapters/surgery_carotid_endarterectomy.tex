\chapter{Carotid Endarterectomy}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) is a surgical procedure designed to reduce the risk of stroke by correcting stenosis (narrowing) in the common carotid artery. This narrowing is often caused by atherosclerosis, a condition characterized by the buildup of fatty deposits, cholesterol, and other substances in the arterial wall. The surgery is typically performed at the carotid bifurcation, where the common carotid artery divides into the internal and external carotid arteries. By removing the atherosclerotic plaque, CEA aims to restore normal blood flow to the brain, thereby preventing the occurrence of transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) and ischemic strokes. This procedure is particularly significant for patients who have experienced symptoms related to carotid artery disease, as it has been shown to reduce the incidence of future cerebrovascular events.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item CEA
  \item Carotid artery surgery
  \item Carotid plaque removal
  \item Carotid thromboendarterectomy
  \item Open carotid surgery
  \item Carotid revascularization
  \item Carotid artery endarterectomy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The carotid arteries are essential components of the cerebral circulation, originating from the brachiocephalic artery on the right and directly from the aortic arch on the left. The common carotid artery (CCA) ascends in the neck, bifurcating at the level of the C3-C4 vertebral bodies into the internal carotid artery (ICA) and the external carotid artery (ECA). The carotid bulb, located at the bifurcation, is a common site for atherosclerotic plaque formation and contains baroreceptors that help regulate blood pressure.

The ICA supplies blood to the anterior and middle cerebral arteries, while the ECA supplies the face and neck. Key anatomical structures to be aware of during surgery include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The sternocleidomastoid muscle, which is retracted to expose the carotid sheath.
  \item The internal jugular vein, which lies lateral to the common carotid artery.
  \item The vagus nerve, located posterior and medial to the common carotid artery.
  \item The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII), which crosses the ICA anteriorly.
  \item The recurrent laryngeal nerve, which is particularly important to identify and protect during dissection.
\end{itemize}

Understanding these anatomical relationships is crucial for minimizing complications during the procedure.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The primary surgical principle of carotid endarterectomy is the mechanical removal of atherosclerotic plaque from the carotid artery to restore normal blood flow to the brain. Atherosclerosis leads to the formation of fibrofatty plaques that narrow the arterial lumen, increasing the risk of embolization and subsequent ischemic events. The rationale for performing CEA is to eliminate the source of emboli and to widen the arterial lumen, thereby improving cerebral perfusion.

During the procedure, a longitudinal incision is made in the carotid artery, allowing for the careful dissection and removal of plaque from the arterial wall. This process aims to create a smooth, unobstructed lumen. To prevent restenosis, the arteriotomy is typically closed with a patch, which can be made from synthetic materials or autologous tissue, enhancing the long-term success of the procedure.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The surgical intervention for carotid artery disease began to take shape in the mid-20th century. The first successful carotid endarterectomy was performed by Dr. Michael DeBakey in 1953, marking a pivotal moment in vascular surgery. This initial procedure laid the groundwork for future advancements in the field.

In the following decades, techniques evolved significantly. The introduction of intraluminal shunts, pioneered by surgeons such as J. Gordon McMurrich, allowed for continuous cerebral perfusion during clamping, reducing the risk of intraoperative stroke. The adoption of patch angioplasty, advocated by Dr. E. Stanley Crawford, further improved outcomes by decreasing the incidence of restenosis.

The efficacy of CEA was established through landmark trials in the 1990s, including the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET) and the European Carotid Surgery Trial (ECST). These studies provided robust evidence supporting the use of CEA in both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients with significant carotid stenosis, solidifying its role as a standard treatment option.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Carotid endarterectomy is indicated for patients with significant carotid artery stenosis who are at increased risk of ischemic stroke. The decision for surgery is based on the degree of stenosis, the presence of symptoms, and the patient's overall surgical risk.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptomatic carotid artery stenosis of 50-99\% in patients who have experienced a transient ischemic attack (TIA) or minor ischemic stroke attributable to the ipsilateral stenotic lesion.
  \item Amaurosis fugax (temporary monocular blindness) caused by ipsilateral carotid stenosis, representing a form of TIA.
  \item Asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis of 70-99\% in patients with a life expectancy of at least 5 years and low surgical risk, particularly when performed by experienced surgeons with documented low complication rates.
  \item Progressive neurological deficits or recurrent TIAs despite optimal medical therapy in patients with moderate to severe stenosis.
  \item Carotid artery stenosis identified as the likely source of an embolic stroke of undetermined source (cryptogenic stroke) after thorough diagnostic workup.
  \item Patients with carotid artery stenosis undergoing other major surgical procedures, such as coronary artery bypass grafting, where the combined risk of perioperative stroke is deemed high enough to warrant prophylactic CEA.
  \item Carotid artery stenosis with features suggestive of high-risk plaque morphology (e.g., ulcerated plaque, intraplaque hemorrhage) even at lower degrees of stenosis, though this remains a subject of ongoing research.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Carotid endarterectomy is primarily positioned as a first-line surgical intervention for the prevention of ischemic stroke in appropriately selected patients with symptomatic, high-grade carotid artery stenosis. It is often the preferred revascularization strategy for patients at standard surgical risk with favorable anatomical characteristics.

In cases where patients present with anatomical challenges, such as a very high carotid bifurcation or significant scarring from prior surgeries, carotid artery stenting (CAS) may be considered as a primary alternative. Additionally, in patients with significant medical comorbidities that elevate the risk of general anesthesia and open surgery, CAS may be favored. In rare instances where CAS fails or results in incomplete revascularization, CEA may be considered as a secondary, salvage procedure. The choice between CEA and CAS requires careful consideration of patient characteristics, plaque morphology, and institutional expertise.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Carotid endarterectomy is typically performed under general anesthesia, although regional anesthesia may be utilized in select cases. The patient is positioned supine with the head turned approximately 45 degrees to the contralateral side to optimize exposure of the neck.

The procedure involves the following key steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia and Patient Positioning:} General endotracheal anesthesia is induced. The patient is positioned supine with the head extended and turned to the side opposite the operative carotid artery. The neck and upper chest are prepped and draped in a sterile fashion.
  
  \item \textbf{Incision and Exposure:} An oblique incision is made along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, typically extending from just below the mastoid process to the level of the cricoid cartilage. The platysma muscle is divided, and the sternocleidomastoid muscle is retracted laterally to expose the common carotid artery and its bifurcation.

  \item \textbf{Systemic Anticoagulation:} Once the carotid arteries are exposed, systemic heparin is administered to achieve an activated clotting time (ACT) typically greater than 250 seconds.

  \item \textbf{Arterial Clamping and Shunting:} The common, internal, and external carotid arteries are clamped in sequence. An intraluminal shunt is often placed to maintain cerebral perfusion during the endarterectomy.

  \item \textbf{Arteriotomy and Plaque Removal:} A longitudinal incision is made in the common carotid artery, extending across the bifurcation into the internal carotid artery. The atheromatous plaque is meticulously dissected and removed.

  \item \textbf{Patch Angioplasty:} After plaque removal, the arteriotomy is closed using a patch of synthetic material or autologous vein to reduce the risk of restenosis.

  \item \textbf{Declamping and Hemostasis:} The clamps are removed in a specific sequence to flush any air or debris. Meticulous hemostasis is achieved.

  \item \textbf{Closure:} A suction drain may be placed, and the incision is closed in layers with sutures or staples.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A thorough preoperative workup is essential for optimizing patient outcomes and minimizing surgical risks. This includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Comprehensive Medical Evaluation:} Detailed medical history and physical examination focusing on cardiovascular risk factors and neurological status.

  \item \textbf{Laboratory Tests:} Routine blood tests, including CBC, BMP, liver function tests, coagulation profile, and type and screen.

  \item \textbf{Cardiac Clearance:} An ECG is standard, with further cardiac evaluation as needed for patients with significant cardiac history.

  \item \textbf{Imaging Studies:} Carotid duplex ultrasound is the primary diagnostic tool, supplemented by CTA or MRA for detailed anatomical assessment.

  \item \textbf{Medication Management:} Continuation of antiplatelet medications and optimization of other medications, such as antihypertensives and statins.

  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients are instructed to be NPO for at least 6-8 hours prior to surgery.

  \item \textbf{Prophylactic Antibiotics:} Intravenous antibiotics are administered within 60 minutes prior to incision.

  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} A detailed discussion regarding the procedure, risks, and alternatives is conducted, and informed consent is obtained.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Complete occlusion of the internal carotid artery with no distal flow.
  \item Severe irreversible neurological deficit from a completed stroke.
  \item Severe uncorrectable comorbidities that confer high surgical risk.
  \item Acute intracranial hemorrhage or other acute intracranial pathology.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications and Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Prior neck radiation therapy or previous neck surgery.
  \item Recurrent carotid stenosis after a previous endarterectomy.
  \item Contralateral carotid artery occlusion.
  \item Tandem lesions in both carotid and intracranial arteries.
  \item Unstable angina or recent myocardial infarction.
  \item Severe chronic kidney disease or other systemic conditions.
  \item Very high or low carotid bifurcation.
  \item Significant calcification of the carotid artery.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Carotid endarterectomy carries potential risks, which can be categorized into general surgical complications and procedure-specific complications.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Complications:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textit{Bleeding/Hematoma:} Accumulation of blood in the surgical site.
      \item \textit{Wound Infection:} Infection of the surgical incision.
      \item \textit{Anesthesia Complications:} Risks associated with general anesthesia.
      \item \textit{DVT and PE:} Risk of clot formation in leg veins.
    \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Procedure-Specific Complications:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textit{Stroke:} Occurring in 2-3\% of symptomatic patients.
      \item \textit{Myocardial Infarction (MI):} Susceptibility due to underlying cardiovascular disease.
      \item \textit{Cranial Nerve Injury:} Damage to nerves in the neck.
      \item \textit{Hyperperfusion Syndrome:} Rare but serious complication.
      \item \textit{Restenosis:} Re-narrowing of the carotid artery.
      \item \textit{Pseudoaneurysm:} Formation at the arteriotomy site.
      \item \textit{Carotid Body Dysfunction:} Blood pressure instability.
      \item \textit{Cerebral Edema:} Swelling of the brain post-reperfusion.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Following carotid endarterectomy, patients are closely monitored in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) or intensive care unit (ICU). Neurological assessments are performed frequently to detect any signs of stroke or nerve injury. Blood pressure is closely monitored and maintained within a specified range to prevent complications.

Within the first 24-48 hours, if the patient remains stable, they are typically transferred to a step-down unit or surgical ward. Early ambulation is encouraged, and a soft diet is usually initiated once the patient is fully awake. Most patients are discharged home within 1-3 days post-surgery, with detailed instructions on wound care and activity restrictions. Full recovery typically takes 4-6 weeks, with gradual improvement in energy levels and resolution of neck discomfort.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During carotid endarterectomy, surgeons document key findings, including the location and extent of atherosclerotic plaque, its morphology, and the degree of luminal stenosis. The quality of the arterial wall and the presence of any dissection planes are also noted. The resected plaque specimen is sent for histopathological examination, confirming the diagnosis of atherosclerosis and assessing plaque composition. The pathology report provides crucial information regarding the nature of the disease and helps correlate with the patient's clinical presentation.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course following carotid endarterectomy is characterized by resolution of pre-operative symptoms and stable neurological status. Patients may experience some neck discomfort, which typically resolves with analgesics. Blood pressure should be well-controlled, and patients are expected to gradually return to normal activities within a few weeks. The surgical incision should heal cleanly, without signs of infection or excessive scarring.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Structured postoperative care is essential for monitoring complications and managing cardiovascular risk factors. 

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Post-operative Visits:} Follow-up visits are scheduled within 1-2 weeks for wound check and assessment of recovery.

  \item \textbf{Carotid Duplex Ultrasound Surveillance:} Performed at 1 month, 6 months, and annually to monitor for restenosis.

  \item \textbf{Medication Management:} Lifelong antiplatelet therapy and optimization of cardiovascular risk factors are critical.

  \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} Physical, occupational, or speech therapy may be recommended for patients with deficits.

  \item \textbf{Activity Resumption:} Patients receive specific instructions on gradual return to normal activities.

  \item \textbf{Warning Signs:} Patients are educated on warning signs of complications and instructed to seek immediate medical attention if they occur.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Epidemiology}
Carotid artery stenosis is a common manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis, particularly affecting older adults. Its prevalence increases with age, affecting approximately 5-10\% of individuals over 65 years old. Men exhibit a higher incidence than women, especially at younger ages. In the United States, approximately 100,000 CEAs are performed annually, with symptomatic stenosis being the primary indication. Perioperative mortality and stroke rates have declined significantly, with current rates in experienced centers typically below 3\% for symptomatic patients.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes of carotid endarterectomy are generally excellent, particularly in high-volume centers. For symptomatic patients with high-grade stenosis, CEA significantly reduces the risk of ipsilateral stroke. Functional outcomes are typically very good, with most patients returning to their baseline neurological status. Restenosis occurs in 5-15\% of patients within 5 years, with symptomatic restenosis being less common. Prognostic factors influencing outcomes include age, comorbidities, and plaque morphology. Overall, CEA remains a highly effective intervention for stroke prevention in appropriately selected patients.
