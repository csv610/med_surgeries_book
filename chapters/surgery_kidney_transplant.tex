\chapter{Kidney Transplant}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Kidney transplantation is a surgical procedure that involves the implantation of a healthy kidney from a living or deceased donor into a recipient suffering from end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). This surgery aims to restore the essential functions of the kidneys, which include the filtration of waste products, regulation of fluid and electrolyte balance, and production of hormones such as erythropoietin. The donor kidney is typically placed in the iliac fossa, where it is connected to the recipient's blood vessels and urinary system. This procedure is significant as it not only improves the quality of life for patients but also extends their survival compared to those who remain on dialysis. Kidney transplantation is considered the gold standard treatment for ESKD, offering a chance for a more normal lifestyle and reducing the burden of chronic dialysis.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Renal Transplant
  \item Renal Transplantation
  \item Kidney Graft Surgery
  \item Allogeneic Kidney Transplant
  \item Kidney Replacement Surgery
  \item Cadaveric Kidney Transplant (for deceased donor)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
The surgical site for kidney transplantation is primarily located in the retroperitoneal space of the iliac fossa, either on the right or left side. This area is chosen for its accessibility to major blood vessels and the urinary bladder, which facilitates the surgical connections required for the transplant. 

Key anatomical structures include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{External Iliac Artery and Vein:} These vessels are the primary recipients for the donor renal artery and vein, providing robust blood flow to the transplanted kidney.
  \item \textbf{Urinary Bladder:} The bladder serves as the connection point for the donor ureter, which is anastomosed to the bladder wall.
  \item \textbf{Psoas Major Muscle:} This muscle lies posterior to the iliac vessels and provides a soft bed for the transplanted kidney.
  \item \textbf{Lymphatic Vessels:} Careful dissection and ligation of lymphatics in the iliac fossa are crucial to prevent postoperative complications such as lymphocele formation.
\end{itemize}

The native kidneys are typically left in situ unless specific indications for nephrectomy arise, such as uncontrolled hypertension or recurrent infections. The peritoneum is reflected medially to ensure the transplanted kidney is extraperitoneal, minimizing the risk of intra-abdominal complications.

\section*{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The surgical principle of kidney transplantation revolves around the heterotopic placement of a functional renal allograft to replace the excretory and endocrine functions of the failed native kidneys. The rationale is to restore physiological homeostasis by providing effective glomerular filtration and tubular reabsorption, thereby alleviating the uremic state that characterizes ESKD. 

The donor kidney is positioned in the iliac fossa, which offers several advantages: it is extraperitoneal, reducing the risk of bowel injury; it provides direct access to large recipient vessels, facilitating vascular anastomoses; and it allows for a relatively short, tension-free ureteral anastomosis to the bladder. 

The technical goals of the surgery include establishing robust vascular anastomoses to ensure immediate blood flow to the graft and creating a watertight ureteroneocystostomy to facilitate urine drainage. Successful transplantation aims for rapid graft reperfusion, leading to immediate diuresis and a decline in serum creatinine levels, indicating restored renal function. Lifelong immunosuppression is essential to prevent graft rejection, as the recipient's immune system may recognize the donor kidney as foreign.

\section*{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The history of kidney transplantation is marked by significant milestones and advancements. Early attempts at organ transplantation in the early 20th century, notably by Alexis Carrel, laid the groundwork for surgical techniques but faced challenges due to immunological barriers. A pivotal moment occurred on December 23, 1954, when Dr. Joseph Murray and his team performed the first successful human kidney transplant between identical twins, Richard and Ronald Herrick, at Peter Bent Brigham Hospital in Boston. This landmark procedure circumvented the issue of immune rejection due to the genetic similarity of the donors.

The evolution of kidney transplantation accelerated in the 1960s with the introduction of immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine and corticosteroids, which allowed for transplantation between non-identical donors. However, these early regimens had significant side effects and limited success rates. The landscape changed dramatically in the early 1980s with the advent of cyclosporine, a calcineurin inhibitor that significantly improved graft survival rates by specifically targeting T-cell activation. This breakthrough transformed kidney transplantation into a routine and successful treatment for ESKD, paving the way for further advancements in immunosuppression and surgical techniques.

\section*{Clinical Indications}
Kidney transplantation is indicated for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) who meet specific medical and psychosocial criteria. The primary goal is to restore renal function and improve quality of life. 

Clinical indications include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) due to diabetes mellitus, hypertension, glomerulonephritis, or polycystic kidney disease.
  \item Patients currently on chronic dialysis (hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis) who would benefit from improved survival and quality of life.
  \item Pre-emptive transplantation for patients with progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD) approaching ESKD.
  \item Pediatric patients with kidney failure, where transplantation is essential for normal growth and development.
  \item Severe uremic symptoms refractory to dialysis, such as intractable pruritus or uremic pericarditis.
  \item Specific metabolic disorders affecting the kidneys, such as primary hyperoxaluria.
  \item Cost-effective long-term treatment compared to lifelong dialysis.
  \item Certain forms of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) or C3 glomerulopathy requiring kidney replacement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Kidney transplantation is recognized as the primary and definitive treatment for most patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). It is supported by extensive evidence demonstrating superior survival and quality of life outcomes compared to chronic dialysis. For eligible patients, particularly those without significant comorbidities, pre-emptive transplantation—performed before the initiation of dialysis—represents the optimal approach. This strategy avoids the complications and lifestyle burdens associated with dialysis.

In certain cases, kidney transplantation may serve as a secondary or salvage procedure for patients with a failed previous transplant or those who have been on dialysis for an extended period. While dialysis is a critical bridge therapy, transplantation offers the potential for a return to a more normal physiological state and significantly improved prognosis, making it the ultimate therapeutic goal for most ESKD patients.

\section*{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Kidney transplantation is a complex surgical procedure typically performed under general anesthesia and lasting between 3 to 5 hours. The patient is positioned supine, and the surgical field is prepped and draped in a sterile manner.

The surgical procedure involves the following steps:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia and Incision:} General anesthesia is induced, and a curvilinear incision, often referred to as a "hockey stick" or Gibson incision, is made in the lower quadrant of the abdomen to access the retroperitoneal space.
  
  \item \textbf{Vascular Dissection:} The abdominal wall is carefully dissected, and the peritoneum is reflected to expose the external iliac artery and vein. Lymphatic vessels are ligated to minimize the risk of lymphocele formation.
  
  \item \textbf{Kidney Placement and Vascular Anastomoses:} The donor kidney, preserved in a cold storage solution, is placed in the iliac fossa. The donor renal vein is anastomosed end-to-side to the recipient's external iliac vein, followed by the anastomosis of the donor renal artery to the external iliac artery.
  
  \item \textbf{Reperfusion and Assessment:} After completing the vascular anastomoses, clamps are removed to restore blood flow to the graft. The kidney is assessed for immediate reperfusion, indicated by color change and urine production.
  
  \item \textbf{Ureteral Anastomosis (Ureteroneocystostomy):} A small incision is made in the dome of the bladder, and the donor ureter is anastomosed to the bladder wall using techniques that prevent reflux. A ureteral stent may be placed temporarily.
  
  \item \textbf{Closure:} After confirming hemostasis and satisfactory graft perfusion, a surgical drain may be placed, and the abdominal wall is closed in layers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
A comprehensive preoperative workup is essential for optimizing recipient selection and minimizing surgical risks. This evaluation includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Medical Evaluation:} Assessment of cardiac and pulmonary function, gastrointestinal health, and dental clearance to rule out significant comorbidities.
  
  \item \textbf{Infectious Disease Screening:} Screening for active and latent infections, ensuring any active infections are treated before transplantation.
  
  \item \textbf{Immunological Workup:} Blood group compatibility, HLA typing, and crossmatch testing to assess immunological match and prevent hyperacute rejection.
  
  \item \textbf{Psychosocial Evaluation:} Assessment of social support systems and the recipient's understanding of the lifelong commitment to immunosuppression.
  
  \item \textbf{Cancer Screening:} Age-appropriate cancer screenings to ensure no active malignancies exist prior to transplantation.
  
  \item \textbf{Medication Adjustments:} Instructions on which medications to hold or continue before surgery, along with dialysis to optimize fluid balance.
  
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients must be nil per os (NPO) for a specified period before surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Pre-operative Antibiotics:} Administration of prophylactic antibiotics to reduce the risk of surgical site infections.
  
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} Detailed discussions regarding the procedure, its risks, benefits, and the implications of lifelong immunosuppression.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
Identifying contraindications and precautions is vital for patient safety and graft longevity.

\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Active, uncontrolled systemic infection or sepsis.
  \item Active malignancy with a high risk of recurrence.
  \item Severe, irreversible cardiac disease.
  \item Active substance abuse or severe psychiatric illness.
  \item Untreated severe peripheral vascular disease.
  \item Documented non-adherence to medical therapy.
  \item Irreversible brain damage incompatible with quality of life.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications \& Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Advanced age (typically >70-75 years).
  \item Morbid obesity (BMI >35-40 kg/m\textasciicircum{}2).
  \item Significant vascular calcification of iliac vessels.
  \item Certain autoimmune diseases with a high risk of recurrence.
  \item Significant pelvic anatomical abnormalities.
  \item Active peptic ulcer disease or diverticulitis.
  \item HIV infection, requiring specialized management.
  \item Chronic liver disease or significant organ dysfunction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
Kidney transplantation carries potential risks, both immediate and long-term, related to the surgery and immunosuppression.

\textbf{General Surgical Complications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhage.
  \item \textbf{Infection:} Surgical site infections or deep space infections.
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications:} Adverse reactions to anesthetics or respiratory complications.
  \item \textbf{Thromboembolic Events:} DVT and pulmonary embolism.
  \item \textbf{Nerve Injury:} Damage to peripheral nerves during dissection.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Graft Thrombosis:} Acute clotting of the renal artery or vein.
  \item \textbf{Delayed Graft Function (DGF):} Temporary loss of graft function requiring dialysis.
  \item \textbf{Ureteral Complications:} Urine leak or stricture at the ureteral anastomosis.
  \item \textbf{Lymphocele:} Collection of lymphatic fluid around the graft.
  \item \textbf{Renal Artery Stenosis:} Narrowing of the renal artery anastomosis.
  \item \textbf{Graft Rejection:} Immune response against the donor kidney.
  \item \textbf{Graft Rupture:} Catastrophic complication associated with acute rejection.
  \item \textbf{Recurrence of Native Disease:} Original kidney disease recurrence in the transplanted kidney.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Immunosuppression-Related Risks (Long-Term):}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Increased Susceptibility to Infections:} Opportunistic infections due to immunosuppression.
  \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Disease:} Accelerated atherosclerosis and hypertension.
  \item \textbf{Malignancy:} Increased risk of certain cancers post-transplant.
  \item \textbf{Bone Disease:} Osteoporosis and avascular necrosis.
  \item \textbf{Nephrotoxicity:} Toxicity from certain immunosuppressants.
  \item \textbf{Other Side Effects:} Gastrointestinal upset, mood changes, and electrolyte disturbances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
The postoperative recovery timeline for kidney transplant recipients is structured and involves several phases. 

Immediately after surgery, patients are transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for close monitoring of vital signs, urine output, and pain management. Once stable, they are typically moved to a specialized transplant ward or ICU for the first 24-48 hours. During this period, continuous monitoring of urine output and serum creatinine is essential to assess graft function. Intravenous fluids are carefully managed to maintain hydration, and immunosuppressive medications are initiated to prevent acute rejection.

In the first 1-2 weeks post-transplant, the focus shifts to optimizing immunosuppressive drug levels, managing pain, and preventing infections. Patients are educated about their medication regimen and potential complications. Dietary restrictions are gradually lifted, and physical therapy is encouraged to promote mobility. Most patients can return to light activities within 4-6 weeks and resume normal activities within 2-3 months, although heavy lifting is restricted for several months. Full recovery and adaptation to the new lifestyle typically take 6-12 months.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During kidney transplantation, surgeons document several key intraoperative findings, including the condition of the recipient's iliac vessels, the appearance of the donor kidney upon reperfusion, and the quality of the vascular anastomoses. Any anatomical variations or unexpected findings are noted.

Postoperatively, pathology reports on resected tissues, such as native kidneys, provide definitive diagnoses of underlying kidney diseases. If graft dysfunction occurs, a percutaneous kidney biopsy may be performed, with the pathology report guiding subsequent therapeutic interventions. This report details histological findings, such as inflammatory infiltrates or vascular changes, which are crucial for diagnosing rejection or other complications.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A successful postoperative course following kidney transplantation is characterized by a progressive return to health. Patients typically experience a significant reduction in uremic symptoms within days to weeks. Pain at the incision site is managed effectively, and the most important indicator of success is the improvement in kidney function, reflected by decreasing serum creatinine levels.

Patients should experience robust urine output, and as kidney function normalizes, fluid and electrolyte imbalances resolve. Energy levels improve, and patients can gradually resume normal daily activities. The surgical wound heals without complications, and any temporary ureteral stent is removed. A successful course means the transplanted kidney effectively sustains life, allowing the recipient to return to a more active and fulfilling life while adhering to their lifelong immunosuppression regimen.

\section*{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Lifelong postoperative care and follow-up are essential after kidney transplantation to monitor graft function and manage immunosuppression. 

Key components of postoperative care include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Frequent Clinic Visits:} Regular follow-up visits initially weekly or bi-weekly, transitioning to monthly and then quarterly as the patient stabilizes.
  
  \item \textbf{Laboratory Tests:} Routine blood tests to monitor serum creatinine, electrolytes, and immunosuppressant levels.
  
  \item \textbf{Urine Tests:} Regular urinalysis to screen for proteinuria or signs of infection.
  
  \item \textbf{Imaging Studies:} Periodic Doppler ultrasound to assess graft blood flow and monitor for complications.
  
  \item \textbf{Kidney Biopsy:} Performed if there are signs of graft dysfunction to diagnose rejection or other pathologies.
  
  \item \textbf{Blood Pressure Monitoring:} Close monitoring and management of hypertension.
  
  \item \textbf{Diabetes Screening:} Regular screening for new-onset diabetes after transplant.
  
  \item \textbf{Infection Surveillance:} Monitoring for opportunistic infections and providing appropriate vaccinations.
  
  \item \textbf{Cancer Screening:} Enhanced screening for malignancies due to increased risk with immunosuppression.
  
  \item \textbf{Bone Health Monitoring:} Assessment for osteoporosis and management as needed.
  
  \item \textbf{Medication Adherence Counseling:} Ongoing education to ensure strict adherence to immunosuppressive therapy.
  
  \item \textbf{Suture/Staple Removal:} Typically occurs 10-14 days post-surgery.
  
  \item \textbf{Rehabilitation/Physical Therapy:} Encouraged for gradual return to activity.
\end{itemize}

Patients are instructed to contact their transplant team for any concerning symptoms, such as fever, decreased urine output, or abdominal pain.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Kidney transplantation is the most frequently performed solid organ transplant worldwide, reflecting the high prevalence of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). In the United States, over 25,000 kidney transplants are performed annually, yet more than 100,000 individuals remain on the waiting list, highlighting the disparity between demand and donor availability. The incidence of ESKD is rising, primarily due to increasing rates of chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension. ESKD affects all age groups, with the highest incidence in individuals over 60 years. There is a slight male predominance among kidney transplant recipients. The mortality rate for patients on the transplant waiting list is significant, underscoring the critical nature of transplantation. Post-transplant, patient and graft survival rates have improved over the decades, although long-term morbidity remains a concern due to the lifelong need for immunosuppression and its associated complications.

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes of kidney transplantation have significantly improved, establishing it as the most successful form of organ replacement therapy. For deceased donor transplants, 1-year graft survival rates typically range from 90-95\%, with 5-year rates around 75-85\%. Living donor transplants often yield even better results, with 1-year graft survival rates exceeding 95\% and 5-year rates above 85-90\%. Patient survival rates are also significantly higher for transplant recipients compared to those remaining on dialysis, with a 5-year survival advantage of 10-20\% or more.

Functional outcomes are excellent, allowing patients to return to normal daily activities and experience a substantial enhancement in quality of life. Recurrence of original kidney disease in the transplanted graft is a concern for certain conditions, but this risk is managed with specific protocols. Prognostic factors influencing long-term outcomes include donor type, HLA matching, cold ischemia time, recipient age, and adherence to immunosuppressive therapy. Clinical guidelines consistently emphasize the superior long-term survival and quality of life benefits of kidney transplantation over dialysis for eligible patients.
