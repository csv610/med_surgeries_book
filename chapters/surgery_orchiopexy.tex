\chapter{Orchiopexy}

\section*{What Is This Surgery?}
Orchiopexy is a definitive surgical procedure primarily designed to correct cryptorchidism, a common congenital condition characterized by an undescended testicle. This involves the meticulous mobilization of the testicle from its abnormal location, which can range from the abdomen to the inguinal canal or ectopic sites, and its subsequent repositioning within the scrotum. The procedure culminates in securing the testicle within a surgically created scrotal pouch, typically a dartos pouch, to prevent re-ascent. This intervention is crucial not only for addressing cosmetic and psychological concerns associated with an empty scrotum but, more importantly, for optimizing testicular function, facilitating easier cancer surveillance, and mitigating the significant risks of testicular torsion and trauma inherent to maldescended testes.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Orchidopexy
  \item Testicular Fixation
  \item Undescended Testicle Repair
  \item Cryptorchidism Repair
  \item Scrotal Orchiopexy
  \item Inguinal Orchiopexy
  \item Laparoscopic Orchiopexy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Relevant Surgical Anatomy}
A thorough understanding of the relevant anatomy is paramount for successful orchiopexy. The testicles normally reside within the scrotum, a cooler environment essential for spermatogenesis. Each testicle is supplied by the testicular artery, a direct branch of the aorta, and drained by the pampiniform plexus of veins, which coalesce to form the testicular vein. The right testicular vein drains into the inferior vena cava, while the left drains into the left renal vein. The vas deferens, a muscular tube responsible for sperm transport, accompanies these vessels, along with lymphatic vessels and nerves (genitofemoral nerve branches), forming the spermatic cord.

The spermatic cord traverses the inguinal canal, an oblique passage through the anterior abdominal wall. Key anatomical landmarks include the deep inguinal ring (an opening in the transversalis fascia) and the superficial inguinal ring (an opening in the external oblique aponeurosis). The gubernaculum, a mesenchymal cord, plays a crucial role in guiding testicular descent. In cryptorchidism, the testicle may be arrested anywhere along this normal path of descent or in an ectopic location (e.g., superficial inguinal pouch, femoral triangle, perineum). The scrotum itself is a fibromuscular sac composed of several layers, including the dartos muscle, which is vital for creating the scrotal pouch for fixation. Meticulous dissection is required to preserve the delicate testicular blood supply and the vas deferens, which are often shortened or abnormally coursing in cases of cryptorchidism.

\section{Surgical Principle \& Rationale}
The fundamental principle of orchiopexy is to achieve a tension-free placement of the testicle into its anatomically correct and physiologically optimal position within the scrotum. Testicular descent is a complex process, typically completed by birth or within the first 6 months of life, influenced by hormonal, mechanical, and genetic factors. Failure of this descent, known as cryptorchidism, results in the testicle remaining in a warmer environment outside the scrotum, which is profoundly detrimental to germ cell development and increases the risk of malignant transformation.

The surgical rationale centers on several key objectives. First, by relocating the testicle to the cooler scrotal environment, the procedure aims to improve the potential for normal germ cell development and future fertility, particularly in unilateral cases. The optimal temperature for spermatogenesis is approximately 2-3 degrees Celsius below core body temperature. Second, a scrotal testicle is readily palpable, allowing for easier self-examination and clinical surveillance for testicular cancer, a risk that is elevated in undescended testes even after orchiopexy. Third, securing the testicle within the scrotum significantly reduces the risk of testicular torsion, a painful emergency caused by twisting of the spermatic cord, and protects the testicle from external trauma. Technical goals include achieving adequate spermatic cord length without tension, preserving the delicate testicular blood supply and the vas deferens, and creating a stable scrotal fixation to prevent re-ascent.

\section{History \& Surgical Evolution}
The concept of surgically correcting an undescended testicle dates back to the 19th century, following early anatomical observations by figures like John Hunter in the late 18th century. The first documented attempts at orchiopexy were performed in the mid-19th century, though often with limited success due to a nascent understanding of testicular vascularity and surgical technique. In 1877, Annandale performed one of the earliest successful orchiopexies, marking a significant step forward.

Significant advancements occurred in the early 20th century, shaping modern techniques. In 1899, Arthur Dean Bevan introduced the inguinal approach, emphasizing meticulous dissection of the spermatic cord to achieve sufficient length, a technique that laid the groundwork for contemporary orchiopexy. Torek, in 1909, described a method of temporarily suturing the testicle to the thigh, known as orchidopexy with external fixation, to maintain its scrotal position. This was later refined by Ombr\'edanne in the 1910s, who popularized the creation of a dartos pouch within the scrotum for internal fixation, a technique still widely used today. The understanding of the optimal timing for surgery has also evolved, shifting from later childhood to early infancy (typically 6-12 months of age) to maximize fertility potential and reduce long-term risks, reflecting a growing appreciation for the impact of supra-scrotal temperatures on germ cell development. The advent of laparoscopy in the late 20th century further revolutionized the management of non-palpable testes, allowing for diagnostic localization and, in many cases, definitive repair in a minimally invasive fashion.

\section{Clinical Indications}
Orchiopexy is indicated for a range of conditions primarily related to testicular maldescent or instability. The most common indications include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item To correct congenital cryptorchidism, where one or both testicles have not spontaneously descended into the scrotum by 6-12 months of age, following a period of watchful waiting.
  \item To address an ectopic testicle, which has descended outside the normal path of descent, often found in the superficial inguinal pouch, perineum, or femoral region.
  \item To prevent testicular torsion, especially in cases of previously diagnosed cryptorchidism or in patients with a "bell-clapper" deformity predisposing to torsion, where the tunica vaginalis completely surrounds the testicle and spermatic cord.
  \item To treat recurrent testicular torsion, where previous episodes indicate an anatomical predisposition requiring surgical fixation to prevent further occurrences.
  \item To manage testicular re-ascent, where a previously descended testicle retracts permanently out of the scrotum, often due to a short spermatic cord or hyperactive cremasteric reflex.
  \item To facilitate easier clinical examination and self-examination for early detection of testicular malignancy, as undescended testes have a higher lifetime risk of cancer.
  \item To improve the potential for normal spermatogenesis and fertility by placing the testicle in a cooler scrotal environment, thereby mitigating the damaging effects of elevated temperature.
  \item To alleviate psychological distress and cosmetic concerns associated with an empty scrotum, which can impact body image and self-esteem in children and adolescents.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Positioning}
Orchiopexy is overwhelmingly considered a primary surgical treatment for cryptorchidism. For infants diagnosed with an undescended testicle that has not spontaneously descended by 6-12 months of age, surgical correction is the recommended first-line intervention. This timing is chosen based on evidence suggesting that earlier intervention optimizes the chances of preserving testicular function and reducing the long-term risks associated with maldescent, particularly the detrimental effects of supra-scrotal temperatures on germ cell development.

In cases of acute testicular torsion, orchiopexy is performed as an emergency procedure, often concurrently with detorsion, to salvage the testicle and prevent recurrence. Here, it serves as a primary, urgent intervention. It can also serve as a secondary or prophylactic procedure in specific scenarios, such as managing testicular re-ascent after initial descent or as a prophylactic measure following a non-surgical detorsion of a torsed testicle where the underlying anatomical predisposition (e.g., bell-clapper deformity) needs correction. However, its most common and critical role remains as the initial definitive treatment for cryptorchidism, aiming to prevent future complications and optimize testicular health.

\section{Surgical Technique \& Procedure}
Orchiopexy is typically performed under general anesthesia. The patient is positioned supine, with the legs slightly abducted, and the surgical area, encompassing the groin and scrotum, is meticulously prepped with an antiseptic solution and draped in a sterile fashion.

The procedure generally involves the following key steps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia and Positioning:} General anesthesia is induced. The patient is placed in a supine position. The groin and scrotum are prepped with an antiseptic solution (e.g., povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine) and draped to expose the surgical field.
  \item \textbf{Incision:} For palpable undescended testes, a small, transverse incision (Lanz incision) is typically made in the inguinal crease, or sometimes a slightly oblique incision, on the affected side. For non-palpable testes, a diagnostic laparoscopy may precede or be combined with the open approach, often requiring a small umbilical incision for the camera port.
  \item \textbf{Inguinal Canal Exploration and Testicular Mobilization:} The external oblique aponeurosis is incised, and the inguinal canal is opened. The spermatic cord, containing the testicle, vas deferens, and testicular vessels, is identified. The testicle is carefully dissected free from surrounding tissues, including any adhesions or remnants of the processus vaginalis. The spermatic cord is meticulously freed from the posterior wall of the inguinal canal and surrounding structures to achieve maximum length without tension. A patent processus vaginalis, if present, is ligated and divided to prevent future hydrocele or indirect inguinal hernia formation.
  \item \textbf{Scrotal Pouch Creation:} A separate, small incision is made in the dependent part of the scrotum, usually transversely. A dartos pouch is created by dissecting a pocket between the dartos muscle and the scrotal skin, ensuring it is large enough to comfortably accommodate the testicle.
  \item \textbf{Testicular Transposition and Fixation:} A tunnel is created from the inguinal incision to the scrotal pouch, typically by blunt dissection. The mobilized testicle is gently guided through this tunnel into the scrotal pouch. It is then secured within the dartos pouch using absorbable sutures (e.g., 4-0 or 5-0 chromic gut or polyglactin) to the dartos muscle, preventing re-ascent. Care is taken to avoid tension on the spermatic cord.
  \item \textbf{Closure:} The inguinal incision is closed in layers. The external oblique aponeurosis is reapproximated, followed by closure of the subcutaneous tissues. Skin closure is typically performed with absorbable sutures (e.g., subcuticular polyglactin) or surgical glue. The scrotal incision is also closed in layers. No drains are typically required for routine orchiopexy.
\end{itemize}
For intra-abdominal or very high inguinal non-palpable testes, a laparoscopic approach may be used. This can be a single-stage procedure if sufficient cord length can be achieved after extensive mobilization, or a two-stage Fowler-Stephens orchiopexy for very high testes requiring division of the main testicular artery and reliance on collateral blood supply, with the second stage performed several months later.

\section*{Preoperative Workup \& Preparation}
Preoperative preparation for orchiopexy is generally straightforward, especially for otherwise healthy children, focusing on ensuring patient safety and optimizing surgical conditions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Medical Evaluation:} A thorough history and physical examination are performed by the surgeon and anesthesiologist to assess the child's overall health, identify any comorbidities (e.g., cardiac murmurs, asthma), and confirm the diagnosis of cryptorchidism. This includes careful palpation of the groin and scrotum to locate the testicle.
  \item \textbf{Laboratory Tests:} Routine blood tests, such as a complete blood count (CBC) and basic metabolic panel, are often performed, particularly for younger children or those with underlying conditions. Coagulation studies (PT/INR, PTT) may be ordered if there is a history of bleeding disorders or if the child is on anticoagulant medications.
  \item \textbf{Imaging Studies:} For palpable undescended testes, imaging is often not required. However, for non-palpable testes, an ultrasound may be performed to attempt to locate the testicle in the inguinal canal or abdomen. If the testicle remains non-palpable, diagnostic laparoscopy is often the most accurate method to locate an intra-abdominal testicle or confirm its absence (vanishing testis).
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Consultation:} An anesthesiologist evaluates the child to determine the safest anesthesia plan, considering age, weight, medical history, and any potential airway challenges.
  \item \textbf{NPO Status:} Patients are instructed to be NPO (nil per os - nothing by mouth) for a specified period before surgery, typically 6-8 hours for solids and 2-4 hours for clear liquids, to prevent aspiration during anesthesia.
  \item \textbf{Medication Adjustments:} Parents are advised on which regular medications, if any, should be continued or held before surgery. For instance, NSAIDs might be held to reduce bleeding risk, though this is less common in pediatric orchiopexy.
  \item \textbf{Informed Consent:} A detailed discussion with parents or legal guardians regarding the procedure, its benefits, risks, alternatives, and expected postoperative course is conducted, followed by obtaining written informed consent.
  \item \textbf{Prophylactic Antibiotics:} While not universally routine for simple orchiopexy, a single dose of prophylactic antibiotics (e.g., a first-generation cephalosporin like cefazolin) may be administered intravenously prior to incision, especially in cases involving prosthetic material (rare) or higher risk of infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contraindications \& Precautions}
\textbf{Absolute Contraindications:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Severe, uncontrolled systemic illness (e.g., acute respiratory distress syndrome, decompensated cardiac failure, severe sepsis) that poses an unacceptable anesthetic or surgical risk. Elective surgery should be deferred until the patient is medically optimized.
  \item Active infection in the inguinal or scrotal region, including cellulitis or abscess, which should be treated and resolved before elective surgery to minimize the risk of wound infection.
  \item Uncorrected coagulopathy or severe bleeding disorder that cannot be safely managed preoperatively, as this significantly increases the risk of hemorrhage.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Relative Contraindications \& Precautions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prematurity/Low Birth Weight:} Surgery is often deferred in very premature infants or those with extremely low birth weight until they are more physiologically stable and have reached a certain age or weight threshold, typically around 6 months corrected age.
  \item \textbf{Significant Cardiopulmonary Disease:} Requires thorough preoperative optimization, consultation with specialists (e.g., cardiology, pulmonology), and careful anesthetic management to minimize risks.
  \item \textbf{Testicular Agenesis:} If diagnostic laparoscopy or other investigations strongly suggest or confirm the absence of a testicle (vanishing testis), further surgical exploration may be deemed unnecessary, and the procedure may be aborted or converted to a diagnostic exploration only.
  \item \textbf{Precautions:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Vas Deferens and Testicular Vessel Preservation:} Meticulous, gentle dissection is paramount to avoid injury to the delicate vas deferens and the testicular artery and veins. Damage to these structures can lead to testicular atrophy or impaired fertility, especially if bilateral.
      \item \textbf{Tension-Free Placement:} Ensuring the testicle is placed in the scrotum without any tension on the spermatic cord is critical to prevent ischemia, which can lead to atrophy, and to prevent re-ascent. If adequate length cannot be achieved, a staged procedure (e.g., Fowler-Stephens) may be necessary.
      \item \textbf{Hemostasis:} Careful attention to hemostasis during dissection minimizes the risk of hematoma formation, which can compromise testicular viability and increase the risk of infection.
      \item \textbf{Associated Anomalies:} Surgeons must be aware of potential associated anomalies, such as a patent processus vaginalis (which should be ligated to prevent hydrocele or hernia) or a short gubernaculum, which may complicate mobilization.
      \item \textbf{Previous Inguinal Surgery:} Prior surgery in the inguinal region can lead to scar tissue, making dissection more challenging and increasing the risk of injury to the cord structures.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications \& Risks}
While orchiopexy is generally a safe procedure with a high success rate, like all surgeries, it carries potential risks. These can be broadly categorized into general surgical complications and procedure-specific risks.

\textbf{General Surgical Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anesthesia Complications:} These are rare but can include adverse reactions to medications, respiratory depression, cardiac arrhythmias, or aspiration pneumonia.
  \item \textbf{Bleeding:} Hematoma formation in the surgical site (groin or scrotum) can occur, potentially requiring drainage in rare cases. Significant intraoperative hemorrhage is uncommon.
  \item \textbf{Infection:} Wound infection, though uncommon (incidence <1\%), can occur and may manifest as redness, swelling, pain, or purulent drainage, potentially requiring antibiotics or drainage.
  \item \textbf{Pain:} Postoperative pain is expected but usually well-controlled with oral analgesics. Chronic pain is rare in pediatric patients.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Procedure-Specific Risks:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Testicular Atrophy or Ischemia:} This is the most significant and feared complication, occurring if the blood supply to the testicle is compromised during dissection or due to excessive tension on the spermatic cord after fixation. This can lead to shrinkage, hardening, and loss of function of the testicle. Incidence is typically 1-5\%.
  \item \textbf{Vas Deferens Injury:} Accidental damage to the vas deferens, the tube that carries sperm, can impair fertility, especially if bilateral. This risk is higher in re-operative cases or with very high undescended testes.
  \item \textbf{Epididymal Injury:} Damage to the epididymis, a structure adjacent to the testicle involved in sperm maturation and storage, can also impact fertility.
  \item \textbf{Testicular Re-ascent:} Despite fixation, the testicle may pull back out of the scrotum over time, requiring repeat surgery. This is often due to inadequate cord length or insufficient fixation.
  \item \textbf{Hydrocele Formation:} Accumulation of fluid around the testicle can occur if a patent processus vaginalis is not completely ligated or if there is lymphatic disruption. Usually benign and often resolving spontaneously, but sometimes requiring aspiration or further surgery.
  \item \textbf{Wound Complications:} These include hypertrophic scarring, keloid formation, or wound dehiscence (opening of the incision).
  \item \textbf{Nerve Injury:} Damage to sensory nerves in the inguinal region (e.g., ilioinguinal nerve) can lead to numbness or chronic neuropathic pain in the groin or inner thigh, though this is rare in pediatric cases.
  \item \textbf{Failure to Locate Testicle:} In cases of non-palpable testes, despite extensive exploration (open or laparoscopic), the testicle may not be found, confirming a vanishing testis (agenesis or atrophy in utero).
  \item \textbf{Persistent Malignancy Risk:} While orchiopexy facilitates surveillance, it does not completely eliminate the slightly increased lifetime risk of testicular cancer in previously undescended testes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Postoperative Recovery Timeline}
Immediately after orchiopexy, the child is transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for close monitoring as they recover from general anesthesia. Vital signs, pain levels, and the surgical site are continuously assessed. Pain management typically involves oral analgesics, often a combination of acetaminophen and ibuprofen, which are usually sufficient for pediatric patients. Most children are discharged home on the same day, as orchiopexy is commonly performed as an outpatient procedure.

During the first 24-48 hours, parents are advised to monitor for signs of complications such as excessive swelling, redness, fever, or drainage from the incision sites. Mild pain, bruising, and swelling in the groin and scrotum are normal. Activity restrictions are usually in place for the first 1-2 weeks; this includes avoiding strenuous activities, rough play, bicycle riding, or any activity that might put pressure on the surgical site or cause straddling injuries. A scrotal support or snug underwear may be recommended to provide comfort and help maintain the testicle's position. Diet can typically resume as tolerated. Wound care usually involves keeping the incision sites clean and dry, with absorbable sutures or surgical glue that do not require removal. Long-term, the goal is to ensure the testicle remains in its scrotal position and grows appropriately, with ongoing surveillance for any potential complications. Full return to normal activities, including sports, is usually permitted after 2-4 weeks, depending on the surgeon's assessment.

\section*{Surgical Findings \& Pathology}
During orchiopexy, the surgeon meticulously documents several key findings. These include the precise location of the undescended testicle (e.g., intra-abdominal, high inguinal, superficial inguinal pouch), its size and appearance, the length and integrity of the spermatic cord and vas deferens, and the presence of any associated anomalies such as a patent processus vaginalis or an epididymal anomaly. The mobility of the testicle and the tension on the cord after mobilization are critical observations.

If a biopsy of the testicle is performed (which is rare in routine orchiopexy, typically reserved for specific indications like suspected gonadal dysgenesis or very unusual morphology), the pathology report will confirm the presence of testicular tissue, assess germ cell density, and identify any signs of dysplasia or malignancy. For a patent processus vaginalis, the resected sac will be confirmed as such. The primary "pathology" in cryptorchidism is the malposition itself, leading to histological changes in the testicle over time, including a reduction in germ cell count and potential for Leydig cell hyperplasia, which are not typically assessed by routine biopsy during orchiopexy but are the underlying rationale for early intervention.

\section*{Expected Postoperative Course}
A normal, successful postoperative course following orchiopexy is characterized by a relatively swift recovery and the stable positioning of the testicle within the scrotum. Patients typically experience mild to moderate pain in the groin and scrotum for a few days, which is effectively managed with over-the-counter pain relievers. Swelling and bruising around the incision sites and in the scrotum are common and gradually subside over 1-2 weeks. The incision sites should heal cleanly, with absorbable sutures or surgical glue dissolving or flaking off within 2-3 weeks, leaving minimal scarring. The testicle should remain palpable and securely fixed within the scrotal dartos pouch, without signs of re-ascent. Children are usually able to resume light activities within a few days and return to school within a week. Full physical activity, including sports, is typically permitted after 2-4 weeks, once the surgical sites are well-healed and the risk of injury is minimized. The ultimate goal is for the testicle to grow normally and maintain its scrotal position long-term, optimizing its potential for function and facilitating future surveillance.

\section{Postoperative Care \& Follow-up}
Postoperative care for orchiopexy focuses on wound management, pain control, and monitoring for complications, followed by long-term surveillance.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wound Care:} Keep incision sites clean and dry. Sponge baths are often recommended for the first few days, with showering permitted after 48-72 hours. Avoid soaking in baths or swimming pools until incisions are fully healed.
  \item \textbf{Pain Management:} Administer prescribed or over-the-counter pain medications (e.g., acetaminophen, ibuprofen) as needed.
  \item \textbf{Activity Restrictions:} Avoid strenuous activities, rough play, bicycle riding, and contact sports for 2-4 weeks as advised by the surgeon.
  \item \textbf{Initial Postoperative Visit:} Typically scheduled 1-2 weeks after surgery to check wound healing, assess the testicle's position, and address any immediate concerns. No sutures or staples typically need removal as absorbable materials are used.
  \item \textbf{Annual Physical Examinations:} Regular check-ups with the pediatrician or surgeon are recommended to monitor testicular growth, consistency, and position within the scrotum. This ensures the testicle remains descended and is developing normally.
  \item \textbf{Testicular Self-Examination Education:} As the child approaches adolescence, education on testicular self-examination is vital due to the persistent, albeit reduced, risk of testicular cancer in previously undescended testes.
  \item \textbf{Fertility Assessment:} For individuals with a history of bilateral cryptorchidism, or in cases of unilateral cryptorchidism with concerns, fertility assessment (e.g., semen analysis) may be considered in adulthood.
\end{itemize}
Parents and patients should be educated on warning signs such as sudden scrotal pain (suggesting torsion), increasing swelling, redness, fever, or the testicle disappearing from the scrotum, which warrant immediate medical attention.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cryptorchidism, the primary indication for orchiopexy, is one of the most common congenital anomalies in male infants. It affects approximately 3-5\% of full-term male neonates, with a significantly higher incidence of up to 30\% in premature infants. The condition often resolves spontaneously within the first 6 months of life, with the prevalence dropping to about 1\% by one year of age. Unilateral cryptorchidism is far more common than bilateral, with the right testicle being affected slightly more often than the left. Approximately 80\% of undescended testes are palpable in the inguinal canal, while 20\% are non-palpable (either intra-abdominal or absent). Orchiopexy is consequently one of the most frequently performed pediatric surgical procedures worldwide. The mortality rate associated with orchiopexy is exceedingly low, primarily related to general anesthesia risks in otherwise healthy children, estimated to be less than 0.01\%. Morbidity rates are also low, with testicular atrophy occurring in a small percentage of cases (typically 1-5\%) and re-ascent being similarly uncommon (2-5\%).

\section*{Outcomes \& Prognosis}
The outcomes of orchiopexy are generally excellent, with high success rates in achieving and maintaining scrotal testicular position. Over 90-95\% of orchiopexies successfully place the testicle in the scrotum, where it remains long-term. The prognosis for fertility is significantly improved, especially in cases of unilateral cryptorchidism, where fertility rates often approach those of the general population. For bilateral cryptorchidism, fertility may still be reduced compared to controls, but it is substantially better than if the condition remains untreated. Early intervention (6-12 months of age) is a key prognostic factor for optimizing fertility potential.

Orchiopexy also effectively reduces the risk of testicular torsion and protects the testicle from trauma. While the procedure facilitates early detection through self-examination, it does not completely eliminate the slightly increased lifetime risk of testicular malignancy associated with cryptorchidism (estimated 2-8 times higher than the general population), emphasizing the importance of ongoing self-examination and clinical follow-up. Prognostic factors influencing outcomes include the initial position of the testicle (lower success rates and higher atrophy rates for intra-abdominal testes), the age at which surgery is performed (earlier intervention generally leads to better fertility potential), and the presence of associated anomalies or a very short spermatic cord. Landmark studies and guidelines from organizations like the American Urological Association consistently support early orchiopexy as the standard of care for cryptorchidism.
